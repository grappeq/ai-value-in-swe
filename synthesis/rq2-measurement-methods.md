# RQ2 — Measurement methods: how was AI's effect on software engineering actually measured, and how much should we trust each method?

## 1. Headline answer

This literature splits into a small core of methods that can support a causal claim and a much larger periphery that cannot — and the periphery is where almost all the famous numbers live. True randomised/quasi-experimental designs number in the low single digits (`metr-2025-rct`, `peng-2023-copilot-rct`, `google-rct-2024`, plus DiD/RDD telemetry from one CMU lab and one TSE paper); everything else is survey, vendor telemetry, or narrative. The single most consequential finding is that **self-report magnitude is uninformative about measured change** — confirmed by four independent, methodologically distinct paired designs (belief-vs-measured within the same RCT for `metr-2025-rct` and `peng-2023-copilot-rct`; self-rank-vs-measured-rank cross-sectionally for `stanford-denisov-blanch`; within-person change-score correlation for `stray-2026-nav`) converging on the same conclusion despite disagreeing on *direction* (tension-1: high confidence on the operative claim, low confidence on any general direction rule). Method hierarchy: RCT > quasi-experimental telemetry with a counterfactual > vendor telemetry (direction only) > survey (sentiment/adoption only) > practitioner narrative (mechanism only) — self-report sits at the bottom for magnitude claims regardless of instrumentation quality.

## 3. The perceived-vs-measured deltas, quantified

Four independent paired designs (the `measurement_basis: both` rows in `evidence/evidence-table.tsv`) — strictly three AI-specific pairings plus one general-calibration corroborant (the Stanford 43-developer exercise measures self-assessment accuracy generally, not AI benefit specifically; tension-1 carries this caveat and it travels here too):

- **`metr-2025-rct`** — experienced OSS devs believed ~20–24% faster; measured 19% *slower* (S=0.188). **~39–43pp over-estimate.** Expert forecasters (34 economists, 54 ML researchers) also predicted +38–39% speedup — directionally wrong, not just off on magnitude.
- **`peng-2023-copilot-rct`** — Upwork freelancers self-estimated ~35% speedup; measured effect was 55.8% faster. **~21pp under-estimate — opposite sign to METR.**
- **`stanford-denisov-blanch`** — 43 developers' self-rated productivity percentile vs measured score: off by ~30 percentile points on average, "almost as good as flipping a coin," 1-in-3 within own quartile.
- **`stray-2026-nav`** — same 25 developers, individual-level change-score pairing: self-reported vs measured commit-activity change correlates at **ρ≈0.17 (p=0.406, ns)** across six metrics; 0/25 adopters reported a decrease despite several showing measured decline.

The 2026 inflation: **`metr-2026-survey`** reports self-reported multipliers growing more extreme (median 3x speed, 1.4–2x value, 2.5x forecast for 2027) with *no measured comparator* — METR's own post cites its 2025 RCT's ~40pp overestimate as a reason to distrust these new numbers, and notes its own staff report lower gains than external respondents. Hype-baseline evidence, not a fifth paired datapoint.

**`vella-blincoe-2026`**'s internal decoupling: with no measured comparator at all, self-reported productivity (84% improved, stable across two waves) and self-reported experience (cognitive load/flow — the "any worsening" composite grew 14%→27%) are statistically independent within the same people at the same time (p=0.313). Self-report is internally incoherent even before being checked against reality.

## 4. The citation-corruption meta-finding

This project caught roughly ten instances of secondary/vendor coverage corrupting primary numbers: METR's CI misquoted; Stanford's "7–9% Copilot-class" figure, absent from both talk transcripts, likely fabricated downstream; InfoQ attributing Faros's throughput numbers to Agoda; Noda's "coding = 16% of dev time" not actually in the Microsoft paper cited (real: ~11% actual/~20% ideal); Devin/Codex merge rates misquoted as 42.9%/81.6% (true: 61.6%/77.9%, with 81.6% belonging to an unrelated paper); Böckeler's "$91k/year" cost figure, an anecdote-of-anecdote extrapolation; Shen & Tamkin's comprehension figure quoted as absolutes ("50% vs 67%") unreconcilable against the primary paper (defensible figure: d=0.738); the "84% of AI PRs unreviewed" headline, dissolved against a matched human-PR baseline (30.1% vs 30.8%); GitClear's 2026 churn numbers riding an undisclosed ~3.3–3.8x dataset-composition jump; and DORA 2025 inverting its own 2024 construct's name (stability *reduction* became instability *increase*). Rule adopted: **never cite this literature from summaries.** Every table number traces to a primary text, and even primaries needed cross-checking against each other (two AIDev vintages give Copilot acceptance rates of 35% and 68% for nominally the same statistic).

## 5. Claims list with confidence levels

1. **Self-report magnitude/direction is uninformative about measured productivity change.** Confidence: **high** — four independent designs, different populations, same conclusion despite disagreeing on sign.
2. **No single popular metric (LOC, PR/commit counts, acceptance rate, "AI-generated %", token volume, self-report multipliers) should be used alone to judge AI impact.** Confidence: **high** — tension-7 resolved, sourced failure mode against every named metric.
3. **Rigorous causal evidence on mature agentic tools remains extremely thin — three RCTs on productivity outcomes (plus one on comprehension), all caveated, none on an experienced-developer brownfield population at agentic scale.** Confidence: **high**, per the pre-registered scarcity clause (PROTOCOL §8) — scarcity is the finding.
4. **Vendor telemetry can indicate direction but not trustworthy magnitude; vendor-vs-vendor comparisons should be assumed to measure different things until reconciled.** Confidence: **medium** — demonstrated on the one pair (Faros/DX) checked thoroughly.
5. **Adoption/sentiment surveys are legitimate for sentiment/adoption trend, illegitimate as productivity evidence; adoption figures are highly population-sensitive.** Confidence: **high** — three surveys diverge 31–55% on adoption in the same window.
6. **Shared unvalidated instruments (the AIDev heuristic) silently cap triangulation — authorial independence does not imply measurement independence.** Confidence: **medium-high** — demonstrated on this instrument; the general rule is untested elsewhere.

## What this means for us

The first question when reading a vendor or industry AI-productivity claim is not "is the number big" but "what produced it" — disproportionately "a self-report survey" or "an undisclosed vendor telemetry pipeline," both with documented failure modes. Treat any bare "developers report X% improvement" claim as sentiment, not evidence: the METR/Peng/Stanford/Stray quadrant shows self-report can be wrong in either direction. Demand a stated comparison group and a disclosed measurement definition before treating any internal or vendor number as more than directional colour — Faros-vs-DX shows even that bar catches real errors cheaply. For our own internal measurement (decision 2), pair any self-reported instrument with an independent telemetry-based outcome measure from day one — not because self-report is worthless, but because it cannot be validated after the fact, and this literature's clearest lesson is that self-report and measured outcome diverge without warning, in either direction, even among sophisticated respondents.
