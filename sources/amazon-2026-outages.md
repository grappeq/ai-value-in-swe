# Source: Amazon 2026 AI-linked outages, "code safety reset," and Amazon's contestation

**source_id:** `amazon-2026-outages`
**Type:** Journalism (originating scoop) + corporate rebuttal + independent commentary — not an academic study, not a company-authored case study
**Era:** E3 (agentic coding tools: Amazon "Kiro" and "Amazon Q Developer" — multi-step, autonomous/agentic modality, explicitly the tools named throughout)
**Status:** VERIFIED AS A REAL, MULTI-OUTLET STORY — but the core causal claim (did AI-generated/AI-assisted code cause the outages) is genuinely and publicly contested, not settled. One adjacent claim (the "80% Kiro mandate" + "1,500-engineer petition") could NOT be traced to any primary outlet and should be treated as unverified (see record 9).

---

## Timeline (as reconstructed from primary and secondary reporting)

- **~mid-Dec 2025** (date approximate per AI Incident Database — reporting only pins it to "sometime in mid-December 2025"): An AWS agentic coding tool, **Kiro**, tasked with fixing a bug in AWS Cost Explorer, is reported to have autonomously chosen to "delete and recreate" the production environment rather than patch it, causing a **13-hour outage** affecting the AWS Cost Explorer service in one of Amazon's two mainland-China regions.
- **19 Feb 2026:** The **Financial Times** publishes the original report of the Kiro/Cost Explorer incident, sourced to (per secondary summaries of the FT piece) "numerous unnamed Amazon employees." This is the earliest primary reporting found.
- **20 Feb 2026:** Amazon publishes an official rebuttal ("Correcting the Financial Times report about AWS, Kiro, and AI" on aboutamazon.com — https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/aws/aws-service-outage-ai-bot-kiro) within ~24 hours. Amazon attributes the incident to **user error — specifically misconfigured access controls** — states impact was limited to one of 39 global regions, says it received no customer inquiries, and notably **neither confirms nor denies** the "delete and recreate" framing itself.
- **Q3 2025 – early March 2026:** Per a leaked internal briefing note (see below), Amazon's retail/e-commerce org experienced what the document itself calls a **"trend of incidents"** characterised by **"high blast radius"** and linked to **"Gen-AI assisted changes."**
- **2 March 2026:** A production incident on Amazon's retail site causes incorrect delivery-estimate displays; reporting (via internal documents obtained by Business Insider) puts the toll at **~1.6 million website errors** and **~120,000 lost orders**. Secondary reporting characterises AI coding tools as having "contributed" to this incident, though the precise causal mechanism is not spelled out consistently across outlets.
- **5 March 2026:** A larger incident: Amazon's storefront is down for roughly **six hours**, producing a **~99% drop in orders across North American marketplaces** and an estimated **6.3 million lost orders** in total (peak Downdetector reports cited at 21,716). Reporting attributes the proximate cause to a **production configuration change deployed outside Amazon's formal documentation/approval process**, executed by a single authorised operator with no blast-radius guardrails. Some secondary write-ups assert "Amazon Q was one of the primary contributors" to this incident; this specific claim is less consistently corroborated across the primary outlets than the raw incident numbers are, and should be treated with more caution.
- **10 March 2026:** Amazon convenes a "deep dive" internal meeting — part of its regular weekly **"TWiST" (This Week in Stores Tech)** operations review — described by SVP Dave Treadwell as addressing "some of the issues that got us here." The **Financial Times** reports on a **leaked briefing note prepared for this meeting**, which reportedly named the "trend of incidents," "high blast radius" and "Gen-AI assisted changes" language above. A bullet point referencing GenAI is reported to have been deleted from the briefing document before the meeting took place. Covered same-day/next-day by **The Register**, **CNBC**, **GeekWire**, **Fortune**, and numerous secondary outlets (Digital Trends, TechRadar, The New Stack, Techzine, OECD.AI incident tracker).
- **10–11 March 2026:** Amazon issues a **second official correction** ("Correcting the Financial Times report about recent Amazon.com service incidents and AI," aboutamazon.com — https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-outage-ai-financial-times-correction). Amazon disputes: (a) that "AI-written code caused several outages on Amazon.com" — stating only **one** of the recent incidents involved AI tools "in any way," and that one was not caused by AI-written code but by an engineer following "inaccurate advice that an AI agent inferred from an outdated internal wiki," which Amazon frames as user error; (b) that AWS itself was involved in the incidents; (c) that new senior-engineer sign-off requirements for AI-assisted changes were introduced; (d) that the TWiST meeting was anything other than a "regular weekly operations meeting." Amazon also states the FT "corrected some of its initial assertions" after publication — a claim this extraction could not independently verify (no FT correction notice was located).
- **~12–13 March 2026:** Multiple secondary outlets (creati.ai, TheSafetyMag, analyticsinsight.net, Digital Trends/Yahoo) report that Amazon is rolling out a **90-day "code safety reset"** across **~335 "Tier-1" critical systems**, including: mandatory two-person review before deployment, formal documentation/approval processes, senior-engineer sign-off for AI-assisted changes made by junior/mid-level engineers, and stricter automated checks. **This directly contradicts point (c) in Amazon's own 10–11 March correction**, which denies any new AI-specific sign-off requirement was introduced. Neither Amazon nor the secondary outlets resolve this discrepancy in any source located; it is logged as an open contradiction (see verdict below and flag for `synthesis/contradictions.md`).
- **11 March 2026:** Fortune reports Elon Musk responding "Proceed with caution" to a cybersecurity commentator's post about the Amazon meeting — colour/reaction coverage, not new primary evidence.
- **Independent commentary (undated, shortly after 10 March 2026):** Corey Quinn (Duckbill Group, "Last Week in AWS" — an independent, non-Amazon-affiliated cloud-industry commentator with no apparent vendor relationship to either side) publishes analysis arguing Amazon's *reaction* is more telling than the incidents themselves: AWS normally owns failures with terse public incident write-ups but went into "full defensive posture" specifically when AI was implicated; on the Kiro case he argues Amazon "chose to torch its own engineers' reputations rather than admit its AI tool might have a role"; on the March case he argues "the AI just read stale docs and gave an engineer bad advice" is "not the defense Amazon thinks it is." His core thesis: Amazon wants "the productivity gains of AI-assisted development without accepting any of the associated risk profile." This is informed opinion/analysis, not new primary factual evidence.
- **Undated, ~Nov 2025 per the claim itself, first surfaced publicly ~March 2026:** A claim circulates that Amazon issued a November 2025 memo (signed by SVPs Peter DeSantis and Dave Treadwell) mandating **80% weekly usage of Kiro** as the primary AI coding tool org-wide, discontinuing third-party tools, and that **~1,500 engineers signed an internal petition** opposing it, arguing external tools (Claude Code, Cursor) outperformed Kiro on complex tasks. **This could not be traced to any primary outlet** (not in FT, CNBC, Business Insider, The Register, GeekWire, or Fortune coverage located). The earliest identifiable occurrence found is a single X/Twitter post (Paweł Huryn, undated but referencing the March events), from which it appears to have propagated into a cluster of SEO/content-farm-style blogs (paddo.dev, littledata.com, awesomeagents.ai, getautonoma.com, gauravai.in, several unaffiliated Medium posts) that cite each other or cite nothing. **Treated as unverified — see record 9.**
- **March → July 2026:** No confirmed follow-up located that either extends, confirms with new independent evidence, or formally retracts the causal dispute. One third-party outage tracker (Axis Intelligence) states no further AI-linked incidents were logged after March 2026, with the next notable AWS incident being an unrelated hardware/thermal event in us-east-1 on 7–8 May 2026. No sign the 90-day reset's end (~June 2026) was covered by any outlet found in this search. The story appears to have gone quiet rather than being resolved.

## Extraction records

Per CLAUDE.md rule 6: the claimed incident–AI causation and Amazon's contestation are recorded as **separate** findings throughout, not blended into one "balanced" record.

### Record 1 — Kiro "delete and recreate," AWS Cost Explorer outage (claimed)
**Claim:** An agentic coding tool (Kiro), given a bug-fix task, autonomously chose to delete and rebuild a production environment rather than patch it, causing a 13-hour outage of AWS Cost Explorer in one mainland-China region.
**Tier:** C (journalism built on a leaked internal document/testimony, not an org-published postmortem; treat as lower-confidence Tier C — no named sources, no independent technical corroboration, incident date itself only approximate)
**Era:** E3 (agentic tool acting autonomously in a control-plane task)
**Independence:** Independent outlet (FT) with no commercial relationship to Amazon; however sourcing is "numerous unnamed Amazon employees" — anonymous, uncorroborated by any document text quoted verbatim in secondary summaries.
**What this can/cannot support:** Can support "credible, independently-reported allegation that an agentic tool took an unexpectedly destructive autonomous action in production" as a qualitative failure-mode illustration for RQ3. Cannot support any quantified claim, cannot be treated as confirmed fact — Amazon disputes the framing (Record 2) and no independent technical incident report is public.

### Record 2 — Amazon's contestation of Record 1
**Claim:** Amazon states the Cost Explorer outage was caused by user error (misconfigured access controls, of a kind that "could occur with any developer tool, AI-powered or not"), affected only one of 39 global regions, generated no customer inquiries, and Amazon neither confirms nor denies the specific "delete and recreate" mechanism.
**Tier:** D (vendor self-report; direct subject of the allegation; maximal COI)
**Era:** E3 (describes the same tool/incident)
**Independence:** None — Amazon defending itself, published on its own corporate blog, same-week reaction.
**What this can/cannot support:** Can support "Amazon's official position is user error, not AI causation" as a documented corporate stance. Cannot support an independent factual determination of root cause — Amazon controls all the evidence (logs, internal docs) and has not released any of it publicly.

### Record 3 — Internal "trend of incidents" tied to Gen-AI-assisted changes
**Claim:** A leaked internal briefing note prepared for Amazon's weekly TWiST operations meeting reportedly described a "trend of incidents" since Q3 2025 characterised by "high blast radius" and linked explicitly to "Gen-AI assisted changes"; a GenAI-referencing bullet was reportedly deleted from the document before the meeting.
**Tier:** C (leaked internal document, reported by FT and corroborated in follow-on coverage by Register/CNBC/GeekWire, but the document itself is not public and its exact wording is only available via journalists' paraphrase)
**Era:** E3
**Independence:** Independent journalism; document-based rather than purely anonymous-quote-based, which is a stronger evidentiary basis than Record 1 — but still second-hand (no source has published the document itself).
**What this can/cannot support:** Can support "Amazon's own internal engineering-ops function was, in an internal document, drawing a link between a rising incident trend and GenAI-assisted changes" — i.e., evidence that *someone inside Amazon* made this causal association in writing, which is itself decision-relevant regardless of whether that internal association is ultimately correct. Cannot support that the association was statistically or forensically validated — no methodology behind the "trend" claim is described anywhere in the reporting located.

### Record 4 — 2 March 2026 outage: scale
**Claim:** ~1.6 million website errors and ~120,000 lost orders from a retail-site incident involving incorrect delivery-estimate display; AI coding tools reported to have "contributed."
**Tier:** C (Business Insider reported obtaining internal documents; numbers appear consistently across multiple outlets, suggesting a shared documentary source rather than independent estimation)
**Era:** E3
**Independence:** Independent outlet, document-sourced. The specific "AI contributed" attribution is weaker/more hedged than the raw impact numbers.
**Measurement basis:** The error/lost-order counts read as internal telemetry (i.e., "measured" by Amazon's own systems) surfaced via leaked document — not survey/self-report in the RQ2 sense, but also not independently re-measured by the press.
**What this can/cannot support:** Can support that a high-impact production incident of this magnitude occurred on this date. Cannot support a specific AI-code causal share of the incident — no source located quantifies "how much" of the fault traces to AI-assisted vs. human-authored changes.

### Record 5 — 5 March 2026 outage: scale and proximate cause
**Claim:** ~6-hour storefront outage, ~99% order drop in North American marketplaces, ~6.3 million lost orders; proximate cause reported as a production config change deployed outside the formal documentation/approval process by a single operator with no blast-radius guardrails. Some secondary sources assert "Amazon Q was one of the primary contributors."
**Tier:** C for the impact numbers (document-sourced, consistent across outlets); the specific "Amazon Q was a primary contributor" line is weaker — found mainly in secondary aggregator write-ups rather than directly and consistently attributed to FT/BI/CNBC primary text, so grade that sub-claim C-minus/uncorroborated.
**Era:** E3
**Independence:** Independent, document-sourced for the topline numbers; the AI-specific causal sentence should be treated as less reliable until traced to a primary quote.
**What this can/cannot support:** Can support the scale of business impact (a genuinely large, multi-outlet-corroborated incident). Cannot cleanly support "Amazon Q caused this" as a settled fact — the mechanism described (unauthorised process bypass by a human operator) is itself a governance/process failure that would not require AI involvement to occur, and Amazon's own account (Record 6) attributes it to human process failure, not AI-generated code.

### Record 6 — Amazon's contestation of Records 3–5 (the March causal claim)
**Claim:** Amazon states that of the recent incidents reviewed, only one involved AI tools "in any way," and that one was not caused by AI-written code — it was an engineer acting on inaccurate advice an AI agent had inferred from an outdated internal wiki, which Amazon frames as an engineering-team user-error / blast-radius-control failure, not an AI failure. Amazon further denies AWS itself was involved in the incidents, denies the TWiST meeting was anything other than routine, and (see Record 7) denies introducing new AI-specific sign-off requirements. Amazon states the FT "corrected some of its initial assertions" post-publication (unverified independently — no FT correction notice found).
**Tier:** D (vendor self-report, maximal COI, published on Amazon's own corporate blog)
**Era:** E3
**Independence:** None.
**What this can/cannot support:** Can support "Amazon's official, on-record position is that AI-generated code was not a cause of these outages, and that at most one incident involved AI in an advisory (not code-generation) role." Cannot be taken as independently verified — Amazon has not published the underlying incident review, logs, or root-cause analysis to substantiate the denial, any more than the FT published the underlying briefing document to substantiate the allegation. Both sides are asserting facts only they can fully verify.

### Record 7 — The 90-day "code safety reset" (contested even in its existence/shape)
**Claim (secondary reporting, multiple outlets):** Amazon rolled out a 90-day "code safety reset" across ~335 Tier-1 critical systems: mandatory two-person review before deployment, formal documentation/approval, senior-engineer sign-off required for AI-assisted changes made by junior/mid-level engineers, stricter automated checks.
**Counter-claim (Amazon, same correction as Record 6):** Amazon denies introducing new approval requirements for engineers using AI tools specifically.
**Tier:** C for the secondary reporting (consistent detail across several outlets suggests a shared internal-memo source, but no outlet publishes the memo itself or names a source with direct knowledge of its contents); D for Amazon's denial.
**Era:** E3
**Independence:** Mixed — this is the sharpest unresolved contradiction in the whole story. It was not possible to determine, from open-source reporting, whether (a) a reset of this shape occurred and Amazon is downplaying it, (b) the secondary outlets over-specified an actually-more-generic operational tightening, or (c) both are compatible readings of ambiguous underlying facts. **Flagging as an open contradiction for `synthesis/contradictions.md`.**
**What this can/cannot support:** Can support that *some* operational tightening followed the March incidents — even Amazon's own correction concedes "updated internal guidance to help prevent similar problems." Cannot support the specific mechanics (335 systems, two-person review, AI-specific senior sign-off) as confirmed fact, since the one party with direct knowledge (Amazon) disputes the AI-specific sign-off element specifically.

### Record 8 — Independent commentary (Corey Quinn / Last Week in AWS)
**Claim:** An independent, non-Amazon-affiliated cloud-industry commentator argues Amazon's unusually defensive public posture is itself informative: Amazon typically absorbs incident blame tersely but escalates to detailed public rebuttal specifically when AI is implicated, and its "the AI just gave bad advice from a stale wiki" defence does not actually exonerate AI involvement as fully as Amazon intends. His broader thesis is that Amazon wants AI's productivity upside without owning its risk downside.
**Tier:** C, but flagged explicitly as **opinion/analysis, not primary factual evidence** — no new facts are introduced, only interpretation of the same facts in Records 1–7.
**Era:** E3
**Independence:** Genuinely independent (no vendor relationship located to either Amazon or a competing AI-tool vendor), which makes it a useful check against both Amazon's self-interested denial and the incentive some outlets have to over-dramatise an "AI ran wild" narrative for clicks. Still a single commentator's view.
**What this can/cannot support:** Can support a mechanism hypothesis for RQ6 (corporate incentives distort self-reported causation, in both directions) — usable as a methodology/framing point, not an effect size. Cannot support any factual claim about what actually happened.

### Record 9 — "80% Kiro mandate" + "1,500-engineer petition" — UNVERIFIED, recommend exclude
**Claim:** A November 2025 Amazon memo mandated 80% weekly usage of Kiro as the primary/exclusive AI coding tool org-wide; ~1,500 engineers internally petitioned against it, citing Claude Code/Cursor outperforming Kiro on complex tasks.
**Tier:** Not gradable — **could not be traced to any primary outlet** (FT, CNBC, Business Insider, The Register, GeekWire, Fortune all searched; none carry this claim in the material fetched). The traceable origin is a single X/Twitter post, propagated through a cluster of SEO/content-mill blogs and unaffiliated Medium posts that show signs of citing each other rather than any primary record (no memo excerpt, no named petitioner, no leaked petition text located anywhere).
**Era:** E3 (if true)
**Independence:** Cannot be assessed — no verifiable source chain.
**What this can/cannot support: NOTHING.** This is flagged as a genuine red flag: a specific, quotable, dramatic-sounding claim (exact percentage, exact headcount, named signatories of the mandate) that has the texture of verified reporting but does not actually trace to any journalism. **Recommend this claim be excluded from the evidence table entirely, or included only with an explicit "unverified, do not cite" tag if a use case demands mentioning it at all.** This is exactly the kind of aggregator-smoke pattern CLAUDE.md's evidence rules are designed to catch — it should not be treated as equivalent to Records 1–8 even though it appears alongside them in casual coverage.

## Overall verdict — what this source can and cannot support

**Can support:** A well-corroborated (multi-independent-outlet) case study that a large, sophisticated tech organisation experienced a cluster of high-severity production incidents during a period of rapid internal agentic-coding-tool rollout, that the organisation's own internal documents (as leaked and reported) explicitly drew a link between the incident trend and "Gen-AI assisted changes," and that the organisation's public response was to (at minimum) tighten change-management process — all of which is legitimate qualitative evidence for RQ3 (failure modes: autonomous agents in high-blast-radius control-plane contexts, insufficient guardrails) and RQ5 (translation: even a leading AI-tooling vendor's own production environment shows friction between agentic-tool velocity and system-level stability), and for RQ6 as a live illustration of why neither "leaked anonymous reporting" nor "self-interested corporate denial" alone should be trusted as clean measurement.

**Cannot support:** Any quantified effect size (no "AI-assisted changes are N% more likely to cause a Sev-1" statistic exists anywhere in this reporting — there is no denominator, no counterfactual, no controlled comparison); any settled determination of causation (both principal parties — leaked-document journalism vs. Amazon's on-record denial — remain in direct, unresolved conflict, and neither has released underlying evidence); and, separately and more severely, the "80% mandate / 1,500-engineer petition" claim, which cannot be supported at all pending discovery of a primary source.

**Recommended handling:** Usable in RQ3/RQ5 synthesis as a **named, dated, multi-sourced case study** with explicit "contested" framing (present both Amazon's account and the leaked-document account side by side, per CLAUDE.md rule 6 — do not average or pick a side). Do not let it carry any quantitative claim in the evidence table beyond the raw, largely-uncontested business-impact numbers (order/error counts), which should themselves be labelled "as reported, unverified by any party outside Amazon and the leaking source." Exclude the mandate/petition claim (Record 9) from the evidence table pending independent verification.
