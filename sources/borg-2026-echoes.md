## Tier, era, independence

- **Tier: A.** Genuine two-phase design: Phase 1 is an observational comparison (self-selected/stratified AI-use vs. not) on a feature-addition task; Phase 2 is a **true randomized controlled trial** — new, independent participants randomly assigned to evolve either the AI-derived or the non-AI-derived Phase 1 solutions, with no knowledge of which they'd received. Pre-registered (Registered Report, in-principle-accepted before data collection — the strongest bias-control device in this entire evidence base), with both frequentist and Bayesian analysis (including sensitivity analysis across optimistic/pessimistic/neutral priors) reported alongside each other. This clears the PROTOCOL §3 Tier A bar cleanly — arguably a cleaner causal design for the *downstream-maintenance* question specifically than most Tier A sources in this project, because Phase 2 randomises the *maintainer's* exposure to AI-derived code directly, rather than inferring maintenance cost from proxies.
- **Era: E1/E2 — NOT E3. This is the load-bearing fact for the cross-ref adjudication below.** Data collection ran **October 2024 (pilot, n=31) through 18 January 2025.** Tool mix, per the paper's own framing: GitHub Copilot was the most-used tool (autocomplete-era, E1), with ChatGPT, Amazon CodeWhisperer, JetBrains AI Assistant, Visual Studio IntelliCode, and TabNine also reported (all E1-style completion/chat tools), and some use of Cursor, which the paper itself labels "a second-generation chat-based tool" (E2 framing — this is Cursor used as a chat/completion aid in late 2024, well before its agent/multi-file-edit mode was the dominant workflow). The paper's own vocabulary — "first generation of AI assistants refers to LLM-driven code completion tools" — is explicitly an E1/E2 framing, not an agentic one. No participant is reported using an agentic, plan-execute-verify tool (Claude Code, Cursor-agent-mode, Devin-class) to author the Phase 1 code. **Tag: E1, with a minority E2 admixture; zero E3 content.**
- **Independence — significant vendor/consultancy COI, disclosed by the authors' own affiliations rather than hidden.**
  1. **Markus Borg is affiliated with CodeScene** (Malmö, Sweden; also Lund University) — CodeScene is a commercial code-health/tech-debt analytics vendor, and **CodeHealth, the study's primary quality metric, is CodeScene's own proprietary commercial product.** This is a direct conflict: the study's central "no maintainability penalty" finding is measured using the metric one author's employer sells. The paper discloses a third party (Codility) assisted with customising CodeHealth's thresholds for this study, which is a mitigating step but does not remove the conflict.
  2. **Dave Hewett, Noric Couderc, and Emma Söderberg are affiliated with Equal Experts** (a software consultancy, offices in London/Cape Town/Bengaluru); **Donald Graham, Uttam Kini, and Dave Farley are affiliated with Equal Experts and/or Continuous Delivery** (Dave Farley's own consultancy/training brand). Equal Experts' own recruitment/assessment platform, `snapcode.review`, was used to administer the experimental tasks — a second vendor-adjacent dependency (the platform provider is also a study co-author group).

## Method & population

- **Design:** two-phase controlled experiment, pre-registered as a Registered Report (design peer-reviewed and in-principle-accepted at ICSME 2024 before data collection began).
- **Codebase/task:** a Java web application ("RecipeFinder"); Phase 1 participants extended search functionality (added a new feature); Phase 2 participants evolved the Phase 1 solutions further (a second, related feature/change), without AI assistance in Phase 2 regardless of arm.
- **Recruitment:** 449 people prescreened; recruited via social media (YouTube, LinkedIn, X) and personal networks — a volunteer/convenience sample, not an enterprise-internal population. **92.2%–95% reported as professional developers** (two slightly different figures appeared across fetch passes — recorded as reported, not reconciled); median age band 40–49.
- **Phase 1 (n=76 completed Task 1):** participants stratified by self-reported AI experience/preference into an AI-permitted arm and an AI-prohibited arm (exact per-arm split not confirmed verbatim — see Verification status above). AI-permitted participants chose their own preferred tool (no single tool mandated) — hence the mixed E1/E2 tool inventory above. One AI-arm participant completed the task twice (excluded/flagged for non-independence); one prohibited-arm participant self-reported having used AI anyway (reclassified).
- **Phase 2 (n=75 completed Task 2):** an entirely new, independent set of participants, randomly assigned (stratified random sampling) to evolve either an AI-derived Phase 1 solution or a non-AI-derived one, blind to which they had received. This randomisation is what makes Phase 2 a true RCT on the *downstream-maintenance* question specifically, rather than an observational comparison.
- **Data-quality handling:** 48.3% of participants (73/151) did not complete their task in one uninterrupted sitting (remote, unsupervised task administration via `snapcode.review`); time estimates were substituted for interrupted sessions; 11 participants with implausible completion times (>16 hours) were excluded from timing analysis.
- **Outcome measures ("what maintainability was operationalised as" — directly relevant to comparing against this project's other quality-debt constructs):**
  1. **Completion time** (Phase 2: how long the *evolving* developer took) — a proxy for downstream maintenance effort, conceptually closest to this project's construct (d) *downstream-agent-code maintenance cost*, except the "maintainer" here is a human evolving human-typed-with-AI-suggestions code, not an agent evolving agent-authored code.
  2. **CodeHealth** — CodeScene's proprietary file-level composite metric (1–10 scale), built from thresholded cyclomatic complexity (≥4) and nesting depth (≥3) among other code-smell indicators, customised for this study. This is the construct most directly comparable to base construct (c) *complexity/static-warnings growth* (the CMU trio's SonarQube-style complexity and static-warning metrics) — both are static, structural composite scores computed on the code as committed, not on any downstream cost of working with it. It is **not** the same instrument as GitClear's proprietary churn/duplication/refactor-line classifier, and not the same as construct (d)'s task-resolve-rate/behavioural-drift measures in `agent-maintainability-2026`.
  3. **Test coverage** (JaCoCo line coverage, 0–100%) — a construct not otherwise used elsewhere in this project's quality-debt evidence base; closest analogue would be a verification-cost/thoroughness proxy, relevant more to RQ4 than to tension 4 directly.
  4. **Perceived productivity** — a 10-item, 5-point Likert self-report scale, given to Phase 2 participants. Paired with the *measured* completion-time outcome on the same participants, this is a direct measured-vs-self-reported pair in the RQ2 sense (CLAUDE.md rule 4) — both came back null in this study, so there is no perception–reality *gap* to report here, but the fact that both instruments agree (on a null) in the same sample is itself worth noting for RQ2 as a case where self-report and measurement did not diverge.
- **Analysis:** both frequentist (Wilcoxon rank-sum for time/coverage, presumably non-normal; Welch's t-test for CodeHealth/perceived-productivity) and Bayesian analysis (posterior 95% credible intervals, "probability of direction ≥95%" significance threshold, with sensitivity analysis across optimistic/pessimistic/neutral priors) were run and reported side by side — a stronger-than-typical robustness posture for a Tier A source in this project.

## Key findings

**Phase 1 — measured productivity gain (not the paper's main claim, but its most quotable number):**
- **30.7% median reduction in task completion time** for participants who used an AI assistant during feature development (measured, Phase 1, AI-permitted vs AI-prohibited arm).
- **Habitual AI users** (those self-rating "strongly agree" to being a habitual AI-assistant user while programming) showed an estimated **55.9% speedup** — larger than the pooled 30.7% figure, i.e. the effect concentrates in frequent/practised users rather than occasional ones.
- These are **measured** completion-time effects (task-timer data), consistent in direction and rough magnitude with prior Copilot-era RCT literature (Peng et al. 21–56%) already in this project's base — this paper corroborates the *productivity* side of the E1/E2 literature rather than adding new territory there.

**Phase 2 — the null (the paper's headline claim; exact test statistics not independently confirmed — see Verification status):**
- **No significant difference in completion time** between developers evolving AI-derived vs. non-AI-derived code (Wilcoxon rank-sum, reported as non-significant; exact p/Cliff's delta not extracted in this pass).
- **No significant difference in CodeHealth** after evolution (Welch's t-test, non-significant; exact Cohen's d not extracted in this pass).
- **No significant difference in test coverage** (Wilcoxon rank-sum, non-significant).
- **No significant difference in perceived productivity** (Welch's t-test, non-significant) — the self-report instrument agrees with the measured instrument in finding nothing, on this occasion.
- **Bayesian framing, quoted directly:** "any speed or quality improvements from AI use were at most small and highly uncertain" — note this is a symmetric null (no detectable *penalty*, but also no detectable *benefit*), not evidence that AI-derived code is easier to maintain, only that this study could not distinguish it from equally maintainable.
- **One non-null subgroup signal:** Bayesian analysis found a **small CodeHealth improvement specifically when the original Phase 1 solution had been co-developed by a self-identified habitual AI user** — the only non-null result in the entire Phase 2 analysis, and it points in the *positive* (better-health) direction, not toward a penalty. Reported with appropriate uncertainty language by the authors themselves, not oversold.
- **No defect, incident, or production-quality data of any kind** — same absence as every other source in this project's quality-debt base except `he-2026-2x-mandate`'s revert-rate proxy; CodeHealth, test coverage, and completion time are all pre-production, static/procedural proxies.

**Authors' own stated limitations (recorded in full, not cherry-picked):**
- CodeHealth is calibrated for large production systems; applying a custom-thresholded version to a small experimental codebase (RecipeFinder) introduces construct-validity uncertainty the authors flag themselves.
- Phase 2's n=75 is **explicitly stated by the authors as underpowered** for detecting small-to-moderate effects (their own power estimate: d≈0.5 at power≈0.57) — this is a null obtained at low power, which materially weakens how much weight the null can bear; the authors do not claim to have ruled out a real but modest effect.
- Volunteer/convenience sampling — the authors note the sample skews toward developers with limited experience specifically reviewing AI-generated code (as opposed to writing code with AI assistance), a mismatch with the reviewer-experience profile that would matter most for a maintainability question.
- Remote, unsupervised task administration reduces experimental control (nearly half the sessions were interrupted).
- Pre-registration is explicitly cited by the authors as their main defence against reporting bias, alongside the dual frequentist/Bayesian sensitivity analysis.

## What this can and cannot support

**Can support:** that, in a small-codebase, single-language (Java), volunteer-sample, E1/E2-tool (predominantly GitHub Copilot autocomplete, with some ChatGPT/Cursor-as-chat) setting circa late 2024, human developers evolving AI-co-developed code showed no *detectable* difference in completion time, structural code-health, or test coverage versus evolving non-AI code — a genuine, well-designed, pre-registered null, obtained via the strongest causal design (a true RCT on the maintainer's exposure) of any downstream-maintenance study in this project's base. Can also support the Phase 1 productivity figures (30.7% / 55.9%) as further E1/E2 corroboration of the productivity literature, and the paired null measured/self-reported result as an RQ2 data point where the two instruments agreed rather than diverged.

**Cannot support:** any claim about agentic-era (E3) tools or agent-authored code — no participant used a plan-execute-verify agentic tool to produce the Phase 1 code, so this null says nothing directly about Cursor-agent, Claude Code, or Devin-class output; any claim about codebases resembling production/legacy scale (RecipeFinder is a small experimental app, not a mature brownfield system); any claim ruling out a real effect of a magnitude smaller than the study's own stated power to detect (d≈0.5, power≈0.57 — the authors' own caveat, not an inference of ours); any claim about production defect rates, incidents, or rework hours (none measured); any claim of full independence from vendor interest (CodeHealth is CodeScene's own commercial metric, and one author's employer).

## Cross-ref adjudication — does this rebut, bound, or miss tension 4's legs (c) and (d)?

**Verdict: bounds, does not rebut — and mostly on a different axis than legs (c)/(d) test.**

1. **Construct overlap is partial.** CodeHealth (complexity/nesting composite) is a reasonable analogue to leg (c)'s SonarQube-style complexity/warnings metrics — same *family* of static-structural proxy. Completion time on a downstream evolution task is a genuine, if narrower, analogue to leg (d)'s downstream-maintenance-cost question, and arguably a *cleaner* causal instrument for it than `agent-maintainability-2026`'s task-resolve-rate measure, because Phase 2 randomises the maintainer's exposure directly rather than inferring cost from an observational agent-vs-agent comparison. So this is not a "different construct, doesn't engage" case — it is testing recognisably the same *kind* of question as both legs.
2. **The era/authorship-mode mismatch is the decisive limitation.** Tension 4's two best-supported legs are built entirely on E3, agent-authored code: the CMU trio's complexity/warnings growth (leg c) and `agent-maintainability-2026`'s task-resolve-rate collapse (leg d) both study large, autonomously-generated multi-file diffs from Cursor/Claude Code-class agents, reviewed and merged with comparatively light human line-by-line scrutiny. Borg et al. study the opposite authorship mode: a human developer typing their own code with autocomplete suggestions or chat-copied snippets (predominantly Copilot, E1), reviewing and integrating every line themselves, in late 2024 — before agentic tools were the dominant workflow anywhere in this evidence base. Per PROTOCOL's own era-transfer rule (§2), E1/E2 findings do not transfer across the E2→E3 boundary by default, and this null is a textbook case of why: the entire quality-debt hypothesis in tension 4 is specifically about what changes when an agent, not a human, is the primary author of the diff — a mechanism this study's population never encountered.
3. **It is not merely silent on E3 — it plausibly reinforces the *shape* of the existing story.** If a large, well-powered, pre-registered RCT specifically on the pre-agentic, human-typed-with-assistance paradigm finds *no* complexity/maintainability penalty, that is mildly consistent with (not contradictory to) a narrative in which the quality-debt effect is a genuinely new, agentic-scale-specific phenomenon rather than something that was already present and merely under-detected in the Copilot era. It narrows the space in which the CMU trio's E3 findings could be dismissed as "this was always true of any AI assistance, just newly measured" — Borg's null in the E1/E2 setting argues against that alternative explanation, if anything strengthening rather than weakening the E3-specific reading of legs (c)/(d).
4. **The COI and power caveats bound how much weight even the E1/E2 null itself can bear.** Low power (d≈0.5, ~0.57) means this study could not have detected a small-to-moderate penalty even within its own population; CodeHealth is the vendor-author's own commercial metric. Neither caveat is fatal to the null (pre-registration limits the practical bite of the COI; the authors disclose their own power limitation rather than hiding it), but both mean this source should be read as "no penalty detected, with real limits on how hard we should have expected to detect one" rather than "penalty ruled out."

**Recommended revision to the tension-4 verdict:** the "Contested-but-substantiated, medium" verdict does **not** need to change grade or direction — Borg et al. does not rebut legs (c) or (d) because it never tests the E3/agentic-authorship mechanism those legs are about. But §4 (era + independence audit) should be updated to add Borg as a **new, fully independent (no author/dataset overlap with the CMU trio, GitClear, or `agent-maintainability-2026`) null specifically on the E1/E2 side** of the complexity/maintainability question — this is useful triangulation for the *pre-agentic* half of the picture (previously under-evidenced on the null side) and should be logged in `contradictions.md` as bounding context, not as a live contradiction of legs (c)/(d). §6 ("what would change the verdict") should add: **an E3-era replication of Borg's own Phase 2 design** — i.e., a pre-registered RCT that randomly assigns human maintainers to evolve agent-authored vs. non-agent-authored code, rather than inferring cost from agent-on-agent task-resolve rates — as one of the most valuable studies that could actually settle leg (d), since Borg's methodology (not its era) is arguably the strongest instrument for that specific question that exists anywhere in this evidence base.
