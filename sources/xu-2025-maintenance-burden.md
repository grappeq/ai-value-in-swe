# Source record: xu-2025-maintenance-burden

## Identity (verified against arXiv:2510.10165)

- **Current title (v3, 28 Jan 2026):** "AI-Assisted Programming Decreases the Productivity of Experienced Developers by Increasing the Technical Debt and Maintenance Burden"
- **Note — title changed across versions.** v1 (11 Oct 2025) and v2 (24 Oct 2025) carried a more hedged title, "AI-assisted Programming May Decrease the Productivity of Experienced Developers by Increasing Maintenance Burden" (this is the title several aggregators/caches, e.g. papers.cool and ADS, still show, and it's what the assignment brief expected). v3 drops the hedge ("May Decrease" → "Decreases") and adds "Technical Debt" to the title. This is a genuine authorial escalation of the claim between versions, not a typo — worth flagging: the paper's own confidence in causal wording increased between drafts without (as far as I could establish) new data or a materially different design.
- **Authors:** Feiyang Xu, Poonacha K. Medappa, Murat M. Tunc, Martijn Vroegindeweij, Jan C. Fransoo — Tilburg University, Netherlands.
- **Status:** Not peer-reviewed / not journal-published as of v3. It is a working paper cross-posted to SSRN (abstract=5521379) and arXiv, presented at WITS 2025, CIST 2025, SCECR 2025, INFORMS 2024 — these are information-systems/management-science conference and workshop presentations, not peer-reviewed journal venues. Treat as a working paper, not a refereed publication.
- **No retraction or critique found.** A web search for critiques/replications turned up only the paper's own cross-postings (SSRN, RePEc/IDEAS, ResearchGate, ADS, papers.cool) — no independent commentary or replication located as of this check (17 Jul 2026).

## Method (verified from full text, arxiv.org/html/2510.10165v3)

- **Design:** Difference-in-differences (DiD), treating GitHub Copilot's 29 June 2021 technical-preview launch as a natural experiment. Treatment = repositories in five "Copilot-endorsed" languages (Python, JavaScript, Ruby, TypeScript, Go); control = repositories in non-endorsed languages (R, C, C#, C++, Java, PHP, Scala). Repository + month-year fixed effects at project level; individual + month-year fixed effects (errors clustered at individual level) at contributor level, with a `Core Contributor × Copilot` interaction term.
- **Sample:** 2,755 repositories (1,660 treatment / 1,095 control); 1,699 individual contributors (1,186 treatment / 513 control) after filtering out 5,308 of 37,334 raw contributors who worked across >3 repos. 66,120–66,168 repo-month observations; 51,408 contributor-month observations. Window: July 2020–July 2022 (12 months pre/post launch).
- **Repositories studied are Microsoft-owned OSS projects specifically** (stated explicitly by the authors as a scope choice). This is an important, easy-to-miss external-validity constraint: the whole sample sits inside the commercial ecosystem of Copilot's own vendor, and "our interviews with Microsoft employees" is cited as a source of anecdotal context — the paper does not report any COI disclosure or funding statement, and the authors are not Microsoft-affiliated, but the data source is entirely Microsoft's own repos.
- **Core vs peripheral classification:** contributors split by pre-treatment commit volume; **top 25% = "core," bottom 75% = "peripheral."** This is a within-sample relative threshold, not an external seniority/tenure measure (no tenure, role, or expertise data used).
- **Treatment is repo-language-level, not individual-level.** The paper is explicit that "Copilot usage cannot be identified at the individual level" — treatment is inferred from working in an endorsed-language repo, not from confirmed tool usage. Compounding this, "access to Copilot was restricted to selected GitHub users, specifically employees of Microsoft/GitHub and maintainers of popular projects" during the technical-preview window that is the entire study period — so a meaningful share of the nominal "treatment" group plausibly never touched Copilot at all. This is the single biggest identification weakness: it should bias estimated effects toward zero (attenuation), which the authors could have used to argue their (significant) findings are conservative, but they do not make this argument explicitly.
- **Robustness:** Coarsened Exact Matching (CEM) on pre-treatment characteristics (n=2,510 matched) reproduces the main results. Oster (2019) sensitivity analysis reports δ≈7.82 for the PR-rework effect, i.e. unobservables would need to be ~7.8× as selection-relevant as observables to zero out the effect — a genuine, well-executed robustness check, though it only bounds omitted-variable bias, not the attenuation problem above. Event-study pre-trends are statistically insignificant (parallel-trends assumption supported).

## Era — this is E1 (autocomplete), not agentic, despite the 2025/2026 publication date

The tool studied is **GitHub Copilot's June 2021 technical preview, built on OpenAI's GPT-3**, over a July 2021–July 2022 window. This long predates Copilot Chat (2023) and any agentic mode. The paper does not use the words "autocomplete" or "inline suggestion" anywhere I could find explicitly, but the tool, model backbone and date are unambiguous: this is the original ghost-text, single-point inline-completion product. **Per PROTOCOL §2's era-assignment rule (tag by modality studied, not publication year), this is a hard E1 tag.** A 2025/2026 arXiv date must not be read as agentic-era evidence. Per the era-transfer rule (§2), this paper's effect sizes cannot support agentic-era exec-brief claims and must carry an explicit "pre-agentic" label if used; it is admissible as (a) historical baseline, and (c) evidence for the plausibly era-independent effects it documents — specifically **seniority asymmetry** and **bottleneck displacement**, both named explicitly in §2/§5 as candidate era-independent mechanisms.

## Tier grading

**Tier B — structured observational, large-scale telemetry without randomisation.** Not Tier A: treatment is not randomised, is only a repo-language proxy for actual Copilot access (and access itself was gated to a narrow population during the study window), and the whole sample is confined to one vendor's own repos. It clears Tier B comfortably: real counterfactual (control-language repos), large N, fixed effects, event-study pre-trend check, CEM matching, and an Oster sensitivity bound — more rigour than the median Tier C practitioner write-up, but the treatment-identification weakness and narrow (Microsoft-only) sample keep it out of Tier A. Carries claims with caveats, per §3.

**Independence:** Authors are academic (Tilburg University), not vendor employees, and there is no stated funding/COI. However, (a) the underlying data is 100% Microsoft-owned repositories, and (b) the authors cite interviews with Microsoft employees for interpretive context. Not a COI in the Tier D vendor-report sense, but flag the data-source dependency — findings about "OSS" behaviour here are really findings about Microsoft's internal OSS estate, which may have atypical incentives (e.g., MS engineers had early Copilot access and possible reputational incentive to demonstrate adoption).

## Headline findings

### 1. Project-level productivity rises (measured, DiD, E1)
Post-Copilot, treatment repos show: lines of code added +17.7% (coef 0.163, p<0.01), commits +4.1% (coef 0.04, p<0.1), pull requests +4.3% (coef 0.042, p<0.01). Positive, modest-to-moderate, broadly consistent with prior Copilot productivity literature (Peng et al. 2023) but measured via telemetry rather than a lab RCT.

### 2. Productivity gains are concentrated in peripheral (low pre-treatment activity) contributors
Individual-level main Copilot effect for peripheral contributors: commits +14.2% (coef 0.142, p<0.01), PRs +9.1% (coef 0.091, p<0.01). This is the paper's basis for "productivity gains ... primarily driven by less-experienced (peripheral) developers."

### 3. Core contributors' own commit output falls — but the paper reports two different magnitudes for this, and they don't reconcile
- **Table 6 (individual regression, binary Core×Copilot interaction term):** commits interaction coefficient −0.357 (p<0.01) → exp(−0.357)−1 ≈ **−42.9%** relative to the rest of contributors. This is the number stated explicitly in the Section 4.2 prose ("a decrease of 42.9%").
- **Figure 5(a) (quartile/continuous heterogeneity plot):** top-25%-by-pretreatment-activity group shows a **−19% reduction** in commits.
- **The abstract's headline claim ("a 19% drop in their original code productivity") uses the Figure 5 number, not the Table 6 number that appears in the paper's own body text discussion of the same core/peripheral split.** I made two independent extraction passes over the full text and both surfaced the same two figures without any reconciling passage — there is no footnote or methods note in the parts of the paper I could retrieve that explains why a binary top-25% threshold model and a continuous quartile-binned model of the *same* underlying core-group definition produce a 24-point spread (−19% vs −42.9%) on the same outcome variable. **This is a genuine internal inconsistency, not a reading error on my part as far as I can tell** — flagging as a red flag rather than resolving it myself, since the two model specifications aren't described in enough overlap to adjudicate which is the "real" number, and the headline abstract figure is the smaller, more conservative one of the two.

### 4. Core contributors' review burden rises
Table 6 interaction terms: PR reviews +6.1% (coef 0.06, p<0.1), PR-reviewed-repositories (breadth of review activity) +4.6% (coef 0.045, p<0.05). The abstract's "review 6.5% more code" most likely derives from the 6.1% PR-review coefficient (nearest match), but I could not find the exact 6.5% figure verbatim anywhere in the retrieved body text — it may be a rounding/recomputation the authors did in the abstract that isn't shown in-table, or a blend of the two interaction terms. Flagging as unverified-exact rather than asserting the correspondence.

### 5. Peripheral contributors' PR submissions rise most at the bottom of the experience distribution
Figure 5(b): bottom-quartile (0–25%) contributors show +17.7% more PR submissions, the largest gain of any quartile — a clean monotonic gradient (gains shrink, then reverse, as pre-treatment activity/seniority rises).

### 6. Technical debt proxy: PR rework rises modestly at project level
"PR rework" is operationalised as the count of commits added to a pull request *after* its initial submission (i.e. follow-up commits made in response to review feedback) — a reasonable, directly-measured proxy for "code that didn't meet standards on first submission," not a self-reported or survey-based quality measure. Project-level effect: +2.4% (coef 0.024, p<0.05). The event-study specification shows this effect growing over the post-treatment window ("upward trend ... that gradually becomes more positive over time"), i.e. accumulating rather than one-off — this is the paper's basis for calling it "technical debt" rather than one-time friction.

### 7. Extrapolated annual burden (authors' own back-of-envelope, not a separately estimated model)
Using baseline annual averages (976 commits, 160 PRs, 166 PR reviews per core contributor), the authors extrapolate: each core contributor reviews ~10 more PRs/year and their own output falls by ~164 commits and ~9 PRs/year. This is a multiplication of the regression coefficients by baseline levels, not an independent estimate — treat as illustrative arithmetic, not a new finding.

## Nulls / negative results

The paper reports **no null or negative-and-insignificant headline results** — every coefficient discussed in the text is significant at p<0.1 or better. This is worth flagging as a quality note in its own right (per CLAUDE.md rule 3, recording nulls with equal diligence includes noting their *absence*): a paper with this many outcome variables (commits, PRs, PR reviews, reviewed-repos, LOC, rework) reporting universal significance is either a genuinely strong effect, or reflects some combination of researcher-degrees-of-freedom in which outcomes/specifications got reported, or the DiD's large N mechanically producing significance on small effect sizes. The CEM and Oster checks mitigate this concern somewhat but do not eliminate it — no pre-registration is mentioned.

## Limitations (explicit and inferred)

- **Access-gated treatment period**: findings apply to the technical-preview period only (restricted to Microsoft/GitHub employees and popular-project maintainers), not the June 2022+ general-availability period — the paper itself does not discuss whether/how findings might differ post-GA, which is a notable gap given GA is when most real-world Copilot usage happened.
- **Repo-language proxy for treatment**, not confirmed individual usage (discussed above).
- **Single-vendor, single-company data source** (Microsoft-owned repos only) — the paper frames this as enabling clean measurement, but it caps external validity to "how Microsoft's own OSS contributors behaved" rather than OSS broadly.
- **No task-type breakdown** — the taxonomy in this protocol (T1–T11) isn't something the paper distinguishes; "commits/PRs/reviews" are undifferentiated by task type (feature work vs bug fix vs refactor), so it can't inform *which* task types drive the effect, only that it exists in aggregate OSS contribution activity.
- **No individual-level seniority/tenure/role data** — "core" is a relative, sample-internal percentile, not a job title, years-of-experience, or external skill measure. Caution before mapping directly onto "senior engineer" in an enterprise context — an enterprise's core/senior engineers are not equivalent to "top-25%-by-OSS-commit-volume open-source contributors."

## Can / cannot support

**Can support:** (1) the seniority-asymmetry tension (§5.5) as E1/pre-agentic evidence, with a genuinely new mechanism beyond Cui et al.'s "gains concentrate in juniors" — this paper adds that *seniors' burden measurably rises* (more review, less own output), not just that their gains are smaller; (2) the bottleneck-displacement tension (§5.6) as direct, quantified, era-independent-candidate evidence — review load rising while code-submission volume rises faster is exactly the mechanism §5.6 describes; (3) RQ4 cost profile with one of the few quantified, directly-measured (not self-reported) rework/review-overhead numbers in the literature — valuable given RQ4's pre-registered thin-evidence expectation; (4) a caution for RQ6 that raw output metrics (commits, PRs, LOC) can rise in aggregate while masking a burden shift onto a shrinking pool of reviewers — a concrete anti-metric argument.

**Cannot support:** any agentic-era (E3) effect-size claim; any claim about brownfield/mature enterprise codebases specifically (this is OSS, mixed maturity, not explicitly brownfield-tagged); any claim about code quality beyond the rework proxy (no defect-rate, incident, or security data); the precise "19% vs 42.9% vs 6.5%" headline figures should not be quoted in the exec brief without picking one specification and noting the other exists — quoting the abstract's numbers uncritically risks repeating an internal inconsistency the paper itself doesn't resolve.

## Quality notes summary (red flags)

1. Title escalated from hedged ("May Decrease") to unhedged ("Decreases") between v1/v2 and v3 without an apparent new-data justification.
2. Abstract's headline "19%" and Table 6's in-text "42.9%" describe the same core/Copilot commit-decline relationship via two different model specifications, unreconciled in the text.
3. Abstract's "6.5%" review-increase figure doesn't exactly match either in-table coefficient (6.1% or 4.6%); nearest match is 6.1% but not exact.
4. Data source is entirely Microsoft-owned repos studied by non-Microsoft academics — not a COI in the Tier D sense, but a real external-validity dependency worth naming.
5. Universal statistical significance across every reported outcome, with no discussion of multiple-comparisons or pre-registration.
6. Not peer-reviewed; presented only at IS/management workshops and conferences as of this check.
