## What this document is
A staged systematic literature review (SIG-SOFT Empirical Standard) of human-centred studies of AI coding assistants and developer productivity. Stage 1 covers papers that explicitly study productivity (27); stage 2 adds papers with SPACE-aligned developer outcomes not labelled as productivity (18). It does not do a meta-analysis (declared infeasible). It extracts how productivity is operationalised (markers), which confounders are discussed, and the direction of reported effects, coded as positive / negative / mixed / neutral.

## Full notes
**Method and funnel.**
- Inclusion: peer-reviewed, preprint or industry report with original empirical design and results (IC6); human developers; publicly available in English. Exclusions: published before 2023 (EC1); student/teaching-only populations; automated benchmarks and LLM-vs-LLM; other grey literature.
- Three sources: (a) venue search across 12 SE venues, 3,746 papers screened, 1 stage-1 hit and 4 further stage-2 hits (5 total); (b) Semantic Scholar API, 120 refined key-phrase combinations, up to 20 results each, 893 unique candidates, 13 stage-1 and 13 stage-2 hits (26 total as printed); (c) manual Google/Google Scholar, 10 key phrases, first 20 results each, 79 further unique candidates, 13 stage-1 and 1 stage-2 hit (14 total). These sum to 27 and 18 = 45.
- Search terms: tool-type placeholders are chatgpt, LLM, coding assistant, chatbot, copilot; person-type placeholders are human, participant, developer, practitioner, expert. No agent-related term appears.
- Screening by the first author; third author independently screened the first 1,000 venue titles and the second author reviewed venue abstracts for calibration. Completeness checked against a 10-paper control set; search stopped once all were recovered. No snowballing; search date not stated.
- Window: starts 2023. Latest cited empirical items are 2025 (e.g. Becker et al. 2025, Faros 2025, Turing 2025, Weisz 2025, Xu 2025). Manuscript received March 2026.

**Corpus (Table 1).** 45 papers: 27 stage 1, 18 stage 2. Peer-reviewed 26/45 (57.8%): 10/27 (37.0%) stage 1, 16/18 (88.9%) stage 2. Methods: surveys (26) and experiments (12) dominate. N ranges 5 to 88,022 (smallest a think-aloud study, largest a repository-activity analysis, Bonabi et al.). Study length: 34/45 one-shot, 5 short-term, 6 long-term (all six in stage 1). Stage 1 papers used SPACE in 5 cases and other frameworks in 3. Included grey sources include Faros AI (2025), Microsoft Security Copilot report (2025), Turing (2025), Gorilla Logic (2024).

**Markers (Table 2, Figure 1).**
- 418 marker occurrences (178 stage 1, 240 stage 2); 187 unique markers (91 stage 1, 124 stage 2); 20 categories, four groups (AI, artefact/repository, organisational/process, human). Median unique markers per paper: 5 in each stage (6 overall); median 3 to 4 categories. The authors' headline: broad corpus, narrow per-study coverage.
- Largest categories: Time 84 occurrences (65% of the 128 organisational/process markers, split about evenly between objective and perceived), AI assessment 60 (68% of 87 AI markers), Code quality 43 (29 unique markers), Satisfaction 30, Direct productivity assessment 29 (27 in stage 1), Psychological aspects 27, Repository activity 25 (21 in stage 1), Personal career 14, Security 12, Well-being 9 (all perceived, three papers, stage 2 only), Code volume 8, CI activity 3, Production activity 3 (stage 1 only).
- Most common pairing in stage 1: Direct productivity assessment with Time (9 papers); stage 2: AI assessment with Time (13 papers).
- Single-paper markers include time until PR closed, number of tasks per day, PR cycle time, requirement completion per minute, characters per minute (several of these appear once or in one or two papers per Figure 1); perceived items such as guilt, job-related stress, mental health impact and AI-driven time pressure each come from one paper (Meem and Johnson; Bird et al.). The paper does not list a single count of "markers used once", but the 187-vs-median-5 contrast is the point it makes.
- Even central constructs are comparable only by direction: task completion time is reported in seconds, minutes, hours or percentages, measured directly in 6 papers and via Likert, closed-response or qualitative coding in others. Time to PR merged and time to PR review/approved are related but not identical.
- Stage 1 is artefact- and proxy-heavy (time, output, repository); stage 2 recovers satisfaction, psychological aspects and well-being. PERMANOVA found no difference in marker distribution by peer-review status.

**Confounders (RQ2, stage 1 only).** 44 unique confounders in 15 of the 27 stage-1 papers; median 1 per paper; 21 of the 44 come from one paper (Becker et al., the METR study, which has a negative primary finding). Most frequent: developer experience (6 papers), AI tool experience (4), project/task complexity (4), project involvement (2), usage approach (2), usage frequency (2). 38 are reported once (e.g. programming skill, language experience, prompting behaviour, AI output accuracy and speed). Tendencies: juniors tend to benefit more (5 papers); seniors mixed (smaller gains in 2, no significant effect in 1, exclusive benefit in 1, decline in 1); tool experience linked to neutral-to-positive impact (4 papers); higher task complexity linked to less positive impact (3 papers). The authors decline to generalise. The same variable (AI output accuracy, speed) is an outcome in some studies and a confounder in others. Codebase maturity/age and verification cost are not among the named recurring confounders; "maintenance burden" appears only as the mechanism in one included paper's title (Xu et al.) and code-quality markers. This is an inference from the lists given, not a stated finding; the full confounder list is in the Zenodo supplement.

Pooled Fisher exact tests (exploratory, within-paper dependence not modelled): omnibus association of direction with reporting type p<.001 (pairwise Holm-adjusted: tendencies vs proportions p<.001, tendencies vs effects p<.05, proportions vs effects p<.01 as extracted; symbols were partly lost in the PDF text, so treat the thresholds as approximate); perceived vs objective p<.05; grey vs non-grey not significant. Verbatim abstract wording: "Reported effects are often positive, but this pattern is stronger in perceived and tendency-based findings than in objective or statistically evaluated ones."
- Time is almost exclusively positive; exceptions: time until PR merged (one study: significant increase, classed negative, yet "more timely merges", positive; Song et al.) and time until PR reviewed/approved (three studies: one significant increase, one significant decrease, one non-significant).
- Code quality: 3 of 16 reports negative (more bugs/issues in two papers, more PR revisions in one).
- AI assessment is the most negative category, 5 of 8 (62%) negative, mostly tendency-based reports about accuracy and hallucination. The authors point out that productivity markers trend positive while evaluations of the tool trend negative.
- Likert items such as "my productivity is improved" cannot express a negative effect (disagreement may mean no change or harm).

**Stated limitations.** Interpretive coding of markers/confounders; confounders captured only if discussed; mixed source quality (peer-reviewed, preprint, industry); possible missed papers (no snowballing, no further databases); SPACE alignment needed judgement; many markers appear in few studies, so directional summaries are weak; statistics exploratory.

**Implications for measurement (RQ-level).** State which evidence type is reported (perceived productivity, observed behaviour change, tested effect) and do not treat them as interchangeable; specify which productivity dimensions are assessed; include human-centred outcomes; treat context as core design; do not conflate productivity with tool quality; consider downstream correction effort and maintenance burden. DevEx guidance cited: combine subjective perceptions with objective process indicators. Conclusion: "the current literature does not point to a single stable answer"; evidence "does not yet support simple, context-independent conclusions".

**Agentic-era coverage.** The review never mentions agentic tools; it does not state that agentic evidence is scarce. Corpus tools are Copilot, ChatGPT and similar assistants. The closest case is Becker et al. 2025 (METR, early-2025 AI tooling, experienced open-source developers; the review treats it as a negative-finding outlier with 21 confounders), which the review does not classify by modality. Other 2025 items (Faros, Turing, Weisz, Xu, Bonabi) are not described in the paper's text by tool modality; the supplement may name tools. No Claude Code, Cursor-agent or Devin-class study is named.

## Tier & era grading
- Tier: A for method (a protocol-driven systematic review with a documented funnel, control-set validation and calibration subsets, used as an instrument on the literature), but not a primary effect-size source; for effect claims it is no better than Tier B-minus because it codes direction only, with single-screener extraction and mixed source quality. Recommended use: methodology and measurement-validity evidence (RQ2, RQ6), not effect sizes.
- Era: E1/E2 by the modalities covered (autocomplete and chat assistants; window 2023 onwards, evidence up to 2025). The perceived-vs-objective asymmetry is an era-independent measurement finding (PROTOCOL §2(b)/(c)), admissible as such; the percentages must carry a "pre-agentic" label if used in the exec brief.

## Measured vs self-reported
This is the review's central empirical contrast. Across 167 coded stage-1 results, perceived markers are 71.1% positive (64/90) vs objective 54.5% (42/77); negative 6.7% vs 14.3%; neutral 16.7% vs 29.9%. By reporting form, tendency-based findings 81.5% positive vs statistically evaluated 53.9%. Caveats: these are not within-study deltas (the same study does not necessarily report both), direction only, no magnitudes, pooled across tasks and tools, and the authors note that positively worded Likert items structurally cannot record harm, which inflates apparent neutrality of perceived items rather than positivity per se. Perceived items also include the "Direct productivity assessment" category (29 occurrences). This is corpus-level support for an RQ2 perception-vs-reality gap, but it does not measure the gap in the way the METR RCT does.

## Can / cannot support - verdicts
- CAN support (medium confidence, pre-agentic label): literature-level claim that positive results in the 2023-2025 assistant literature are concentrated in perceived and tendency-based evidence, with objective and tested findings showing more neutral and negative results (54.5% vs 71.1% positive).
- CAN support (high confidence for the descriptive point): measurement fragmentation (187 unique markers, median 5 per study) and that time/output/artefact proxies dominate; hence cross-study effect comparison is by direction only. Relevant to RQ2 and to the RQ6 anti-metrics argument (output proxies, single-source self-report).
- CAN support (low-medium): confounder reporting is sparse (median 1 per paper; 44 unique in 15 of 27 papers) and the literature rarely isolates task or codebase context.
- CANNOT support: any effect size, any agentic-era (E3) claim, any claim about task types, codebase maturity, verification cost or RQ5 translation; the review reports none of these and codes direction only.
- CANNOT support: a claim that AI "is not productive"; the authors conclude assistants "can support productivity" but context-dependently.

## Red flags / things the orchestrator should know
1. Era: the corpus is E1/E2; do not cite the 63.5% / 71.1% / 54.5% figures as agentic-era effects. The review itself does not flag the agentic gap; our scarcity finding there is our inference, not the authors' statement.
2. Single-screener extraction; effect-direction coding is the authors' normalisation, and multiple results per paper are counted separately, so the Fisher tests overstate independence (authors say exploratory).
3. Possible overlap with existing sources in our corpus: the review includes Becker et al. (METR), Cui et al., Peng et al., Faros AI and Xu et al. If those are already extracted as primary sources, do not count the review's pooled percentages as independent corroboration (provenance cluster).
4. Precision loss in the PDF text for p-value thresholds; verify against the PDF rendering or the Zenodo supplement before quoting thresholds.
5. Award/venue: confirmed via the ASE 2026 track page, not the /details/56/ URL (404). Distinguished Paper status is a venue signal, not evidence of effect-size validity.
6. Search date is not stated; window is 2023 to the 2025 literature despite a March 2026 submission, so 2026 studies are absent.
7. Zenodo supplement (full marker list, per-study tool/modality, protocol) was not opened; a follow-up read could allow the E1/E2/E3 split by study to be derived exactly.
