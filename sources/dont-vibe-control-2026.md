## What this document is
A two-part qualitative study of experienced developers (>=3 years professional) using IDE/terminal agents: 13 think-aloud field observations and an invited 15-minute qualitative survey (n=99). It asks about values, strategies, task suitability and sentiments. It measures no outcomes (no time, quality, cost or throughput). It is a practitioner-behaviour signal, not an effect-size source.

## Full notes
### Method
- Observations (n=13): 1 female, 12 male; 12 recruited via networks, 1 snowball; 3-25 years (median 9, sd 6.5), verified by LinkedIn (12) or company site (1). Tools: Cursor 6, Claude Code 4 (one alongside Cursor, one with Codex, one with Windsurf), Copilot 2, Windsurf (2 incl. one with Claude Code), Kilo Code + Terragon 1, Codex 1. Models mostly Sonnet 4/4.5/3.7/3.5, GPT-5, o3, Gemini 2.5 Pro. Each: 45-minute observation of a self-selected task plus 30-minute interview, Zoom recorded, auto-transcribed. Tasks: 5 production work (P2, P3, P6, P7, P13), 3 exploratory R&D (P4, P5, P10), 5 non-work (OSS P1, P9, P12; side projects P8, P12 as listed). 5 of 13 on tasks outside their expertise. Sectors/companies not named; participants were described as adopting AI "due to workplace requirements".
- Survey (n=99): sampled from 4,141 GitHub users active in the past year in AI-related repositories (Cursor, Claude Code, Kilo Code, Cline, RooCode, AI/ML frameworks, agentic repos >=500 stars); 249 responses (6%); 104 qualified; 5 discarded as suspected AI-generated; 99 analysed. 97 male, 1 female, 1 undisclosed; 12.8 mean years (3-41, median 10). Regions: Asia 29, North America 27, Europe 24, South America 13, Africa 5, Oceania 1. Experience domain: full-stack 51, backend 21, AI/ML 15, frontend 10. Tools used: Claude Code 58, Copilot 53, Cursor 51, Windsurf 15, Codex 15, Roo Code 12, Gemini CLI 12, Cline 10. Most recent task: app building 53, backend 12, automation 10, refactoring 4. Instrument: 11 questions on the last agentic use (task, priorities, tool, prompting, multi-agent, modification frequency 1-5, suitability 1-6, enjoyment 1-6), 2 open questions on tasks preferred with/without agents, demographics. Three pilots (14 participants) excluded.
- Analysis: thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke), open coding by two authors, agreement by discussion, no inter-rater reliability; affinity mapping; secondary analysis of prompts (43 context types, top 10 reported) and a reflexive task-suitability coding (189 tasks, 89 codes, 59 with >=5 mentions; GPT-5-mini used to pull quotes). Five non-English responses machine-translated.

### Findings (counts as reported)
- Motivations (RQ1): productivity valued by 9 of 13 and 35 of 99; software quality attributes by 6 of 13 and 67 of 99 (correctness 20, readability 18 in the desired-attribute chart; security 2); 37 survey respondents named non-quality attributes; curiosity/peer influence 5 of 13 and 36 of 99.
- Control strategies (RQ2): see evidence row 3. All 13 controlled implementation; 11 of 13 controlled design; 9 of 13 reviewed every change (all in own domain); 3 on unfamiliar tasks monitored outputs and rejected dependencies instead of reviewing code. Largest plans up to 917 words / 71 steps (P1, P2) but never more than 6 steps executed at once; median 1.8 steps per prompt. Verification: CLI/tests (6), UI manual (4), IDE or git diff (3), debugger. Testing stayed manual for UI, databases and remote apps. Multi-agent use 4 of 13 and 31 of 99. User rules: 3 of 13 and 18 of 99. Plan files and context files: 3 of 13 (P1, P2, P11).
- Suitability (RQ3): see row 2 and Table 5 counts. Mean suitability 4.73/6; contested: high-level plans/architecture 13:23, general debugging 12:8, brainstorming 11:5, code review 7:4, understanding architecture 6:7. No respondent said agents could be fully autonomous.
- Sentiment (RQ4): enjoyment 5.11/6 (self-report), all observed participants positive. Agents as thinking partner: 34 of 99 and 5 of 13; humans want to drive brainstorming: 12 of 99 and 5 of 13. Concerns: agents doing more than asked (8 of 13), maintainability "crutch" for the inexperienced (P4), 6 of 13 stressed human understanding for production review.
- Anecdote: P3 reported a React migration in three days vs an estimated two weeks, "not caught a single bug" (single self-report; authors speculate React is common and prior scaffolding helped).

### "SE best practices underpin effective agent use"
Evidence: participants' own statements (domain expertise cited as critical by 11 of 13 and 65 of 99) and observed use of plans, diffs, tests and version control. Interpretation: that these practices make agent use effective. The study has no outcome or comparison group, so effectiveness is untested; the authors themselves say these patterns "may not be optimal", cite the METR -19% result, and call for validation. Treat as a description of what experienced developers do, not as proof that it works.

### Recommendations
Prompt with explicit context; draft multi-step plans in files and execute in chunks; do not expect to vibe; use Table 5 to choose use cases; expect early discouragement. Interface opportunities: automated testing in remote/realistic environments, UI debugging via prompting, explaining why an agent fails or hangs, and planning-step interfaces. Future work: study the most productive developers and verify agents are measurably helpful.

### Limitations (authors')
AI-positive selection (deliberate), small observation sample, 92% and 98% male, GitHub email scraping self-selection, unverifiable expertise of survey respondents, single 45-minute sessions with no longitudinal or full-cycle view. Authors also note agents have improved since the study.

## Tier & era grading
- Tier C (practitioner signal, academic). Not B: the survey is structured but is a self-selected 6% response rate from AI-enthusiast repositories with open-ended coding and no outcome metric; B is for telemetry or longitudinal industry reports. The 13 observations are valuable as observed behaviour but are small.
- Era E3: tools are agentic (Cursor agent, Claude Code, Windsurf, Copilot agent mode, Codex), data collected Aug-Oct 2025. Survey respondents also reported Copilot (53) so some tool answers may include non-agentic use, but eligibility required agentic tool experience (excludes web chat).
- Independence: academic, NSF-funded; no vendor or consultancy tie disclosed. Sample is drawn from AI-tool GitHub communities, so not independent of enthusiasm. Tier D flag: no.
- One Tier C source supports directional claims and mechanism hypotheses only (PROTOCOL §3); it contributes one independent source to a triangulated claim.

## Measured vs self-reported
- Observed behaviour (n=13, 45 minutes each): plan/prompt sizes, steps executed per prompt, review and verification tactics, scope-creep incidents. These are researcher-recorded counts, not outcome measures.
- Self-reported (n=99 and interviews): all survey ratings (suitability 4.73/6, enjoyment 5.11/6, modification 3.0/5), all S:U task-suitability counts, strategy mentions and motivations.
- No measured outcomes, so no perception-reality delta can be computed. The paper itself cites METR (-19%) and notes this open question.

## Can / cannot support — verdicts
Can: (i) directional claim that experienced developers in 2025 used agents with plan-first, small-step, diff-review control rather than autonomously; (ii) task-suitability perceptions: broad agreement on boilerplate, tests, docs, simple refactors and prototypes, and on legacy integration, business logic, security and architecture being kept human or contested (supports T3/T6/T10 versus T2/T4 mechanism hypotheses); (iii) a source for the "review/verification cost is human-borne" mechanism and for interface pain points.
Cannot: any effect size, productivity or quality claim; population prevalence (counts are mentions in a self-selected sample); claims about juniors; claims that the control practices improve outcomes; evidence on cost.

## Red flags / things the orchestrator should know
- Selection bias is deliberate and towards AI-positive respondents (6% response, GitHub email scraping); S counts will be inflated relative to the wider developer population.
- Sample is 98% male; sector/company of observed participants not given; only 5 of 13 observed tasks were production work.
- S:U counts are mentions and not mutually exclusive; the 2.5:1 threshold is the authors' judgement; one respondent may count in several rows.
- Title ("Don't vibe") is stronger than the evidence: 3 of 13 observed participants (on unfamiliar tasks) did not necessarily review code, and the survey's code-modification mean (3.0/5) implies substantial editing, not absence of vibing in all cases.
- v2 reframed the sentiment finding; cite v2 and note the version difference. Venue unconfirmed; preprint only.
- Task-suitability counts sit in tension with greenfield-vs-brownfield (§5.2): legacy integration 3:17 and complex 3:16 support it as perception only, not measurement.
- LLM use in analysis (machine translation, GPT-5-mini quote retrieval) is disclosed but unaudited.
- Not needing escalation: Tier C, no paywall, no taxonomy break (suitability themes map to T1-T11; "accelerating productivity" is not a task).
