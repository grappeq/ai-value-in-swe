## Tier & era & independence

- **Tier: D, mandatory per PROTOCOL §6 ("DX / LinearB / Swarmia / Faros-class published customer analyses — grade these D, not C, when vendor-published").** This is vendor-published customer telemetry from an engineering-intelligence analytics company, presented as a research report. COI flag is **non-negotiable** and is recorded below.
- **COI — and its direction is worth stating precisely, because it is the *inverse* of the usual vendor-report COI pattern.** Faros sells an "AI harness"/governance/observability platform (per the report's own closing description: "the system for running engineering with AI... visibility... and control... deploy AI effectively and improve engineering throughput with stronger cost efficiency"), and its own ten recommendations (pp.23–26) are close-to-direct pitches for exactly the product categories Faros sells: an "AI harness" with guardrails, a "context engine," automated review-comment-to-rule feedback loops, automated churn-investigation loops, and workflow-stage-time monitoring agents. Unlike GitHub/Copilot-class vendor reports (which have a commercial incentive to *oversell benefits*), Faros's commercial incentive here cuts toward **overselling the problem** — alarming quality/incident numbers create demand for the instrumentation and governance tooling Faros sells. Both directions of vendor COI degrade evidentiary weight equally; this is recorded as a directional COI note, not a mitigating factor.
- **Era: E2/E3 blend, with E3 well-represented and growing across the window — but the report's own "adoption" metric is not modality-split, and the single most consequential scope caveat in the whole document narrows the E3 claim further.** Tool list named explicitly (p.4): "Claude Code, Cursor, GitHub Copilot, Windsurf, and similar tools, as well as autonomous agents integrated directly into the SDLC." The report states code-acceptance-rate growth (20%→60%) is "driven substantially by tools like Cursor and Claude Code operating in agent mode" (p.7) — genuine E3 signal. Agentic PR *review* has grown from 0%→25% of PRs (p.6, p.8) — an autonomous-role E3 marker. **However, the report explicitly states that agentic PR *authorship* — an AI agent independently writing, committing, and submitting code without human initiation — is under 1% of PRs in this dataset (p.8), and the "What Engineering Organizations Should Do" section states this outright: "Agentic authoring currently accounts for less than 1% of PRs in this dataset... The data in this report reflects what happens when developers use AI as a primary authoring tool with humans still in the loop. Remove that human from the loop entirely, and every metric here faces pressure that is an order of magnitude greater"** (p.22). **This means the whiplash findings describe human-submitted, AI-assisted authoring at scale (agent-in-the-loop, human-in-the-loop-for-submission), not autonomous agentic authorship** — an important scope narrowing relative to how "agentic era" evidence is often invoked. Tag as **E2/E3, human-in-the-loop-authorship-dominant, with a growing E3 minority in review-role**; do not cite this source for claims specifically about autonomous-agent-authored code without this caveat attached.
- **Independence flags:** vendor-published, own customer base (Faros platform customers who opted into/constitute Faros's telemetry — self-selected by virtue of already buying engineering-intelligence tooling, the identical selection-bias pattern already documented for GitClear and Uplevel). One stated exclusion criterion: "Organizations running on non-standard infrastructure were excluded" (footnote 1, p.2) — criteria and impact on population representativeness are not further specified. **No randomisation, no counterfactual, no matched comparison group** — see Method section for the exact within-team before/after design and its confounds.

## Method & population/dataset

- **Measurement basis: measured (telemetry) throughout, explicitly and repeatedly contrasted against survey/self-report methods** (see "A direct counterpoint to DORA's 2025 findings," p.5) — "Real engineering data from every stage of the workflow, not self-reported estimates" (landing page framing, consistent with PDF's own framing). No self-reported companion metric is published alongside the telemetry findings in this report (unlike METR-style perception-gap studies) — there is therefore no internal measured-vs-self-reported delta to record from this source alone, only an *external* comparison the report itself draws against DORA 2025 (a self-report source) — see Cross-ref section.
- **Population:** 22,000 developers, "more than 4,000 teams," across the Faros platform, ~two years of telemetry (dataset window not stated as exact calendar dates; "analysis as of March 2026" with "approximately two years" of history implies roughly April 2024–March 2026, not independently confirmed from the PDF beyond "two years").
- **Telemetry sources (p.4):** task management systems, IDEs, static code analysis tools, CI/CD pipelines, version control systems, incident management systems, and HR system metadata.
- **AI-adoption definition:** teams counted as "high AI adoption" once they exceed a **50% weekly-active-user (WAU) threshold** for AI coding tools; 80% of teams in the 2026 dataset exceed this threshold (up from 50% in the 2025 dataset).
- **Exact comparison methodology (the load-bearing design choice — reproduced precisely because it determines what the percentages mean):**
  1. Standardise every metric **per company** to remove *between*-company variance (so a company with a naturally slow baseline doesn't bias the pooled figure).
  2. Compute **Spearman's rank correlation (ρ)** between each metric and AI-usage level.
  3. **Report only** metrics with data from **≥6 companies** and a **statistically significant correlation (p<0.05)** — this is a reporting filter (which metrics make the cut), not itself the headline number.
  4. **For each team, calculate the percent change in the metric's value between the two quarters of lowest AI adoption and the two quarters of highest AI adoption, within that team's own observation window** (up to ~2 years, aggregated by quarter).
  5. Exclude outliers and metrics with insufficient historical coverage.
  6. The published headline percentages are (per the appendix bar-chart y-axis label, "Avg. % Change (Low → High AI)") **averages across teams of these per-team, within-team percent changes**, restricted to companies/teams that passed the significance filter. Some metrics (deployment frequency, lead time) are computed over only the ~10% of the dataset that instruments them, flagged explicitly each time they appear.
- **What this design actually estimates, and its confounders (methodology verdict):** this is a **within-team, before/after comparison of two quarter-pairs selected from within an up-to-two-year window per team** — not a between-team high-adopter-vs-low-adopter comparison, and not a randomised or matched design.
  - Per-company standardisation removes cross-company *baseline-level* differences but does **not** remove within-company *time trends* unrelated to AI. Because industry-wide AI adoption rose roughly monotonically across almost exactly this two-year window, for most teams the "lowest AI adoption" quarter will fall early in the window and the "highest AI adoption" quarter will fall late in it — making this design functionally close to a **plain two-year before/after comparison per team**, with AI-adoption level used as the ordering variable rather than as an independently-varying treatment.
  - This invites the standard confounds of any long-window before/after design: **headcount and team-size change, codebase growth/maturity, product-portfolio shifts, non-AI tooling changes (new CI/CD, new incident-management systems), org-wide process changes (a push for stricter review, or conversely for more speed), and general organisational growth** all move over the same two years and would show up as an "AI adoption" association under Spearman correlation with a slowly, near-monotonically rising variable — this is the identical composition-drift risk already documented for GitClear's undisclosed sample-size jump, applied here to organisational growth instead of dataset composition. **The report does not report, control for, or discuss concurrent headcount or team-size change anywhere** — a first-order gap given that several headline metrics are explicitly "per developer" or "per PR" ratios.
  - A sharper, specific confound: **AI adoption plausibly correlates with organisational growth/scaling generally** (both are "push engineering capacity harder" decisions), so rising incident/bug/churn rates could partly reflect general scaling stress rather than AI-authored code specifically. The report's "maturity doesn't protect you" finding (quality degradation is present regardless of pre-AI DORA performance) is offered as a counter to one version of this concern, but engineering maturity and concurrent growth rate are different variables, so this does not fully address it.
  - **Denominator sensitivity:** several of the most extreme percentages (incidents/PR +242.7%, code churn +861%) are ratio metrics where a small low-adoption-quarter baseline value inflates the percentage change for a given absolute change. **The report gives no absolute baseline values for any headline metric** — only percentage deltas — which materially limits how the magnitudes should be read. This is a significant transparency gap, not merely a stylistic choice, since "how big is the number in absolute terms" is unanswerable from the document as published.
  - **Countervailing factors, recorded fairly:** the within-company design does avoid the cruder mistake of pooling raw cross-company levels; the ≥6-company/p<0.05 filter is a real (if minimal) statistical bar against single-anomalous-org noise; and the authors are unusually candid about at least one metric's ambiguity — the code-churn discussion (p.12) explicitly offers three competing explanations (rework of insufficient AI code; productive large-scale refactoring finally made tractable by AI; general acceleration of return-to-improve behaviour) and states "the metric as measured in cross-customer observational research cannot resolve the ambiguity" and "all three explanations are consistent with the data" — a genuinely good-faith caveat that is rare in vendor reports and is recorded as a point in the report's favour even though it does not rescue the underlying design.
  - **Verdict for use in synthesis:** treat this as a real, within-company telemetry association between AI-adoption level and outcome metrics, robust to cross-company baseline differences and single-org noise, but **not a natural experiment isolating AI's causal effect** — the magnitude of the headline percentages should be treated as substantially inflated by the organisational-growth/time-trend confound described above; the *direction* (more code, slower review, more incidents, more unreviewed merges, rising churn) is a different matter — see Cross-ref section for how it triangulates against independently-sourced evidence using different designs.
- **2025→2026 "prior report" comparisons are not a longitudinal panel — the report says so itself (p.3):** "This study and our 2025 report are independent cross-sections of the industry, not a longitudinal panel. The datasets differ in size, time window, and composition. Where we reference comparisons to the prior report, we do so to indicate directional consistency, not to assert precise year-over-year trends." This means every "up from X in prior report" figure below compares **two different (though presumably overlapping and Faros-customer-drawn) populations of different sizes** (10,000 devs/1,255 teams → 22,000 devs/4,000+ teams) — the same composition-drift risk already flagged for GitClear's year-on-year editions, here disclosed openly by the authors rather than discovered by the reviewer.

## Authors' own caveats (collected verbatim/near-verbatim from the 2026 PDF)

- Footnote 1 (p.2): "Organizations running on non-standard infrastructure were excluded" — criterion and population impact unspecified.
- "Note on continuity" (p.3): 2025 and 2026 are independent cross-sections, not a longitudinal panel; comparisons to the prior report indicate directional consistency only, not precise year-over-year trends.
- Deployment frequency and lead time are measured by only ~10% of the dataset; both carry explicit "treat directionally" / "recommend caution" hedges, strongest for lead time (+480.4%).
- Code churn: three competing explanations offered; explicitly stated as unresolvable from cross-customer observational metadata alone; readers are pointed to their own Git-level provenance data if they want to resolve it internally.
- Review-comment volume/length: confounded by PR size growth (mechanically expected) and by unseparated human/agent comment sources (agents "known to comment more verbosely and on a broader range of issues... which may be inflating both volume and length... independently of underlying code quality").
- Explicit *underestimate* framing (asymmetric — always in the direction of "the true numbers are worse than shown," never the reverse, outside the churn and lead-time hedges): "many organizations in this dataset use automated workflow nudges to surface stalled work. These findings reflect environments where teams are already intervening. Without that automation, the numbers are likely higher" (p.19).
- Adoption-mechanism caveat: licence/seat penetration was already flat; the observed adoption growth reflects a deliberate one-year utilisation-driving push by customer organisations, not new tool capability alone (p.7) — relevant if attributing effects to *model* improvement versus *rollout intensity*.
- Agentic-authorship scope limiter (already highlighted under Era above): <1% of PRs are agent-authored; the report explicitly states every metric would face "an order of magnitude greater" pressure if that changed (p.22).
- **Reviewer-identified gap (not author-acknowledged, kept distinct per the record convention already used for GitClear/Uplevel):** no discussion anywhere in the document of concurrent headcount, team-size, or organisational-growth change as an alternative explanation for the "per developer" and "per PR" metrics — the identical class of gap already flagged for GitClear's undisclosed sample-composition jump, here applied to organisational growth rather than dataset composition, and equally unaddressed.
- **Reviewer-identified gap:** no absolute baseline values are published for any headline percentage-change metric — only the percentage deltas — which limits independent assessment of practical magnitude (see Method section).

## What this can and cannot support

**Can support:** a large-N (22,000 developers, 4,000+ teams), within-company, telemetry-based, statistically-filtered (Spearman ρ, p<0.05, ≥6 companies) association between rising AI-tool adoption and simultaneous rises in PR size/complexity, review-queue time at every workflow stage, unreviewed-merge share, code churn, and production incidents/bugs — coincident with genuine throughput gains (epics/dev, task throughput, PR merge rate). Can support a directional, telemetry-based case for **bottleneck displacement** (§5 tension 6) — code generation accelerating faster than review/verification/deployment capacity — as a real pattern worth cross-checking against independently-designed evidence. Can support the narrower, well-caveated claim that this specific bottleneck-displacement pattern, in this dataset, occurs under human-in-the-loop AI-assisted authoring (agentic authorship <1% of PRs), not autonomous agentic authorship.

**Cannot support:** any causal attribution of the magnitude of these percentage changes to AI specifically, given the unaddressed organisational-growth/time-trend confound built into the lowest-vs-highest-adoption-quarter design (see Method verdict); any claim about autonomous agentic authorship (explicitly out of scope per the report's own <1% figure); any RQ4 cost claim (none quantified); any claim that "engineering maturity provides no protection" beyond this dataset's own DORA-score proxy (the report's rebuttal of DORA 2025's amplifier thesis rests on maturity, not on the growth-rate variable that the confound analysis above identifies as the more precise alternative explanation); and, as a single Tier D source, no exec-brief claim alone under PROTOCOL §3/§8 (Tier D never carries a claim alone; needs independent Tier B/C corroboration using different designs — see Cross-ref below for exactly which corroborating sources exist and their status).

## Cross-ref assessment (for the human's review — synthesis/contradictions.md was NOT edited)

**Overall: converges directionally with the bottleneck-displacement pattern, but each comparison needs its own caveat; nothing here is a clean replication because every candidate uses a different design and most are still queued, not yet extracted in this repository.**

1. **arXiv:2607.01904, "AI Writes Faster Than Humans Can Review" (Denisov-Blanch co-authored; 802 devs, 196k PRs, diff-in-diff; E3) — queued, not yet extracted.** Title and design (diff-in-diff on a large PR corpus, explicitly framed around review-capacity lagging authorship-capacity) are the closest conceptual match to Faros's central thesis and would be the single most valuable independent corroboration to obtain, because a diff-in-diff design on individual PRs is a materially stronger identification strategy than Faros's per-team lowest/highest-adoption-quarter comparison. **Recommend prioritising this source's extraction specifically to test the Faros review-bottleneck claim against a non-vendor, quasi-experimental design.**
2. **Agoda ("coding was never the bottleneck," via InfoQ) and Zendesk ("absorption capacity," via InfoQ) — both queued, not yet extracted, Tier C practitioner postmortems.** Directionally consistent at the level of the headline claim (review/downstream absorption, not code generation, is the constraint) — both are named in the Phase 0 batch-3 log as "the richest directive" for bottleneck displacement. Cannot yet confirm effect sizes or design quality; both are single-organisation case studies, so even once extracted they would only ever support triangulation, not carry a claim alone (§3).
3. **DX/Reock (Mar 2026), "gains are ~10%" — 400-company telemetry, AI usage +65% vs PR throughput +7.76% — queued, Tier D, COI.** This is the most directly comparable *design* to Faros (multi-company telemetry, adoption-vs-outcome association) and reaches a **much smaller** throughput effect size (+7.76%) than Faros's own throughput figures (task throughput +33.7%, epics/dev +66.2%). Both are Tier D vendor telemetry with COI in opposite commercial directions (DX's product is a productivity-measurement platform whose customers want reassurance gains exist; Faros's product benefits from alarm about downstream problems) — the throughput-magnitude discrepancy between the two is itself worth flagging as a **candidate contradiction once DX/Reock is formally extracted**: two vendor-telemetry sources measuring conceptually similar "adoption vs throughput" associations across large multi-company samples, in the same rough time window, disagree by roughly 4–8× on the size of the throughput gain. I have not written this to `synthesis/contradictions.md` per instruction, but flag it for the human's attention now, before DX/Reock's own extraction locks in its numbers.
4. **DORA 2025 (`dora-2025.md`, already extracted, Tier B) — Faros explicitly names this report and claims to directly counter it (p.5).** On close reading, the relationship is **more nuanced than "direct counterpoint"** as Faros frames it:
   - DORA 2025's own data already shows a **negative** signal on the same underlying construct Faros calls "quality" — the self-reported "software delivery instability" association with AI adoption **persisted** from 2024 into 2025 (DORA's own words, per `dora-2025.md`: "AI's relationship with... instability remains adverse"), even as DORA's throughput/other associations flipped positive. So Faros's telemetry and DORA's self-report **agree on direction** for the instability/quality-degradation axis — they are not opposed on this point.
   - Where they genuinely diverge is DORA's **moderation/protective-factor claim** — that strong engineering foundations blunt AI's downsides (the "amplifier" thesis) — versus Faros's telemetry finding that quality degradation is present **regardless of pre-AI DORA maturity**. This is a real, checkable disagreement, but it is a disagreement about a *moderator* (does maturity protect you), not about the *main effect* (is there a quality/instability cost) — Faros's own rhetorical framing ("a direct counterpoint") somewhat overstates the scope of the disagreement.
   - DORA 2025's own report is self-report/survey-based (per `dora-2025.md`'s verified extraction) — Faros's telemetry-vs-survey framing is accurate as a methodological point, but note DORA is *also* vendor-adjacent (Google Cloud, sponsored by GitHub/GitLab) — this is a COI-vs-COI comparison, not an independent-vs-vendor one, and should be presented that way in synthesis.
   - **DORA's own 2026 interim, the "ROI of AI-assisted Software Development" report (v2026.01, queued, not yet extracted)** introduces an "instability tax" concept that sounds directly complementary to Faros's incident/bug findings — recommend prioritising this extraction as the most direct DORA-side test of convergence, since it is DORA's own most recent and most quantitative treatment of the instability side of the story.
5. **DX/Noda companion ("why aren't gains higher"; coding ≈16% of dev time) — queued, Tier D.** If accurate, a ≈16% coding-time share would itself partially explain why organisation-level throughput gains (DX's own +7.76%, or the 2025 Faros predecessor's "no company-level correlation") lag individual-level task gains — a mechanism consistent with, but independent of, Faros's review-bottleneck mechanism.
