## What this document is
A measurement-instrument paper plus an exploratory re-analysis. It (1) defines RAMP, a four-level cumulative maturity model of AI configuration committed to version control; (2) validates a classifier on 441 private corporate repositories; (3) characterises adoption dynamics (Study 1); and (4) re-estimates the staggered-DiD agent-adoption panel of Agarwal, He and Vasilescu (MSR '26) within RAMP strata (Study 2). The headline claim is explicitly hypothesis-generating, not causal.

## Full notes
### RAMP instrument
- Levels (highest level with >=1 artifact): L1 Unconfigured (no AI files); L2 Grounded Prompting (rules/instructions files such as CLAUDE.md, copilot-instructions.md, .cursorrules; tool config; architecture docs; coding-standards docs); L3 Agent-Augmented (named agents with roles/tool limits, reusable commands/prompt templates, skills guides); L4 Orchestration (multi-agent flows, pipelines, session logs).
- Detection: 43 file patterns over 12 tools (Claude Code .claude/, Cursor .cursor/, Copilot .github/, Windsurf, Aider, AMP, Gemini CLI, Google IDX, JetBrains AI, Kiro, OpenAI Codex, OpenHands); cross-tool AGENTS.md and *mcp*.json (root AGENTS.md labelled tool=shared); then non-standard markdown swept for discovery statistics only. Classification into nine categories by priority ensemble: tool-pattern category, then path-token and content embeddings (nomic-embed-text-v1.5, cosine similarity to 40-120-word templates). Content alone matches the full pipeline for 42.4% of repos. Presence only; content quality, specificity and enforcement are not scored. Instructions files normalised to 'rules'.
- Sample: 1,931 repos / 30,216 candidate artifacts in a discovery sweep of 27 volunteering organisations' private GitHub repos; filters (single-artifact repos, boilerplate, top-10% artifact-count outliers, near-duplicates) gave 441 repos (~4,300 candidate files); 217 have >=1 validated AI artifact (1,046 artifacts). Levels: L1 294 (66.7%; includes 63 with only non-levelled documentation), L2 109 (24.7%), L3 38 (8.6%), L4 0. Mean validated artifacts 0.5 / 2.7 / 15.8.
- Cumulativity: Guttman CR 0.997, CS 0.983 (thresholds 0.90/0.60); mean Loevinger H 0.299 (authors: weak at item-pair level); permutation of category-to-level mapping p=0.0074 (CR), 0.0051 (CS); Guttman covers only L2-to-L3 because no L4 in the dev sample. Authors concede some cumulative structure is imposed by construction.
- Human validation (Study 2 corpus, held out): 3 annotators, 195 artifacts, 35 repos, level-stratified, blinded. Krippendorff alpha 0.572. File-level accuracy 81.7% (Wilson 95% CI 75.3-86.7; kappa 0.743; cluster-bootstrap 63.3-95.6); 93.6% on majority-vote items (n=47). An LLM labeller (Claude Haiku) scored 81.1% (McNemar p=1.0). Repo-level: 34/35 exact match (97.1%; weighted kappa 0.829; Wilson 95% CI 85-99). The '97% annotator reproduction' is this 34/35 figure on a level-balanced sample (stratum-weighted, not a population rate); disagreements concentrate in skills and flows.
- Tool bias: maturity distributions differ by tool (chi-squared 111.3, Cramer's V 0.483); Copilot repos concentrate at L1, Cursor at L2-L3, partly a measurement artefact of file conventions.

### Study 1 adoption dynamics (196-repo temporal subset, git events Jan 2025 to Feb 2026)
- 73.8% of artifacts committed once and never modified. 95.9% of adopters enter at L2 (rest directly L3). 83.1% introduce artifacts at exactly one level; 14.9% progress L2 to L3; 2.1% inverted. Reversals 0%, abandonment 0.5%, plateau 12.8% of non-transitioning repos. Median time to first artifact >=441 days (633 days among the 87% with pre-adoption history); L2 to L3 median 154 days (n=18). Kaplan-Meier: L1-to-L2 404 at risk / 191 events; L2-to-L3 191 / 18; L3-to-L4 37 / 0. Lower-before-higher ordering ~4.5:1 (rho 0.866, one-sided permutation p=0.029); about a third of multi-level repos add both levels the same day or commit. Maintenance effort disappears once artifact count is controlled (partial rho -0.072, p=0.32). L3 repos average 3.4 contributors to AI artifacts vs 1.0-1.5 at L1-L2.
- Interpretation by authors: 'AI artifacts are configured once and left in place', so an end-of-window snapshot is a proxy for stable practice. Note this is a Study 1 (corporate) finding used to justify snapshot labelling in Study 2 (open source).

### Study 2 outcome design
- Source panel: Agarwal, He and Vasilescu (2026), 'AI IDEs or Autonomous Agents? Measuring the Impact of Coding Agents on Software Development', MSR '26 pp. 857-862. Staggered DiD with PS-matched controls; unit repository-month; treatment = first agent-attributed PR (branch prefixes such as cursor/ and claude/, author logins, commit metadata, PR text). Original sample: 401 agent-first treated matched to 606 controls; 117 IDE-first matched to 73 controls. Authors Agarwal, He, Vasilescu are co-authors here; the panel is public and re-used 'without modification' (Borusyak et al. imputation estimator, SEs clustered by repo).
- Treated repos: 518; 9 untraceable; 509 classified: L1 236, L2 210, L3 23, L4 40 (agent-first 214/155/5/22 = 396; IDE-first 22/55/18/18 = 113). Maturity read from a Nov 2025 snapshot, after the matching step; strata share the control pool. Primary contrast L1 vs L2+ within the agent-first stratum (214 vs 182) because IDE-first L1 has only 22 repos.
- Outcomes (all log-transformed): velocity = monthly commits and lines added; quality = SonarQube cognitive complexity, static-analysis warnings, duplicated line density, comment line density. Window: panel to end-Nov 2025; event study -6 to +6 months.
- 'Agent-first' = agents were the repo's first AI tool (no prior IDE-based tool); 'IDE-first' = IDE tool preceded the agent. This is Agarwal et al.'s partition, not authors' own.

- The abstract's '28-38% more commits' is the rounded 27.5% / 37.6%. No CIs reported; no test of the between-stratum difference is reported (only per-stratum significance). Betas and SEs are for log outcomes; percentage = exp(beta)-1.
- Dose-response (pooled agent+IDE sample, four levels): complexity L1 +54.4%, L2 +39.8% (both p<0.001), L3-L4 indistinguishable from zero; warnings L1 +23.0%, L2 +16.7%, non-significant above; duplication significant only at L1 among L1-L3, but L4 +31.4% (p<0.01), the only significant L4 quality effect, read by authors as heavy agent use. L3 (23) and L4 (40) strata are small. Combined L1-2: complexity +46.6%, warnings +20.9% (both p<0.001). Pooled L1 vs L2+: complexity +54.4% vs +35.9% (1.5x), warnings +23.0% vs +20.0% (gradient nearly erased by pooling). Pooled duplication: L2+ +5.9% (n.s.) vs L1 +16.0%.
- Temporal: L1 quality paths rise through +6 months with little reversion; L2+ show an initial bump then moderation; gap widens. Velocity paths aligned across strata. Warnings: L1 shows elevated pre-treatment coefficients, so parallel trends is doubtful for that contrast (authors say 'more weakly identified').
- Benchmarks: He et al. 2026 (Cursor, earlier period) +30% warnings, +41% complexity; Agarwal et al. pooled +39% complexity, ~+18% warnings. Authors note L2+ falls below both baselines, but the comparison mixes tool, period and model generation.

### Authors' confounder and framing statements (verbatim)
- Abstract: 'Because maturity is observational, correlated engineering discipline or model capability may explain part of the gap; we present these findings as hypothesis-generating and release RAMP as a reusable instrument.'
- 7.2: 'Maturity is not randomly assigned. Level 2+ repositories may differ from Level 1 on (1) engineering maturity, (2) code hygiene, (3) AI usage intensity, (4) domain complexity, and (5) model capability.' DiD and matching partly address (1), (2), (4); 'AI usage intensity and model capability are unobserved and cannot be adjusted.'
- 5.2: 'Any difference reflects heterogeneity in the treatment effect of adoption, not a causal effect of maturity itself.'
- 5.3.1: whether lower L2+ effects 'partly reflect model-capability gains rather than practice is an alternative our design cannot rule out'.
- Three non-exclusive channels (guardrail; marker of engineering discipline; faster organisational learning); 'Our design cannot distinguish among them.'
- Timing: only 11.0% of 273 L2+ treated repos (3.8% in agent-first) committed their first validated artifact before the adoption month. The authors argue reverse causality (config added in response to quality problems) biases the gap downward; the same fact also means L2+ mostly reflects config adopted alongside or after the agent, so it is not a pre-adoption practice.
- Pre-adoption differences (Table 4): L2+ median stars 174 vs 55, forks 38 vs 10, PRs 389 vs 87 (more established projects).
- Practical advice (6.2): commit at least a rules file and a coding-standards document; 'roughly half the quality degradation' for L2 vs none. Caveats: 'we measure the presence of committed artifacts, not the quality of their content or enforcement.'

### Cost, review and defect outcomes
None. Outcomes are four SonarQube static metrics plus commits and lines added. No defects, incidents, review time, merge/rework, delivery stability, or token/licence cost. Authors call SonarQube outcomes 'proxies rather than direct measures of defects or maintainability'.

## Tier & era grading
- Tier: B (structured observational; staggered DiD with matched controls on a large OSS panel, stratified post hoc by an observational moderator). The core exposure contrast (committed config) is not randomised and not pre-registered; the authors self-describe it as hypothesis-generating. Not Tier A. Instrument component is a measurement-validity contribution.
- Era: E3. The treatment is autonomous coding-agent adoption (first agent-attributed PR via cursor/, claude/ branch prefixes and similar). Config files imply Claude Code (CLAUDE.md, .claude/), Cursor (.cursorrules, .cursor/), Copilot (.github/copilot-instructions.md), Windsurf, Aider, Gemini CLI, Codex, Kiro, OpenHands, AMP, plus cross-tool AGENTS.md. Caveat: configuration for IDE tools (Copilot, Cursor rules) counts as L2 maturity; the paper does not separate which agent produced the post-adoption effects. Panel ends Nov 2025.
- Independence: NOT an independent voice. (a) Data reuse: Study 2 outcomes are the Agarwal et al. MSR '26 panel, estimated with their estimator unchanged; Agarwal, He and Vasilescu (CMU) are co-authors, and Agarwal is joint first author and arXiv submitter. (b) He et al. 2026 (Cursor longitudinal, +30% warnings / +41% complexity) is the paper's benchmark and shares He/Vasilescu. (c) Denisov-Blanch is the Stanford productivity-group lead (Schaeffer, Miranda, Koyejo are Stanford); this overlaps with the Stanford cross-company telemetry work named in PROTOCOL §6, so it is one provenance cluster with that Stanford work and with the CMU agent-quality line (He/Agarwal/Vasilescu), and should not be counted as independent confirmation of either. (d) Grid Dynamics (an engineering consultancy, Azaletskiy) is a co-author affiliation; thanks also go to P10Y and Grid Dynamics staff; this is a mild COI-adjacent flag (consultancy selling AI-adoption maturity thinking), not Tier D. (e) Funding: no statement in the paper; corporate Study 1 data supplied by 27 volunteering organisations under research agreements.
- COI flag: moderate. The paper proposes a maturity framework and a reusable classifier that practitioners and consultancies could sell; the SEI and consultancies are cited as having proposed maturity models. No vendor model is promoted.

## Measured vs self-reported
All outcomes are measured from repository artefacts and static analysis (SonarQube); no self-report, no survey. The maturity label is also artefact-derived (presence of committed files), which is objective but is a proxy for practice: unseen governance (wikis, review checklists, PR gates, policies) is scored Level 1. Human validation is the only judgement component (moderate inter-annotator agreement, alpha 0.572). No measured-vs-perceived delta can be derived.

## Can / cannot support — verdicts
- Can support (medium confidence, E3): in OSS agent-first repos, agent adoption raised commit volume in both configured and unconfigured repos (+27.5% to +37.6%), while static complexity and warnings rose more where no AI configuration was committed (complexity +52.7% vs +26.7%; warnings +24.1% vs +14.0%). Supports the point that average effects hide heterogeneity, and that repos adding configuration alongside agents show smaller static-metric deterioration. Can support RQ6: stratify by AI practice maturity rather than adopted/not; presence of AGENTS.md/CLAUDE.md-class files is a cheap observable. Can support a descriptive claim that committed AI configuration is typically written once and not revised (corporate sample, 14 months).
- Cannot support: a causal claim that committing a rules file reduces quality cost (authors themselves refuse this); any defect, stability, review, or cost claim (RQ4, RQ5b); any claim about mature enterprise brownfield codebases (OSS sample; no complexity or maturity-of-codebase split); any L3/L4 benefit claim (23 and 40 repos, null at L3-L4 may be underpowered); any claim independent of Agarwal et al. 2026 or He et al. 2026. 'Quality cost' means SonarQube proxies only.

## Red flags / things the orchestrator should know
1. Observational maturity: not randomised; L2+ repos were pre-adoption larger, more starred, more active (median PRs 389 vs 87). Engineering discipline and model capability are named, unadjusted confounders. Confidence is low-to-medium for any 'config reduces quality cost' reading.
2. Self-selection and timing: maturity is read from a Nov 2025 snapshot; only 3.8% of agent-first L2+ repos had config before the adoption month. Config may be a consequence of, or concurrent with, adoption, so it is not a pre-adoption exposure. Authors argue the bias is downward; the direction is asserted, not tested.
3. Static-metric proxies, not defects: SonarQube complexity/warnings/duplication; no defect, incident, review or delivery data. Do not enter these as quality-in-production evidence.
4. Dataset reuse and provenance cluster: same panel and estimator as Agarwal et al. MSR '26; overlapping CMU authors with He et al. 2026; Stanford group overlaps with other Stanford telemetry. Count as one provenance cluster with the Agarwal and He extractions, not as triangulation.
5. Warnings contrast has elevated L1 pre-trends (authors' admission); the stratum difference is not formally tested; no CIs are given. Betas 0.216 vs 0.131 with SEs 0.077 / 0.066 are unlikely to differ significantly on their own; complexity (0.423 vs 0.236, SEs 0.118 / 0.077) is also not formally contrasted.
6. Strata share one control pool, partitioned after matching; within-stratum covariate balance not guaranteed. Duplicated-line contrast vanishes within agent-first (+15.4% vs +14.2%); only the pooled sample shows it. Lines added shows the opposite sign (higher with config).
7. Two samples are conflated: instrument and adoption dynamics (corporate, private, unreplicable) versus outcomes (OSS). The 73.8% set-and-forget figure comes from the corporate sample and is applied as justification for the OSS snapshot.
8. 'Agent-first' vs 'IDE-first': IDE-first L1 has only 22 repos so the maturity contrast is identified essentially within agent-first (214 vs 182). 91 of 113 IDE-first repos are L2+, so pooling confounds exposure with maturity.
9. Version drift: v1 to v2 changes are undocumented (equal 350 KB size). The ASE '26 stamp indicates acceptance; re-check at the camera-ready if cited.
10. The Level 4 duplicated-line result (+31.4%, p<0.01) and null L3-L4 complexity may reflect dose (heaviest users) and small N, respectively.
