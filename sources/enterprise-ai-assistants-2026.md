## What this document is
A single-firm, one-shot, self-report online survey (25 questions, opened May 2025, n=57 responses) of staff at the authors' own employer, plus a secondary literature review of 35 published developer surveys (2023 x2, 2024 x14, 2025 x19; screened by Gemini 2.5 Pro and Claude Sonnet 4 extraction prompts, then "manually verified"). No telemetry, no code outcomes, no time measurements, no interviews. The paper itself calls the findings "perceived". It is a requirements-elicitation and perception study, not an effects study, despite "Effects" in the title.

## Full notes
**1. Setting and design**
- Population: 57 respondents from one tech company. Divisions: Finance and Operations 30%, Software 25%, Research 25%, others (Consulting, Global Sales named in figures). Experience: 42% >20 years; 26% <5 years. Many respondents are not software-development staff (finance/ops, sales, research), so this is a mixed "people who write code" population, not a product-engineering population.
- Response rate / sampling frame: not reported. Self-selected online form. Selection towards enthusiasts is plausible and unquantified.
- Tools: questionnaire asked which assistants respondents had used (options: watsonx Coding Assistant, GitHub Copilot, Cursor, Windsurf, Cody, ChatGPT web chat, Gemini web chat, Amazon Q, AugmentCode, other; up to three). The distribution is only in Figure 5, which is not extractable as text; no counts are given in prose. Preference ranking offered IDE-based / editor-only / command line / web chat. Modality split of actual use is not reported in text. The paper says explicitly that agentic assistants are NOT analysed ("our future work will tackle the study ... of the agentic AI coding solutions"; "notable absence of analysis targeted to agentic workflows"). Era: E1/E2 (IDE assistant + chat), mid-2025 snapshot; no E3 findings. Some respondents may have used Cursor/Windsurf in agent mode, but the paper does not separate this.
- Period: data collection from May 2025.

**2. Usage findings**
- Adoption: not a rate. The instrument asks "Have you used an AI-based coding assistant in the past?" and the analysed set is users; the paper does not report non-users. Adoption share therefore cannot be derived.
- Frequency: "Many users reporting productivity gains used the AI coding assistant daily or weekly". Options Daily / Weekly / Occasionally; no counts given in text.
- Ease of adoption: 39% "somewhat easy", 47% "extremely easy" (86% easy).
- Tasks: of 35 respondents answering the role question, 47% (c.16-17) said their focus was code generation (the paper folds in "guidance for coding, code reviews"), followed by code understanding and testing. Motivations (free text coded into six themes): productivity/efficiency dominant in every division; Research almost solely productivity; Consulting also understanding code; Software spread across themes; Global Sales weights code quality equal to productivity. Benefits narrated: learning packages/docs, documentation, prototyping and scaffolding, code drafts, tests, debugging. Mapping to taxonomy: T3 (prototyping, boilerplate), T10 (documentation, code understanding, learning), T6 (testing), T8 (debugging), T5 (refactoring, mentioned), T7 (code review, folded into generation count). No T4 (brownfield) data; no T1/T2/T11 data.
- Replaced tools: over 65% previously relied on Google Search, then Stack Overflow, then non-AI IDE completion.
- Code retention (self-reported): "The majority of users (48) kept the code generated"; "a large portion retained between 25-50%". Question was bucketed 0/25/50/75/100. Denominator for 48 not stated (of 57?). Not a measured acceptance rate.
- Features ranked: everyone values correctness first; security most valuable for Sales; customisation to specific codebases/domains most valued in Research (quantum cited).
- Seniority / role splits: the paper reports splits by division (motivation, feature ranking) but NOT by seniority or experience band, despite collecting experience. Its conclusion that gains are "irrespective of programming skill level" is asserted without a reported cross-tab.

**3. Effects (all self-reported)**
- Perceived productivity gain: "88% of them perceived productivity gains"; "44 out of 57 users (97.8% of the users who answered on the perceived gain) reported a gain of at least 25%"; 26 users at least 50%; "18% estimate up to 100%". Instrument: Y/N then a bucket (0/25/50/75/100%) "compared to the existing tools" (i.e. against Google/Stack Overflow/non-AI completion, not against a no-AI counterfactual on the same task). The smallest non-zero option is 25%, so "at least 25%" is the floor of the lowest non-zero bucket, not evidence of a 25% effect.
- Internal inconsistencies in the reported numbers: 44/57 = 77%, but the text says 97.8% of respondents to the question (implies 45 answered); "88% perceived gains" (c.50 of 57) conflicts with "13 of 57 (22%) little or no gain" (50+13=63>57); the productivity figure is therefore not cleanly reconstructable. Treat the headline as roughly 78-88% of respondents reporting some gain, with an unresolved denominator.
- Perceived process change: 42 of 57 (73.7%) said AI assistants "transformed" their development process (faster feature delivery, more time on high-level design, less repetitive work, easier to try new technologies). Respondents also claim improved quality and standards compliance; no measurement.
- No-gain group: 13 of 57 (22%) reported little or no productivity gain. Reasons: limited on custom/complex logic (boilerplate only); correctness/completeness problems and not following codebase conventions; prompting effort not less than writing the code; some reported it made debugging harder; unsupported languages or non-integrated IDEs. This is a recorded negative/null group, self-reported.
- Measured outcomes: NONE. No telemetry, task times, defect rates, PR throughput or code-quality data. Perceived-vs-measured delta: cannot be computed. The authors cite Becker et al. 2025 (METR) in related work only, and do not reconcile their own 77-88% gain figures with it. Their conclusion ("reaffirmed that AI coding assistants are helping drive the productivity") over-reads a perception survey.

**4. Requirements (open-text, coded; counts mostly NOT reported)**
Figure 9 holds the counts and is not available as text; the prose gives rank order only.
- Short term: (1) more automation and agents (Git/PR/merge, CI/CD, issue management, proactive bug detection and fixes; interactive pair-programming) - most frequent; (2) deeper context: full-repository awareness without RAG set-up or degradation at token limits; (3) seamless IDE integration with in-line edits; (4) broader, up-to-date knowledge (less popular languages named: Perl, Shell, Apex, OPL; current SDK/library versions, incompatibility monitoring); (5) quality and reliability, including stating confidence ("confidently incorrect" is the complaint); (6) personalisation, model choice (local vs remote), learning own coding style; also API/UI test generation and multimodal/voice.
- Long term: assistant as architect (multi-file design, project from specification), agent (end-to-end lifecycle to PRs and deployment monitoring), expert (full repository understanding; security, compliance and governance awareness across regions).
- Customisation: asked how important behaviour customisation is, 50% "somewhat important", 30% "extremely important".
- Cross-check: "full repository awareness" recurs in both short and long term lists, which the authors read as high importance. Governance/security demand is strongest in Sales (division) per the ranking.

**5. Cost or licence data**: none. No licence, token or review-cost figures. Not RQ4 evidence.

**6. Literature review component (35 surveys)**
LLM-assisted extraction (Gemini 2.5 Pro, Claude Sonnet 4) with manual verification; tables are in a supplement (appendix tables reproduced as extraction prompts and per-paper summaries). Findings: perceived productivity is the number one perceived benefit across surveys; code generation, unit test generation, debugging, code explanation dominate tasks studied; ChatGPT and Copilot dominate tools; quality assurance covered in only 4 of 35; participant counts range 10 to 17,420; six student surveys and two researcher surveys; only one study surveyed expectations rather than experience; no analysis of agentic workflows; no comparison across divisions within a company. This is a secondary summary of self-report surveys; no new measured evidence, and it inherits their era (E1/E2). Selection was by web search plus forward citation, not systematic.

**7. Limitations (authors and ours)**
Authors state gaps in the literature, not limitations of their own sample. Ours: n=57; single firm and the authors' own employer; unknown response rate and selection; mixed non-SWE roles; coarse bucketed answers; comparison baseline is web search, not no-AI; internally inconsistent percentages; no seniority analysis; tool/modality mix not reported in text; free-text coding method (who coded, agreement) not described; figures carry the counts.

## Tier & era grading
- Tier: D-adjacent but not vendor marketing. Strictly it is a small, self-selected, single-firm perception survey by the employer's research arm, whose firm sells an AI coding assistant (watsonx Code Assistant, an option in the survey). Under PROTOCOL §3 it does not meet Tier A/B (no telemetry, no counterfactual, no structured longitudinal data). Grade: **Tier C- (practitioner signal, perception only)**, with a COI flag that would tip to D if the firm's assistant were prominent in the results (not determinable from text). Not usable as Tier C triangulation for effect-size claims; usable as one directional self-report data point.
- Independence / COI: authors are employees of the surveyed firm; the firm has a commercial coding assistant. No statement on funding or disclosure. Requirements section is plausibly shaped by the vendor's product roadmap interests ("tailor modes/models", customised CodeLLMs are the stated takeaway). Population selection by authors, not independent.
- Era: all primary findings **E1/E2** (IDE assistants and web chat, May 2025 survey), self-reported; no agentic modality studied by the authors' own statement. Requirements wish-lists for agents/autonomy/full-repo context are **expectations, not experience**, tag as "expectation (pre-E3 users asking for E3)". Literature-review component: E1/E2 (2023-2025 surveys, ChatGPT/Copilot).
- Recency: published 2026 but data from May 2025; no supersession search needed beyond the version check (done: single version).

## Measured vs self-reported
- Measured: nothing.
- Self-reported: 88% perceived gain; 44/57 at least 25% (bucketed); 26 at least 50%; 73.7% process transformed; 22% no gain; ease of adoption 86%; code retention 48 "kept" code, modal 25-50% retained.
- Delta: none computable. The paper provides no measured counterpart; per CLAUDE.md rule 4 do not present these as effects. Under tension 1 (perception-reality gap), this source is an instance of the type of evidence the gap discredits, not a test of it.

## Can / cannot support — verdicts
- CAN support: (a) a directional data point that a self-selected mixed-role enterprise population reports high perceived gain, with a minority (22%) reporting none and giving task-complexity, correctness, and prompting-overhead reasons (mechanism hypotheses for RQ3, consistent with brownfield/complex-logic failure but not testing it); (b) requirements signal: full-repository context, security/compliance/governance, calibrated confidence, IDE/workflow integration, and git/CI/PR automation top the wish list; (c) the observation that motivation, valued features and use differ across divisions (no one-size-fits-all), a methodological point for internal measurement (segment by role, not just "developer"); (d) review-of-reviews: dominant survey topics are generation, tests, debugging, explanation; QA is rarely surveyed (4 of 35); no survey in their set examined agentic workflows as of 2025.
- CANNOT support: any productivity, speed or quality effect size; adoption rates; seniority asymmetry (not analysed); brownfield vs greenfield; cost; any E3 claim; any exec-brief effect-size claim. Cannot be cited as a "reaffirmation" of productivity gains.

## Red flags / things the orchestrator should know
1. Title says "Effects" but there are no measured effects; every effect number is a perception, bucketed at 25% steps against a web-search baseline.
2. Percentages are internally inconsistent (97.8% vs 44/57; 88% vs 13/57 no-gain). Quote with the caveat or do not quote.
3. Venue is the LLM4Code workshop at ICSE 2026, not the main track or SEIP; the metadata DOI/ACM listing may mislead on venue.
4. The firm is not named in the body; IBM is inferred from affiliations and division names. The authors' employer sells a competing coding assistant (watsonx).
5. Counts for tool mix, frequency, requirements ranking are in figures only; the text-level extraction cannot give them. If these matter, a human or an image-capable pass over Figures 4, 5, 6, 7, 9 is needed.
6. Literature-review half uses LLM extraction (Gemini 2.5 Pro, Claude Sonnet 4) with unspecified manual verification; treat its tallies as unaudited.
7. Many respondents are not software engineers (finance/ops 30%, sales, research); do not generalise to engineering orgs.
8. No seniority cut despite 42% >20 years and 26% <5 years; the "irrespective of skill level" conclusion is unsupported in the text.
9. Likely low value for the programme: perception-only, E1/E2, single firm. Suggest relevance 2 and non-saturating for RQ1/RQ2 except as a self-report example; modest value for RQ6 (segment by division) and RQ3 (no-gain reasons).
