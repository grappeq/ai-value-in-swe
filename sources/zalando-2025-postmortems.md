## What the system does

- **Corpus:** "thousands of archived postmortem documents" accumulated over multiple years at Zalando; the analysis effort itself spans **two years** (per the title) with some historical framing reaching back **five years**.
- **Scope:** five datastore technologies — **Postgres, AWS DynamoDB, AWS S3, AWS ElastiCache, and Elasticsearch**. This is an infra/datastore-incident corpus specifically, not all incident types at Zalando.
- **Models used, in evolution order:**
  1. Open-source models, 3B–12B parameters, run locally via LM Studio (early prototyping).
  2. Google NotebookLM (intermediate step) — discarded.
  3. A multi-model "map-fold" pipeline using 3B/12B/27B-parameter models.
  4. Current production model: **Claude Sonnet 4 on AWS Bedrock**.
- **Pipeline architecture (four stages, "map-fold" pattern — independent per-document processing, then aggregation):**
  1. **Summarisation** — condenses each postmortem into a 5-dimension structured summary (Issue, Root Causes, Impact, Resolution, Preventive Actions), using TELeR-style prompting.
  2. **Classification** — determines whether a specific datastore technology directly caused the incident, or "None."
  3. **Analyzer** — produces a 3–5 sentence digest of the technology's role and contributing factors for incidents classified as attributable.
  4. **Patterns** — synthesises digests across incidents into a one-page report of systemic/cross-incident failure themes for leadership.
- **Design principle stated explicitly:** a multi-stage pipeline of narrow-purpose LLM calls outperformed a single high-end model with a large context window, because it avoided the "lost in the middle" effect on long documents.

## Quantified findings (measured vs impressionistic — marked explicitly)

**Throughput/speed (measured, operational figures):**
- Manual baseline: ~15–20 minutes per postmortem, ≈4 postmortems/hour for a focused human reviewer.
- Early NotebookLM step: "boosted productivity three times" over manual reading (impressionistic multiplier, not a controlled comparison — no methodology given for how the 3x was derived).
- Required engineering constraint the team set for itself: total pipeline time per document must not exceed 120 seconds.
- Multi-model open-source stage: ~20 seconds/document to classify, ~60 seconds/incident to analyse (27B-class models needed 90–120 seconds before the multi-stage split).
- Current production (Claude Sonnet 4): ~30 seconds per postmortem; full annual corpus processed in under 24 hours.
- These are **operational/engineering timing figures reported by the author**, not independently audited — treat as measured-but-self-reported (Zalando's own instrumentation of its own pipeline, plausible and specific, but no external audit).

**Accuracy / error rates:**
- **Hallucination, small models (3B–12B):** "up to 40% probability for hallucination" at the summary/analysis phases early on. Mitigated via prompt engineering and "human-led curation until the effect of hallucination became less than 15%." With larger/production models, hallucination is described as having become "negligible" — but no percentage is given for the current-model hallucination rate, so this specific claim is **impressionistic, not quantified**.
- **Surface Attribution Error — the headline finding — ~10%, persistent:** "we still observe approximately 10% attribution, even with advanced models such as Claude Sonnet 4." The failure mode: the model produces "a well-structured and authoritative explanation regarding the contribution of AWS S3 to an incident, even if 'S3' is merely mentioned without being causally linked" — i.e., it anchors on a prominent keyword mentioned in the incident thread and constructs a plausible-sounding causal narrative around it, rather than reasoning through whether that component was actually causal. Negative prompting was applied as a mitigation but did not eliminate the error; it persists at ~10% "even with advanced models."
  - **Measurement basis: explicitly NOT specified in the source. This must be flagged, not glossed over.** A second, targeted fetch pass specifically searching for a sample size, audit method, or validation procedure behind the ~10% figure found none — the article states the rate as an observed outcome but never describes how it was audited (no stated sample size of postmortems checked, no stated inter-rater/human-adjudication process specific to this number, no confidence interval). The closest description of *any* validation process in the piece is generic: colleagues "upvoted or downvoted" pipeline outputs during development, and later, quality control was relaxed from "100% human curation of output batches" to "10–20% of randomly sampled summaries from each output batch." It is plausible the ~10% attribution-error figure emerged from this general sampling/curation process, but the author does not say so explicitly, so this connection is an inference, not a stated fact.
  - **Grade this figure as self-reported/impressionistic-but-specific**, not as a rigorously measured, audited error rate. It is a named practitioner's considered estimate from sustained hands-on exposure to the pipeline's output over two years, which has real value as a directional/mechanism finding, but it does not meet the bar of "measured" per PROTOCOL §4 (no reported n, no reported audit protocol, no CI).
- **Numerical data extraction (GMV/EBIT loss, customers affected, repair time): explicitly failed.** "Reliable accuracy in extracting numerical data... from postmortems was not achieved." The team worked around this by pulling those figures from a separate, already-trustworthy internal incident dataset rather than trying to have the LLM extract them from free text. This is a clean, explicitly stated **negative result** — recorded with the same weight as the positive findings, per CLAUDE.md evidence rule 3.
- **Domain/technology overfitting:** general-purpose LLMs gave "unacceptable analysis of incidents concerning Zalando internal technologies (e.g., Skipper)" — a proprietary/internal technology absent from public training data. Stated as requiring model fine-tuning to fix; not yet resolved as described. Another explicit negative result.
- **Specific ROI figure (positive, and the most concrete outcome number in the piece):** the AWS S3 automated change-validation solution that emerged from the pipeline's pattern-finding "was able to shield us from 25% subsequent datastore incidents." This is presented as a downstream engineering outcome of acting on the pipeline's findings, not a property of the LLM analysis itself — worth keeping separate in the evidence table as an intervention-outcome figure rather than an AI-accuracy figure. No further methodological detail (baseline period, counting method) is given for how the 25% was computed.

## What worked ("data goldmines") — positive findings

1. Scaled cross-incident pattern-finding that would be practically impossible manually: reduced multi-year, thousands-of-document analysis "from days to hours," enabling questions ("what recurring patterns exist across five years of Postgres incidents?") that were previously answerable only by weeks of dedicated-team effort (NotebookLM baseline) or not answerable at all in practice.
2. Surfaced genuinely novel "hidden hotspots" — e.g., improper connection-pool configuration and circuit-breaker misconfiguration causing cascading failures in components "previously considered stable."
3. A stated top-six list of recurring, cross-incident systemic patterns: absence of automated change validation for config/IaC; ad-hoc change-management practice; absence of progressive delivery (canary/blue-green) for datastores; underestimated traffic patterns; failure to scale ahead of demand; and resource (memory/CPU/IOPS) bottlenecks.
4. Technology-specific insights: AWS S3 incidents consistently traced to deployment-artefact misconfiguration (→ led to the automated-validation fix with the 25% figure above); AWS ElastiCache incidents linked to ~80% CPU utilisation at peak traffic informing capacity planning.
5. A meta-finding with genuine strategic value: "our datastore portfolio is mature and resilient, with incidents very rarely directly attributed to technological flaws" — i.e., the analysis's main output for leadership was reassurance that the *process* (change management, capacity planning, progressive delivery), not the *technology choice*, is where investment should go. This is the kind of decision-relevant signal PROTOCOL's evidence bar is designed to capture.

## What didn't work ("dead ends") — negative findings, recorded with equal diligence

1. Small open-source models (3B–12B) hallucinated fabricated incidents outright — the example given: inventing a plausible-sounding but non-existent DynamoDB incident narrative purely because "DynamoDB" appeared in a playbook title.
2. Google NotebookLM: threw away context and produced severe hallucinations at scale; rejected despite an initial 3x productivity boost, precisely because the errors it introduced outweighed the speed gain.
3. Surface Attribution Error (detailed above) — persistent at ~10%, not eliminated by negative prompting, present even in the current production model.
4. Numerical financial/impact-metric extraction from free text — abandoned as unreliable; worked around rather than solved.
5. Fine-tuning gap for proprietary/internal technologies — general models give unacceptably poor analysis of Zalando-internal systems (e.g. Skipper); unresolved as described, flagged as needing fine-tuning.
6. "Lost in the middle" effect in long-context single-pass attempts — motivated the move away from single high-end-model-with-huge-context approaches toward the multi-stage pipeline.
7. A no-code agentic solution was tried and explicitly abandoned as infeasible ("performance limitations, inaccuracies, and hallucinations") — the clearest E3-relevant negative data point in the piece (see Era section above).

## Workflow integration: assists, does not replace

Explicitly and repeatedly framed as **augmentation, not replacement** of human root-cause analysis:
- Every pipeline stage produces a human-readable intermediate artefact (summary, digest, pattern report) that is reviewed, not accepted blind.
- Quality control evolved from 100% human curation of every output batch down to 10–20% random sampling per batch as trust grew — still a standing human check, never zero.
- The final one-page cross-incident report explicitly relies on "human editorial work... to address any novel failure modes that AI may have overlooked."
- Author's own summary framing: human curation, examination, labelling, and QC "at each stage... are essential for refining prompts, ensuring accuracy, fostering trust, and addressing novel failure modes" — stated as a general lesson, not a hedge.

## Lessons stated explicitly by the author

1. LLMs can turn a large corpus of human-authored postmortems into a decision-relevant dataset, surfacing cross-incident patterns "impossible to identify manually at scale."
2. A multi-stage pipeline of narrow single-objective LLM calls was **more effective and reliable** than one large-context single-shot model — directly mitigates "lost in the middle" and improves accuracy.
3. Human-in-the-loop curation at every stage is "essential," not optional, for accuracy, trust, and catching novel failure modes the model missed.
4. Practical recommendations given: start small and iterate; invest heavily in prompt engineering specifically to reduce hallucination *and* surface-attribution error; design intermediate outputs to be human-interpretable to sustain trust.

**Can support:** a genuine, named-practitioner, non-vendor, two-year field account of an LLM RCA/postmortem-analysis pipeline in production, including an explicit, persistent, unresolved negative finding (surface attribution error, ~10%) reported with the same candour as the positive results — exactly the "postmortems of AI rollouts... reporting nulls with the same diligence" material PROTOCOL treats as scarce and valuable. ## Cross-ref assessment (report only — synthesis/contradictions.md NOT edited)

**1. vs. arXiv 2601.22208, "Stalled, Biased, and Confused: Uncovering Reasoning Failures in LLMs for Cloud-Based Root Cause Analysis" (queued, not yet extracted in this repo — confirmed via targeted search of the live paper for this cross-ref only):**
This paper builds an explicit 16-item taxonomy of LLM RCA reasoning failures from 48,000 simulated failure scenarios across six LLMs and two agentic workflows. One of its named failure modes, **RF-03 "Confused provenance" — attributing causation to the component observing/reporting a symptom rather than its true source** — is conceptually the same phenomenon Zalando calls "Surface Attribution Error": the model anchors on a prominently-mentioned component (S3, DynamoDB) rather than reasoning about actual causality. This is a strong convergence between an independent academic taxonomy (built from synthetic cloud-fault-injection scenarios) and a two-year field observation (built from real postmortem prose) — two very different methodologies landing on the same named failure pattern. This is worth flagging to synthesis as a candidate for the RQ3 failure map: "surface/keyword-anchoring attribution bias" looks like a genuine, recurring, cross-context LLM-RCA failure mode, not a one-off artefact of Zalando's specific pipeline or prompts. Caveat: 2601.22208 evaluates fault-injection/simulated scenarios and (per its own framing) agentic REACT/Plan-and-Execute workflows, which is a different population and a different era-tag (E3, agentic) from Zalando's mostly-E2 pipeline over real prose — the convergence is at the level of *failure-mode taxonomy*, not at the level of comparable effect sizes, and should be presented that way, not merged into a single number.

**2. vs. arXiv 2604.12108, Google "Auto-Diagnose" (LLM-based integration-test-failure diagnosis at Google, ICSE-SEIP 2026; queued, not yet extracted — confirmed via targeted search for this cross-ref only):** Auto-Diagnose reports **90.14% accuracy on a manual evaluation of 71 real-world failures**, following Google-wide deployment across 52,635 distinct failing tests, with only 5.8% of user feedback marking it "not helpful." This is a materially higher headline accuracy than Zalando's field-observed ~10% persistent error rate would imply (i.e., Zalando's residual error alone is roughly the same order of magnitude as Auto-Diagnose's entire *reported* error rate). Several reasons this is a tension worth surfacing to synthesis rather than a straightforward contradiction to resolve:
   - **Different tasks entirely.** Auto-Diagnose diagnoses *CI integration-test failures* (a narrower, more structured, more immediately-verifiable target — did the test fail because of code X or environment Y?) against a live, re-runnable system. Zalando's pipeline analyses *production-incident postmortem narratives after the fact* (open-ended prose, no live system to re-query, human-authored write-ups of uncertain completeness). These are not the same population or the same difficulty profile.
   - **Different evaluation populations/methods.** Auto-Diagnose's 90.14% is a "manual evaluation on 71 real-world failures" — small-n, Google-internal, vendor-graded, with no stated inter-rater methodology found in this cross-ref-only search either. Zalando's ~10% is a two-year impressionistic estimate with no stated n or audit protocol. Neither number is rigorously audited by the standard this protocol wants; both should be graded down accordingly rather than treated as precise enough to subtract from one another.
   - **COI asymmetry.** Auto-Diagnose is a vendor (Google)-authored, vendor-deployed, vendor-graded self-evaluation of Google's own internal tool (Tier B/C with COI, per the ledger's existing note) — exactly the profile PROTOCOL §3 flags for COI on accuracy claims. Zalando, by contrast, is a non-vendor organisation with no product at stake, actively reporting a persistent unsolved flaw two years in. This asymmetry (vendor claims high accuracy on its own tool; non-vendor practitioner reports a stubborn, unresolved error mode in a comparable task) is itself a pattern worth naming in the RQ2/RQ6 synthesis on measurement trustworthiness: **self-graded vendor accuracy claims for LLM diagnosis/RCA tools should be treated with more scepticism than field practitioner accounts that report their own tool's persistent flaws**, precisely because the latter has no incentive to under-report error.
   - **Recommend presenting both to synthesis as a tension, not resolving it here** — the task brief for this record asked only to report cross-ref fit, not adjudicate; a genuine adjudication would need the full text of both queued sources (neither is yet extracted in this repository) and a closer look at what "accuracy" meant operationally in each case (Auto-Diagnose's 90.14% is presumably "correct root cause identified," which is a coarser pass/fail judgement than Zalando's specific "was this technology causally implicated or merely mentioned" attribution question).

**3. General fit with the T8 (debugging/incident response) evidence base:** per the ledger, T8/incident-response material is explicitly "thin" so far (LEDGER.md batch-log note, phase-0 batch 3) and this source, plus the two queued arXiv papers above, would together substantially strengthen it. This source is a rare item that reports genuine negative/unresolved findings from sustained production use — exactly the "postmortems of AI rollouts" PROTOCOL §6 flags as "the scarcest and most valuable Tier C material."
