## What this document is
An empirical **cross-sectional perception survey**, not a critique or methods paper. 415 valid responses (688 received) from practitioners recruited by email from 56 open-source communities (including IBM-, Oracle- and Google-hosted repositories, PyTorch and similar). Survey items are adapted from Meyer et al. 2014 and others, mapped to the five SPACE dimensions, and rated on Likert scales for perceived change attributable to AI. Analysis: respondents are split by self-reported usage frequency into non-frequent (never, rarely, sometimes) and frequent (often, always) users, with stacked bars and violin plots only. No inferential statistics are reported. RQ2 is a qualitative coding of 206 open-text answers, with SPACE as the codeset (two coders on 20% of responses, 90% Jaccard agreement, then one coder).

It does not attack metrics empirically. Its measurement argument is a short discussion (s6.2): activity-level metrics look good while performance items do not, so use holistic frameworks (Brooks 1995, SPACE). The "spurious" claim is an interpretation of self-reported data.

## Full notes
**Population.** N=415. Roles: full-stack 36.9%, backend 16.9%, data/ML 15.4%. 90.6% men. 57.8% at large or extra-large organisations. 82.2% have more than 5 years' experience. Sample sizes of the frequent and non-frequent groups are not stated in the text. Open-source community recruitment means selection bias towards OSS-active, senior respondents.

**Tool and period.** Unspecified: "AI tools", "GenAI". Survey fielded in 2025 (two weeks; month not stated). No modality is recorded (autocomplete, chat or agent); one quote mentions "agentic systems". Quotes mention ChatGPT-like and email assistants (Superhuman). Era is therefore not assignable by modality.

**RQ1 results (all self-reported perceived change; frequent vs non-frequent users).**
- Aggregate: frequent users have slightly higher median scores in every dimension except Communication, which is equal. All medians stay inside the neutral "no change" band. The authors conclude no substantial positive or negative shift yet.
- Satisfaction: S1 workload manageable, agree 68.6% vs 55.3%. S4 job security, 55.4% vs 52.9%. S2 exhaustion, still high at 65.2% vs 62.8%. S3 less interested in work, 46% vs 59.6%.
- Performance: P1 lines changed per day, frequent users "more" or "much more" 72.7% (47.2 + 25.5); non-frequent mostly no change (66.7%). P2 test pass rate: 67.4% of frequent users report no change or a decline. P3 API methods learned: 58.6% report no change or a decline.
- Activity: more commits, 48.3% vs 7.9%. More test cases, 56.5% vs 24.8%. More work items closed, 55.9% vs 9.1%. 75.4% of frequent users report no increase in time writing code (A2); 61.3% report no increase in time searching for information (A4). More code reviews done, 25.1% vs 9.8% (A6). 84.3% of frequent users say AI did not reduce time spent reviewing (A7). Note "did not reduce" is not "increased".
- Communication: 90-94% report no change on meetings and email, regardless of usage (C1-C3); C4 76.3% vs 82.3%.
- Efficiency: more time per work item (E1), 35.8% of frequent vs 82.2% of non-frequent. Personal browsing, 19% vs 7.1% (E2; direction ambiguous in text). 76.3% of frequent users report no positive change in interruptions.

**RQ2 (qualitative, N=206).** Challenges: Ch1 cognitive load of verifying output; Ch2 effort shifted to reviewing others' AI output; Ch3 organisational "AI = faster" expectations raise pressure; Ch4 verbosity of AI output (especially tests); Ch5 reliance on AI before foundational knowledge (juniors); Ch6-7 AI supports individual rather than shared coordination work. Strategies: training, assistive-not-output norms, confidence and uncertainty disclosure in PRs, project-context integration, AI for first-pass review only, quality gates for AI-heavy changes. Strategies are respondent suggestions, not tested interventions. The qualitative counts per theme are not reported.

**Heterogeneity.** No seniority, task-type, codebase-maturity or tool split is reported; the only split is usage frequency. Juniors are discussed only in quotes (P192, P234).

**Recommended measures.** None operationalised. Implicit recommendations: do not rely on activity counts (commits, LOC, items closed), pair them with performance and quality items, and track exhaustion and cognitive load. Related-work section calls LOC and commit counts "narrow, misleading, easily gamed", citing earlier literature, not new evidence.

## Tier & era grading
- Type: structured survey of practitioners, self-reported only. Not a methodology source; it does not test any metric.
- Tier: **C (low end)**, directional only. It is a peer-reviewed, independent, large-N survey, which is better than blog anecdote. It has no counterfactual, no objective outcome, a self-selected OSS sample and no inferential statistics, so it cannot sit at Tier B.
- Era: **not assignable by modality (unspecified, mixed)**. Per PROTOCOL s2 and CLAUDE.md rule 5, it may support only baselines, methodology points and era-independent effects (perception patterns, review burden, effort redistribution as a mechanism hypothesis). It must not carry an E3 effect-size claim. Suggested tag in rows: `mixed/unspecified (E1-E3 unresolved)`.
- Independence: academic, no vendor funding, no tool-vendor authors. Not part of a vendor provenance cluster. It is NOT independent of the METR and Stack Overflow stats it cites (uses them as framing only, no data reuse).

## Measured vs self-reported
Everything is self-reported, including items that sound objective (commits, lines changed, test pass rate, time per item). No measured data. No perceived-vs-measured comparison is made inside the paper. The paper's reference to METR (-19%) and Google RCT (-21% time) is related-work context only; it states the Google result as ~21% reduction (Paradis et al. 2025), cited from secondary mention. The "gap" the authors imply (activity up, performance flat) is between two sets of self-reported items, not between perception and measurement, so it must not be recorded as an RQ2 perception-reality delta.

## Can / cannot support - verdicts
- CAN support: directional, Tier C evidence that frequent users perceive more throughput (commits, items closed, lines changed) while perceiving no review-time reduction and persistent exhaustion; a mechanism hypothesis for bottleneck displacement to review (tension s5.6) and for cognitive-load cost (RQ4, skill/cognitive theme); a pre-agentic-or-mixed corroboration that activity-type self-reports diverge from quality-type self-reports.
- CAN support (weakly): RQ6 argument that activity items (commits, items closed, LOC) are what AI users report rising, so these cannot stand as proof of value; and that exhaustion/cognitive-load should be instrumented. This is the authors' interpretation, not a tested result.
- CANNOT support: any effect size on actual productivity; any "AI makes devs slower or net-negative" claim; "spurious" as a demonstrated finding; any E3 claim; seniority, task-type or codebase conclusions; causality (usage frequency is confounded with role, enthusiasm and task mix); any anti-metric verdict on acceptance rate (not studied).

## Red flags / things the orchestrator should know
1. Title and abstract overreach: "spurious" and "hidden costs" are not demonstrated; the body itself says medians are all in the neutral band and offers no objective outcome. Do not cite the title as a finding.
2. v1's abstract said "limited overall productivity change"; v2 hardened this to "redistribution of effort" without new data. Cite v2 with this noted.
3. The "84.3% did not reduce review time" statistic is a non-reduction, which includes no change; it is not evidence of increased review time. Review burden claims rest on 25.1% vs 9.8% doing more reviews and on quotes.
4. Group sizes (frequent vs non-frequent) and test statistics are missing; percentages cannot be weighted or CI'd. Some reported pairs look inconsistent with the text direction (E2 19% vs 7.1% described as frequent users spending less time on personal browsing; S3 reading is ambiguous). Treat item-level percentages as approximate, check the Zenodo appendix before any exec-facing use.
5. Frequent vs non-frequent comparison conflates who adopts with what AI does; non-frequent users also rate "no change" because they use little AI.
6. Sample: OSS-community recruitment, 82% >5 years' experience, 90.6% men; low generalisability to enterprise teams.
7. Era unknown; the same survey could cover autocomplete-only users.
8. Not a methodological critique of metrics, so it only weakly feeds the PROTOCOL s5.7 anti-metrics list (mention only as the authors' stated view, citing earlier work for the LOC/commit critique).
9. Venue acceptance (FSE '26 Companion) is taken from the v2 template only; not independently confirmed.
