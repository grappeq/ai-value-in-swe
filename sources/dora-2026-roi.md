## Tier, era, independence

- **Tier: D — vendor & consultancy publication, not Tier B.** This is a deliberate departure from dora-2025.md and dora-2024.md's Tier B grade, and needs justifying because it carries the "DORA" brand. Reasons:
  1. It reports **no new data collection of its own** (see Method, below) — its only AI-specific empirical input is the 2025 DORA survey (already graded B), re-cited via footnotes to specific pages of that report.
  2. Its central numeric content — the entire "Calculate the ROI" and "Quantify your AI investment" chapters and the Appendix calculator — is an **illustrative financial model built on hypothetical example inputs**, explicitly labelled by the authors as such: *"Treat these calculations as a high-uncertainty estimate meant to spark a conversation, rather than a rigid mathematical formula"* and *"This is a sample ROI calculator that is meant for demonstration purposes only."*
  3. It is **co-authored with Google Cloud's commercial consulting arm** ("delta"), whose bio explicitly names "driving measurable P&L impact" and "agentic transformation" consulting engagements; the report repeatedly invites readers to "contact us" for Google Cloud Professional Services / delta engagements.
  4. It cites **Google Cloud's own marketing statistics as supporting evidence** without independent audit — e.g. "Google Cloud customers found an average of 727% return on their investment in Google Cloud AI in three years" (sourced to a Google Cloud transformation blog post, not a disclosed methodology) and "78% of executives report realizing a return on investment from at least one gen AI use case" / "88% of early adopters utilizing agentic AI are already seeing positive returns" (both sourced to Google Cloud's own "ROI of AI 2025" publication).
  This is exactly the profile PROTOCOL §3 assigns to Tier D ("GitHub/Microsoft/Anthropic/McKinsey-class publications... use for directional signal and to understand the hype baseline"), not Tier B's "large-scale telemetry without randomisation, longitudinal industry reports." The DORA 2025 survey it draws on remains Tier B in its own right; this report is a Tier D interpretive/financial-modelling layer built on top of it.
- **Era: mixed E1/E2/E3, weighted E1/E2 — same underlying data as dora-2025, with an important framing mismatch.** The report's *rhetoric* is heavily agentic ("the software engineering industry has entered the agentic era," "scaling autonomous agents in production," "in the agentic era, an IDP is... the risk mitigator... for AI agents"), but it introduces **no new agent-mode usage statistics** of its own — every AI-adoption-outcome figure it uses is inherited from the DORA 2025 survey, whose own instrument found 61% of respondents "never" use agent mode (per sources/dora-2025.md). The report never repeats or updates that 61% figure, creating a mismatch between the document's agentic framing and the pre-agentic-weighted evidence underneath it. Flag this mismatch explicitly if this source is cited for anything E3-specific — it should not be.
- **Independence / COI: strong vendor flag, stronger than the vendor-adjacency flag on dora-2024/dora-2025.** Published jointly by Google Cloud's DORA team and Google Cloud's paid consulting practice (delta); the report functions in part as consulting-engagement collateral. No independent/non-Google co-authorship, no disclosed sponsor list distinct from Google Cloud itself. COI-flag per CLAUDE.md rule 7.

## Method & population

- **This is not new data collection — it is an interpretive synthesis plus a financial-modelling framework.** There is no new survey, no new sample, no new fielding dates, and no new n disclosed anywhere in the document. Its own citations confirm this: every AI-adoption-outcome claim ("increased AI adoption was associated with...") is footnoted directly to specific pages of **DORA, 2025 State of AI-assisted Software Development** (e.g. footnotes to pp.30, 37–42 of that report). Other empirical inputs are all secondary and external to DORA's own research programme: DORA AI Capabilities Model report (2025); an unspecified "internal research study conducted by Google in 2025" drawing on unnamed "global enterprises" (no n, no methodology disclosed); Stanford HAI 2025 AI Index; Stanford University "Software Engineering Productivity Research" (the Denisov-Blanch telemetry group, already an anchor source in sources/stanford-denisov-blanch.md); the MIT NANDA project's "GenAI Divide" report; and several of Google Cloud's own marketing/whitepaper publications ("The ROI of AI 2025," "The ROI of Gen AI," "How Businesses Achieve Strong ROI with Google Cloud AI").
- **The "citations retrieved February, 2026" line refers to reference-retrieval dates, not primary data collection dates.** There is no equivalent of a survey fielding window to record.
- **The ROI/cost figures throughout the report are illustrative, assumption-driven examples, not measured organisational outcomes.** The Appendix explicitly walks through a hypothetical "500-person engineering organisation" with author-chosen input assumptions (see Key findings below) — none of these numbers were measured from a real customer or survey respondent; they are worked examples meant to demonstrate the calculator's mechanics.
- **Measurement basis: three distinct categories, and they should not be conflated:**
  1. **Self-reported** — the underlying DORA-2025-survey-derived outcome associations (individual effectiveness, code quality, throughput, instability, etc.) are all self-report, inherited wholesale from that report's own self-report basis.
  2. **Modelled/illustrative** — all ROI, cost, J-curve-cost, and "instability tax" dollar figures in this report are the output of a hypothetical calculator exercise with author-chosen assumptions, not measurements of any real deployment. This is a third category distinct from "measured" and "self-reported" and should be tagged as such in the evidence table if extracted (do not tag as "measured").
  3. **Vendor marketing claims** — a small number of figures (727% three-year ROI, 78%/88% executive-survey stats, "~8 month average payback period... from Google Cloud") are lifted from Google Cloud's own promotional publications without independent methodology disclosure.

## Key findings

### The J-curve model (Figures 1 and 2, identical diagram repeated twice)

- **Diagram axes:** x-axis = Time, y-axis = Value realised. The curve starts at "Time zero / Starting baseline," dips below baseline through a labelled trough region, then rises to "Eventual exponential growth."
- **Three named contributing factors for the dip** (not sequential stages — presented as three concurrent systemic causes of the same trough):
  1. **The learning curve** — "teams dedicate time away from regular feature delivery to learn new interfaces, adapt their daily workflows, and master advanced techniques from... prompting AI to systems built on context, intent, and specification."
  2. **The verification tax** — "developers invest time reviewing generated code due to concerns about the trustworthiness of output and hallucinations," compounded by AI increasing the sheer volume of code produced, which "expands the overall review burden."
  3. **Pipeline adaptation** — "as individual developers generate code faster, downstream processes like testing and change approval must scale to handle the increased volume, revealing opportunities to clear legacy constraints."
- **Evidence backing the model: analogy/precedent, not new measurement, and not a reanalysis of the 2025 survey's outcome data as a time series.** The report is explicit that the J-curve is "a well-documented phenomenon in major organizational transformations" and states: "we consistently find this trajectory across various technical disciplines, such as continuous delivery and platform engineering. Furthermore, our recent guidance on innovating with generative AI... confirms that this identical structural reality applies to the adoption of advanced coding models." No panel/longitudinal AI-adoption dataset is cited or newly analysed to show an actual dip-then-rise trajectory for AI specifically — the 2025 DORA survey that supplies the report's only AI-specific data point is a **single-wave cross-sectional survey** (per sources/dora-2025.md), incapable of showing a within-cohort trajectory over time. The J-curve is explicitly presented as a **forecasting heuristic borrowed from general technology-adoption/organisational-change literature and asserted to apply to AI by analogy**, not as an empirical finding derived from AI-adoption panel data. The report itself concedes this uncertainty: "the specific dimensions of this curve, the depth of the productivity trough, and the duration before growth accelerates remain unpredictable."

### The "instability tax"

- **Definition (as used in the report):** the negative financial impact — lost revenue and/or additional cost from downtime — that results from AI adoption's association with rising software delivery instability (i.e., a rising change failure rate), net of any throughput gains from more frequent deployments.
- **Formula (from the "Identify the value drivers" chapter):**
  `Downtime impact = (Current deploys × Current CFR × FDRT × Cost of downtime per hour) − (Target deploys × Target CFR × FDRT × Cost of downtime per hour)`
  where CFR = change failure rate, FDRT = failed deployment recovery time (hours).
- **Worked example (illustrative, not measured):** current deployments/year = 50, current CFR = 5%, target deployments/year = 56 (+12%), target CFR = 6% (+20% relative), FDRT held constant at 4 hours, cost of downtime = $100,000/hour.
  `(50 × 0.05 × 4 × $100,000) − (56 × 0.06 × 4 × $100,000) = $1,000,000 − $1,344,000 = −$344,000`
  The report states this as a **negative** value line item ("downtime impact: −$344,000") inside its total-value calculation, i.e. the modelled instability effect is large enough to be a net cost in this illustrative scenario, not fully offset by the extra two deployments/features. This is the exact figure the follow-up-lead brief (via sources/dora-2025.md) had flagged in advance.
- The report is candid that "the outcome of this calculation may also be negative due to the 'instability tax'" — it is presented as a real risk the calculator can surface, not guaranteed to be small.

### ROI / cost quantification (RQ4-relevant — illustrative worked example, "500-person engineering organisation")

All figures below are the report's own **sample/demonstration** calculation, built from author-chosen assumptions (not measured):

- **Inputs:** 500 FTE; fully loaded salary $176,000; net time saved per developer 12.5% (~1hr/8hr day); AI licence $250/user/yr; additional AI costs $80/user/yr; training $9,600/user/yr; additional AI infrastructure $100,000/yr; J-curve productivity drop 15% over 3 months; portfolio revenue $100M; idea success rate 33%; revenue impact per successful feature 0.5%; current/target deployments 50→56; current/target CFR 5%→6%; FDRT 4 hours; downtime cost $100,000/hour.
- **Total hard costs** (licences + additional AI costs + training, × 500 FTE, + $100k infrastructure) = **$5,065,000**.
- **J-curve cost** (= Staff × Salary × drop% × duration = 500 × $176,000 × 15% × 3/12) = **$3,300,000**.
- **Total first-year investment** = hard costs + J-curve cost = **$8,365,000**.
- **Headcount reinvestment capacity** (= Staff × Salary × net time saved = 500 × $176,000 × 12.5%) = **$11,000,000**.
- **Revenue from extra feature deployments** ((56−50) × 33% × 0.5% × $100M) = **$990,000**.
- **Downtime impact ("instability tax")** = **−$344,000** (see above).
- **Total first-year value** = $11,000,000 + $990,000 − $344,000 = **$11,646,000**.
- **First-year benefit** = value − investment = $11,646,000 − $8,365,000 = **$3,281,000**.
- **ROI** = benefit / investment = **39%**.
- **Payback period** = investment / return = **0.7 years (≈8 months)**.
- This is the rare cost/ROI quantification RQ4 pre-registered as scarce (PROTOCOL §1) — but it must be recorded as a **methodology demonstration with hypothetical inputs**, not as a measured organisational result. It is useful for RQ4 chiefly as a template for what a cost model *could* look like (headcount-reinvestment value, J-curve/tuition cost, verification-tax proxying via deployment frequency, instability-tax formula), not as an effect size to cite.
- Adjacent (non-DORA, secondhand) figures reported in passing, useful as separate leads rather than DORA findings: Stanford telemetry group cited as showing "35–40% productivity gain on simple, greenfield tasks" vs "10% or less" on "complex, legacy brownfield code" (sourced to `softwareengineeringproductivity.stanford.edu`, no further citation detail given — directly relevant to PROTOCOL §5 tension 2 and worth chasing in the Stanford/Denisov-Blanch source stream); Stanford AI Index's claim that inference cost for frontier models fell "by a factor of 280" between Nov 2022 and Oct 2024; Amazon's self-reported "15.9%" developer-experience improvement (Haughwout, 2025, AWS blog).

### Treatment of the 2024 (negative throughput) → 2025 (positive throughput) story, and the persistent instability finding

- The report does **not** re-litigate or re-quote the 2024 anchor numbers (the −1.5% throughput / −7.2% stability figures already extracted in sources/dora-2024.md) — it simply asserts the 2025 findings as the current baseline: "increased AI adoption was associated with an increase in higher software delivery throughput. Teams are able to deliver more software changes in less time," footnoted to DORA 2025 pp.37–38.
- It explicitly restates the persistent instability finding: "in the 2025 DORA report, increased AI adoption was associated with an increase in software delivery instability... AI-assisted coding can increase the volume and velocity of code generation, overwhelming existing deployment pipelines and manual review gates."
- **The reframing move:** rather than treating this instability finding as an open, unresolved caveat (which is how DORA 2025 itself treated it — see below), this report **folds it into the J-curve as an expected, temporary "tuition cost"**: "We view this temporary rise in instability as part of the expected J-Curve (or tuition cost) of early AI adoption. To flatten this curve... organizations must invest in their underlying engineering platforms..." Elsewhere: "This temporary instability isn't a signal of failure but a necessary investment in learning and adaptation."
- This reframing is **not supported by new evidence that the instability trend actually resolves over time**. It is worth setting directly against DORA 2025's own explicit finding (per sources/dora-2025.md): DORA 2025 tested whether AI adoption *moderates* (blunts) instability's harms and "found no evidence of such a moderating effect... instability still has significant detrimental effects on crucial outcomes like product performance and burnout, which can ultimately negate any perceived gains in throughput." The 2025 report treated persistent instability as a live, unresolved risk to the "amplifier" thesis; this 2026 report recasts the identical finding as a self-limiting, temporary phase of a J-curve — a narrative upgrade in tone that is not accompanied by any new longitudinal or panel data showing the dip actually curving back up for AI adoption specifically. (See Cross-reference section below.)

## What this can and cannot support

Can support (Tier D, with the caveats above): a **plausible, well-articulated cost-and-value modelling framework** for organisations building their own AI ROI business case (headcount-reinvestment value, feature-revenue value, instability-tax/downtime-cost value, J-curve tuition-cost budgeting) — useful methodologically for RQ4/RQ6 (what to instrument, how to frame a cost model) even though its own worked numbers are hypothetical. Can support directional colour on how a vendor-adjacent research programme is currently narrating the 2024→2025 instability tension (as "tuition," not as an open risk). **Cannot support:** any measured ROI, cost, or instability-tax effect size (all illustrative); any claim that the J-curve has been empirically observed for AI adoption (no panel data exists in this or the 2025 report); any new agentic/E3-specific usage statistic (none introduced); any claim that DORA 2025's own null finding on instability-moderation has been overturned or resolved (it has not — this report simply reframes it).

## Cross-reference note re PROTOCOL §5 tension 3 (throughput vs. stability) — guidance only, no edits made to synthesis/contradictions.md

This source **reframes rather than resolves** the tension. It does not add new evidence that instability is transient or self-correcting for AI adoption specifically; it applies a general-purpose organisational-change heuristic (the J-curve, borrowed from unrelated domains — continuous delivery adoption, platform engineering adoption) to a finding whose own primary source (DORA 2025) explicitly tested and rejected a related idea (that AI adoption might moderate/blunt instability's harms) and found no such effect. The 2026 ROI report's framing ("not a signal of failure," "necessary investment," "tuition cost") is a rhetorical softening of an unresolved empirical result, not a new empirical resolution of it. If contradictions.md already logs the 2024→2025 throughput flip with persistent instability, the recommended addition (for whoever maintains that file) is a note that the 2026 ROI report attempts a narrative resolution ("it's temporary") that is not evidentially grounded — the underlying data (single-wave 2025 cross-sectional self-report) cannot distinguish a temporary J-curve dip from a persistent structural cost, and DORA's own moderation test found no evidence for the "it resolves with more capability" story either. This is best characterised as **dodge-via-reframe**, not resolution, and should be flagged as such rather than treated as new evidence that the tension has closed.
