## Tier, era, independence

- **Tier: C, weak end — bordering D.** Justification for the call: this is an executive disclosure via press interview, not an engineering-org write-up with disclosed methodology (contrast `cloudflare-2026-ai-review`, which is company-authored with exact instrumented percentiles). No methodology is stated anywhere for how "70% of committed code is AI-originated" or "84% agentic coding users" were computed — no definition of the measurement basis (git-blame heuristic? IDE telemetry? self-report survey of engineers? PR label like he-2026's `created-by-ai`?). The figures have already drifted once between two press outlets (Forbes vs briefs.co) on the same underlying interview, which is direct evidence of exactly the kind of secondary-source unreliability CLAUDE.md rule 2 warns about. It clears Tier C rather than D because: the org (Uber) is named, the disclosing executive is named, the figures are specific and falsifiable (not vague marketing language), and a second independent outlet (Bloomberg) corroborates the existence of the follow-up policy response — this is closer to "practitioner signal with real numbers" than to an anonymous vendor whitepaper. **Grade this source C for the qualitative narrative (budget overrun occurred, response was a spending cap, COO is skeptical of ROI) and treat every individual percentage/dollar figure as C-minus/single-sourced** — do not let the org-level Tier C grade launder the precision of numbers that were never independently measured.
- **Era: E3, confirmed.** Tools named are Claude Code (dominant) and Cursor (plateaued); adoption is explicitly framed by "agentic coding users" classification, consistent with §2's E3 definition.
- **Independence:** Uber is not a developer-tooling vendor — no product-COI in the Tier D sense (it has nothing to sell by publishing these numbers). But two distinct incentive flags apply, in tension with each other:
  1. **AI-forward positioning incentive.** Naga's other public appearances (HumanX talk on "agentic engineering") position Uber as an aggressive, cutting-edge AI adopter — a reputational incentive toward touting high adoption/committed-code percentages.
  2. **Candour-about-failure counter-signal.** Unusually, the same executive is simultaneously disclosing a budget failure, a leaderboard-driven perverse incentive, and a walk-back (spending cap) — companies rarely volunteer this kind of admission, which lends the *qualitative* story (overrun happened, response was a cap) more credibility than a purely self-congratulatory disclosure would carry. The COO's on-record ROI skepticism (below) is a genuine negative/critical data point, not spin.
  3. **The Information's incentive:** subscription tech-business journalism, structurally rewarded for dramatic scoop framing ("blow up AI budgets") — a moderate framing-incentive flag, independent of Uber's own incentives.
  4. **Forbes' incentive:** contributor-network content (janakirammsv is a regular outside contributor, not Forbes staff), which correlates with lower editorial fact-checking rigour than staff reporting — noted, not asserted as demonstrated error here, but consistent with the numeric drift observed versus other outlets.

**Root-cause mechanism (self-report/journalistic inference, not measured):** Uber deployed Claude Code organisation-wide without usage controls and additionally ran **internal leaderboards ranking engineers by Claude Code usage**, creating a direct cultural incentive to consume more tokens — reported consistently across Forbes and TechCrunch as the proximate cause of the overrun. This is a vivid, named-org instance of Goodhart's-law-style metric gaming (surfaced independently by Hacker News commenters as "tokenmaxxing culture") and is a strong candidate for the RQ6 anti-metrics list: **do not rank or incentivise raw AI-tool usage/token consumption as a productivity proxy.**

## Productivity/outcome claims attached — verdict: essentially absent, and one explicit negative

Per CLAUDE.md rule 3 (record nulls with the same diligence as positives): **no source in this chain reports a productivity, velocity, or quality outcome measurement tied to the cost and adoption figures.** Forbes characterises the rollout as "a success" from a productivity standpoint but supplies no metric for this — it is asserted, not shown. The one substantive outcome-adjacent statement is a **negative/skeptical one**: Uber COO Andrew Macdonald (Fortune, 26 May 2026) explicitly declined to draw a line from AI-tool adoption to shipped value: *"it's very hard to draw a line"* between AI coding-tool statistics and "25% more useful consumer features," and *"that link is not there yet."* This is the sharpest and most credible-because-unflattering data point in the whole record — a named C-suite executive at the same company, on record, saying the ROI case has not been made. Record this as a genuine RQ4/RQ5 null result, not merely as colour.

## What this can and cannot support

**Can support:** that Uber, a large named non-vendor organisation with roughly 5,000 engineers, experienced rapid Claude Code/Cursor adoption from December 2025 and disclosed (via a single press interview) per-engineer monthly costs in the low-hundreds-of-dollars range on average with a heavy-user tail reaching $500–2,000/month; that the company's own 2026 AI-tooling budget was exhausted well ahead of schedule; that the company responded with a hard usage cap rather than an unlimited increase; and that at least one senior executive (the COO) is on record stating the productivity/value case for this spend is not yet demonstrated. Can support, at low confidence given the single-interview provenance, the existence of adoption and AI-originated-code percentages in the same general range reported elsewhere in this literature.

**Cannot support:** any precise cost-per-engineer figure as a measured or audited number (no methodology disclosed for any percentage); any claim that 70% "AI-originated" or 11% "agent-only" code was measured the same way any other source in this evidence base measures AI-authorship (contrast he-2026's `created-by-ai` PR label, which at least has a stated definition); any productivity or quality outcome tied causally to the spend (none reported, and the COO explicitly says the link is unproven); any generalisable claim about typical enterprise AI-coding cost, given this is one self-selected, aggressively-rolled-out, incentive-distorted (leaderboard-driven) deployment; and no claim should treat the 84% and 95% figures, or the CTO's 11% and CEO's ~10% figures, as interchangeable restatements of the same underlying metric — they are documented as distinct or at least ambiguously related figures above.

## Cross-ref assessment (for orchestrator review — synthesis/contradictions.md NOT edited)

**Is a coherent RQ4 cost picture emerging, or are Uber, he-2026, and Cloudflare incommensurable? Verdict: partially coherent on order-of-magnitude and shape, but still siloed from outcome data — and this siloing is now a two-source pattern, not a one-off.**

1. **vs. he-2026-2x-mandate (Tier A, DiD-identified, per-developer-month Claude Code token spend $42→$856 over Jul 2025–Apr 2026, ~20× growth, single unnamed B2B firm, several hundred engineers):** The order of magnitude lines up better than the headline numbers first suggest. He et al.'s **org-wide average** ($856/month at the end of a 20× ramp) sits *above* Uber's reported **org-wide average** ($150–250/month) but *below* Uber's **power-user tier** ($500–2,000/month) — i.e. he-2026's "average developer" looks more like Uber's "power user" by the end of the ramp. Both sources independently describe the same qualitative shape: a low, seat-license-like baseline cost that balloons under heavy/incentivised usage, with the heaviest users running into the thousands of dollars per month. This is a genuine (if thin — two organisations, one Tier A/telemetry-based and one Tier C/press-disclosed) triangulation point for the claim "per-developer agentic-coding token spend is growing fast, unpredictably, and is concentrated in a heavy-usage tail" — worth flagging to the orchestrator as the first cross-organisation cost-magnitude corroboration in this evidence base. It is **not** a triangulation of any *specific* dollar figure — the measurement bases are completely different (audited telemetry vs undisclosed self-report) and should never be pooled into a single point estimate.
2. **vs. cloudflare-2026-ai-review (Tier C strong-end, $0.98 median cost per AI code-review run, ~120bn tokens/month, no effectiveness data):** Not directly comparable — Cloudflare's unit is "per review run" (an event), Uber/he-2026's unit is "per developer per month" (a rate). A rough, explicitly-flagged-as-mine, back-of-envelope: Cloudflare's 131,246 review runs/month × $0.98 ≈ $128,600/month organisation-wide is the same rough order of magnitude as perhaps 60–260 Uber engineers running at their power-user rate — i.e. the *scale* of enterprise agentic-AI spend implied by both stories is broadly consistent (tens to low hundreds of thousands of dollars per month at large-company scale), but this comparison rests on unstated assumptions on both sides and should be presented as an illustrative sanity check, not a finding.
3. **vs. QCon Böckeler's ~$91k/year/dev claim (queued, NOT yet extracted — flagging per task instructions, not treating as verified evidence):** At face value, $91k/year ≈ $7,583/month is roughly 4–15× above even Uber's power-user ceiling ($2,000/month) and roughly 9× above he-2026's end-of-ramp average ($856/month). This is either (a) a genuinely different and much higher cost regime (a different tool-usage pattern, a different org, or a different accounting scope — e.g. fully-loaded cost including compute/infra/seat rather than token spend alone), or (b) a figure this extraction cannot adjudicate because the source has not been read in full. **Recommend the orchestrator extract Böckeler next specifically to resolve this apparent order-of-magnitude gap** — stretching an un-extracted Tier C/D headline number to fill the RQ4 picture here would be exactly the kind of grade-inflation CLAUDE.md warns against, so this is flagged as an open question, not resolved.
4. **Structural finding echoing Cloudflare's own cross-ref conclusion:** Cloudflare's record already noted that "no organisation in this evidence base has yet published cost data paired with effectiveness data" for code review. Uber extends the same structural gap to code *authoring*: Uber has cost figures (via press disclosure) and adoption figures, but explicitly **no** paired productivity outcome — and its own COO says so on record. Combined with he-2026 (which has *outcome* data — throughput, review-bottleneck effects — but a much thinner cost figure limited to authoring-token-spend only, no review-cost data) and Cloudflare (cost data only, for review not authoring), the emerging RQ4 picture across all three sources is: **cost telemetry and outcome telemetry are being published by different organisations for different task types, and no single source yet pairs the two for the same task.** This is itself becoming the headline RQ4 finding, consistent with the pre-registered thin-evidence expectation, and is now supported by three independent organisations exhibiting the same gap in three different ways (Uber: cost + adoption, no outcome, explicit COO null; he-2026: outcome + partial authoring-cost, but not review-cost; Cloudflare: review-cost, zero outcome).
5. **RQ6 contribution — distinct from RQ4:** Uber's leaderboard-driven token-burn mechanism is a novel (to this evidence base) *named, real-world instance* of a usage-quantity metric being gamed exactly as the anti-metrics literature (§5 tension 7) predicts — useful concrete illustration for the internal-measurement-proposal output, independent of the cost-figure reliability questions above.
