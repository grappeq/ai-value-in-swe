## Tier, era, independence

- **Tier: B** — the DORA-class exemplar named explicitly in PROTOCOL §3 ("large-scale telemetry without randomisation, longitudinal industry reports (DORA-class)"). Cross-sectional industry survey (this wave: n≈3,000) with structured Bayesian regression and DAG-based causal-inference framework. Carries claims with caveats; not Tier A because there is no randomisation and no telemetry — the delivery-performance outcomes are respondent-reported, not measured (see Method section).
- **Era: E1/E2, no agentic framing at all.** Tagged by the tool modality actually described in the report (fielded "early 2024", per report text). The report has **no concept of "agent mode"** — unlike DORA 2025, which explicitly asks respondents about agent-mode use (61% "never" in that wave), the 2024 report's interaction-surface data covers only: chatbots (78.2%), external web interfaces (73.9%), AI tools embedded in the IDE (72.9%), internal web interfaces (58.1%), and AI as part of CI/CD pipelines (50.2%). Top use cases are code writing (74.9% of coders) and summarising information (71.2%) — consistent with autocomplete/chat-assisted workflows, not multi-file plan–execute–verify agent loops. **Assign E2-dominant with an E1 component** (IDE-embedded autocomplete + chat), **E3 not represented in this wave at all** — this is a stronger E1/E2 call than the seed's tentative "[E2; trendline anchor]" tag, and stronger than DORA 2025's own mixed E1/E2/E3 tag, because the 2024 instrument did not yet ask about agentic modalities.
- **Independence / COI:** published by Google Cloud (a vendor of AI developer tooling); same vendor-adjacency flag as dora-2025. No corporate co-sponsor list is printed in this edition comparable to 2025's GitHub/GitLab/SkillBench/Workhelix credits — research partnership with Stack Overflow is noted only for demographic/firmographic question design, not as a funding sponsor. Population is a self-selected convenience sample recruited via DORA's usual channels (not random), consistent across years.

## Method & population

- Cross-sectional online survey; **this year's data: "nearly 3,000 working professionals"** (cumulative across the decade-long DORA programme: "roughly 39,000 professionals"). The dora-2025 record's earlier assumption of a larger single-wave n was not made — this confirms the 2024 wave n independently: **n ≈ 3,000**, notably smaller than 2025's n = 4,867.
- Fielding window: report states only **"fielded in early 2024"** — no more precise month range is given anywhere in the text (checked; no explicit month names attached to survey fielding). This is materially less precise than 2025's exact dates (13 Jun–21 Jul 2025) and should be flagged as a methodology-reporting gap.
- New this year: survey **augmented with in-depth, semi-structured interviews** ("triangulate, and provide additional context") — qualitative interview quotes (P1–P11 etc.) appear throughout the AI chapter.
- Demographics (this wave): 83% men, 12% women; median 16 years working experience, 5 years in current role, 3 years on current team; industries: Technology 35.7%, Financial Services 15.7%, Retail/Consumer/E-commerce 9.5%; org size skewed large (24.1% at 10,000+ employees). Demographic/firmographic question design benchmarked against the 2023 Stack Overflow Developer Survey; report states "no major discrepancies between our sample and Stack Overflow's."
- **AI adoption measurement:** built as a **factor from a "general AI reliance" item plus covarying task-level reliance items** (code writing, summarising information, code explanation, code optimisation, documentation, test writing) — the report explicitly rejected usage *frequency* as the measure in favour of reliance/centrality: "measuring usage frequency is likely not as meaningful as measuring reliance."
- **Analysis:** Bayesian regression with Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG)-based causal-inference model design (explicitly modelled on DAGitty-style adjustment sets), reporting point estimates with **89% uncertainty intervals** (not confidence intervals — Bayesian framing throughout, consistent with 2025). All "if AI adoption increases by 25%..." statements are the report's own standard framing device, repeated across every capability studied that year (not unique to AI).
- **Measurement basis: all outcomes are survey-based self-report**, including software delivery throughput and stability — there is no telemetry component anywhere in the AI-adoption analysis (telemetry is mentioned only in passing, as one of several *platform-feedback* channels in the unrelated platform-engineering chapter, not as a data source for the AI findings). This is the same self-report caveat already flagged for DORA 2025; it applies with equal force to 2024, so the 2024→2025 "flip" is a **self-report-to-self-report** comparison, not a self-report vs. measured contrast.

## Key findings (exact as reported; all self-reported unless noted)

**Delivery throughput and stability (Figure 10 — the anchor finding):**
- Chart is labelled **"Delivery throughput"** and **"Delivery stability"** (not "instability") — the report's own outcome construct is **stability** (higher = better), and the reported effect is a *decrease* in that construct.
- Exact text: *"the effect on delivery throughput is small, but likely negative (an estimated 1.5% reduction for every 25% increase in AI adoption). The negative impact on delivery stability is larger (an estimated 7.2% reduction for every 25% increase in AI adoption)."*
- Chart point estimates as printed: **Delivery throughput −1.5%**; **Delivery stability −7.2%** (both are reductions in the named construct; both are labelled as point estimates with 89% uncertainty intervals, no numeric interval bounds given in text).
- **Sign/framing note (important, per task instructions):** the 2024 primary source frames this as **"−7.2% on delivery stability"** (stability falling), not as "+7.2% on instability" (instability rising). Both are the same underlying direction of effect (worse stability), but the construct name differs by report: the 2025 report (per sources/dora-2025.md, quoting this 2024 finding) paraphrases it as *"an estimated 7.2% increase in software delivery instability"* — this is a valid restatement of the same effect under an inverted construct label, not a different number, but future synthesis work should use the 2024 report's own construct name ("stability", not "instability") when citing this record, and note the inversion explicitly if quoting the 2025 report's paraphrase.
- **Construct definitions (critical for 2024↔2025 comparability — see below):** this is the **first year** DORA split the traditional "four keys" into two separate factors:
  - **Software delivery throughput** = change lead time + deployment frequency + **failed deployment recovery time** (i.e., time-to-restore is in the *throughput* factor this year)
  - **Software delivery stability** = change failure rate + **rework rate** (rework rate is a **brand-new survey item introduced in 2024**: "approximately how many deployments in the last six months were not planned but were performed to address a user-facing bug")
  - The report's own rationale: change failure rate no longer factored cleanly with the other three "four keys" metrics; the team hypothesised change-failure-rate was really a proxy for rework, tested it with the new rework-rate item, and confirmed the hypothesis, producing the two-factor split.

**Other AI-adoption outcome associations (self-reported; all "per 25% increase in AI adoption"):**
- Individual success/well-being (Figure 7): Flow **+2.6%**, Job satisfaction **+2.2%**, Productivity **+2.1%** (text confirms explicitly: "Productivity...is likely to increase by approximately 2.1%"), Time doing toilsome work **+0.4%** (near-null), Burnout **−0.6%** (small reduction, i.e. beneficial), Time doing valuable work **−2.6%** (the "vacuum hypothesis" finding — AI adoption associated with *less* self-reported time on valuable work, an unexpected/negative-leaning result the report itself calls "curious").
- Development workflow (Figure 9): Documentation quality **+7.5%**, Code quality **+3.4%**, Code review speed **+3.1%**, Approval speed **+1.3%**, XFN coordination **+0.1%** (near-null), Tech debt **−0.8%** (small reduction, beneficial), Code complexity **−1.8%** (reduction, beneficial).
- Organisational/team/product performance (Figure 11): Organisational performance **+2.3%**, Team performance **+1.4%**, Product performance **+0.2%** ("does not seem to have an obvious association with AI adoption" — treat as a **null**, recorded per protocol).
- **No burnout/friction null discussed at the individual level as in 2025** — 2024 actually reports a small *beneficial* direction for burnout (−0.6%), which is worth cross-referencing against 2025's explicit "no relationship with burnout" null.

**Perceived/self-reported productivity (separate from the regression — descriptive survey items):**
- **75%** of respondents reported positive productivity gains from AI "in the three months preceding our survey" (fielded early 2024); >1/3 described the increase as "moderate" (25%) or "extreme" (10%); fewer than 10% reported any decrease. [self-reported, perception — same descriptive-item pattern as 2025's ">80% report increased productivity"]
- Trust in AI-generated code: 87.9% report *some* level of trust, but 39.2% report "little" (27.3%) or "no" (11.9%) trust — the report calls this combination "surprising" given high adoption and positive perceived productivity.
- 67% report AI improved their ability to write code "to any degree"; only 5% report any inhibition.
- Individual AI adoption: 75.9% rely on AI for at least one daily task; org-level: 81% report their organisation has shifted priorities toward more AI incorporation.
- Task-level reliance (survey item, mappable to taxonomy): code writing 74.9% [T3/T4], summarising information 71.2%, code explanation 62.2%, code optimisation 61.3%, documentation 59.6% [T10], test writing 56.1% [T6], debugging 54.6% [T8], data analysis 48.9%, code review 45% [T7], security analysis 44.7% [T8/T11], language migration/codebase modernisation not separately percentaged in the extracted text but listed as survey tasks [T5].
- Interaction surfaces: chatbots 78.2%, external web interfaces 73.9%, IDE-embedded 72.9%, internal web interfaces 58.1%, CI/CD-embedded 50.2%.

**Report's own interpretation and caveats:**
- Explicit surprise at the throughput/stability finding: *"Contrary to our expectations, our findings indicate that AI adoption is negatively impacting software delivery performance."* The report notes this is counter to its own historical finding that better documentation, code quality, review speed, approval speed, and lower complexity (all of which AI improves per Figure 9) normally *predict* better delivery outcomes — AI breaks that usual chain.
- Working hypothesis offered by the report itself (not confirmed, explicitly labelled speculative): the **"small batch size" hypothesis** — AI lets people produce more code per unit time, changelists grow, and DORA's prior research has "consistently shown that larger changes are slower and more prone to creating instability." Report explicitly flags this as one of several possible explanations, not a proven mechanism.
- Section headline: **"AI is hurting delivery performance"** (verbatim chapter subheading) — this is the report's own framing, not a secondary paraphrase.
- Closing caveat for the whole AI chapter: *"AI does not appear to be a panacea."* Also explicitly states throughput and stability have been showing signs of independence from one another "for the past few years" (i.e., this is not a purely AI-driven phenomenon; the two-factor split reflects a multi-year trend the team was already tracking before AI became the focal capability).
- Methodology chapter is candid about correlational limits: causal-inference framing is via DAGs/Bayesian adjustment to *approximate* an experiment from observational survey data, not a substitute for one; the "ice cream causes shark attacks" confound analogy is used explicitly to caution readers.

## What this can and cannot support

Can support (Tier B, self-report caveats apply): a directional, industry-survey-level association between rising AI adoption and reduced self-reported software delivery throughput and (more strongly) reduced self-reported software delivery stability, alongside self-reported gains in individual productivity/flow/job-satisfaction and several development-workflow measures (documentation quality, code quality, review speed) — as of early-2024, autocomplete/chat-era tool usage. Cannot support: any measured/telemetry effect size, any causal claim (the report itself only claims correlational "comparisons," consistent with 2025's later, more explicit disclaimer), or any claim about agentic-era (E3) tooling — no agent-mode concept exists in this survey wave.

2. **Sign/framing correction:** the seed brief in this task ("−1.5%/−7.2%-per-25%-adoption") is directionally correct for throughput but the 2024 report's own construct for the second figure is **"delivery stability" falling by −7.2%**, not "instability" rising by +7.2%. Treat "instability +7.2%" (as used in dora-2025.md's paraphrase of this finding) as a valid but inverted-construct restatement, not the primary source's own wording — future citations of this figure should default to the 2024 report's own framing ("stability, −7.2%") and flag the inversion when quoting secondary paraphrases.
3. **Era tag stronger than seed:** PROTOCOL §6 tags DORA 2024 "[E2; trendline anchor]". Verified reading supports this but with a sharper edge: the 2024 instrument has **zero agent-mode framing** at all (contrast with 2025's explicit but minority agent-mode questions) — this wave is E1/E2 only, with no E3 component whatsoever, stronger than the "mixed, E1/E2-weighted" call already recorded for 2025.
4. **n is smaller than might be assumed:** this wave's n ≈ 3,000 (not "nearly 5,000" as in 2025) — worth noting for anyone assuming comparable sample sizes across waves.

## 2024↔2025 comparability notes (for the contradiction record)

These notes are provided to support later adjudication of the throughput-flip contradiction already logged in `synthesis/contradictions.md` — no edits made to that file per task constraints.

1. **Same self-report basis both years.** Both 2024 and 2025 measure throughput and stability via respondent self-report, not telemetry. The flip (2024: throughput −1.5%; 2025: throughput turns positive per dora-2025.md's "turned from negative to positive") is a **change in reported perception/association**, not a change from unreliable to reliable measurement — the RQ2 self-report-vs-measured gap question is not resolved by comparing these two waves to each other.
2. **Construct definition changed within the 2024 wave itself, one year prior to the flip.** 2024 introduced the throughput/stability two-factor split *and* introduced the new "rework rate" item into the stability factor in the same report that produced the seed anchor figures. This means the −1.5%/−7.2% anchor figures already reflect a **freshly redefined** stability metric (change failure rate + newly-added rework rate) rather than a metric with multi-year continuity — any comparison to pre-2024 DORA waves must account for this discontinuity, and any comparison forward to 2025 must confirm whether 2025 kept the same two-factor construction (dora-2025.md's extraction does not record whether 2025 kept identical throughput/stability item composition — flagged as an open question for whoever adjudicates the contradiction).
3. **Sample size and fielding precision both changed.** 2024: n≈3,000, "early 2024" (imprecise). 2025: n=4,867, 13 Jun–21 Jul 2025 (precise). A larger, more precisely-fielded sample in 2025 is itself a plausible partial explanator for the flip, independent of any real change in AI's effect.
4. **Modality composition likely shifted between waves**, though neither report allows a clean isolation of this: 2024 has no agent-mode item at all; 2025 explicitly asks about agent mode (61% "never") — if agentic tooling adoption grew over 2024→2025 even to a minority-use level, the 2025 "adoption" factor contains some E3 signal that the 2024 factor structurally could not. This is a candidate mechanism for the flip that neither report can directly test, since both bundle all modalities into a single undifferentiated "AI adoption" factor.
5. **RedMonk's independent critique (Rachel Stephens, cited already in dora-2025.md's follow-up leads) explicitly names the metric-restructuring issue** — "notes the 2024 metric restructuring (Time to Restore moved to throughput; Rework Rate added to instability) complicates year-on-year comparison" — this record confirms that critique against the 2024 primary text directly: **Time to Restore (failed deployment recovery time) is indeed in the throughput factor, and rework rate is indeed a new item added to the stability factor, both as of 2024.** This is a load-bearing methodological complication for anyone treating the −1.5%/+7.2%→(2025 flip) comparison as apples-to-apples.
6. **Net assessment for whoever adjudicates the contradiction:** the "flip" cannot currently be cleanly attributed to (a) a real change in AI's effect on delivery performance, (b) a change in survey methodology/construct definition, or (c) a change in the composition of "AI adoption" itself (modality mix) — all three are plausible and none can be ruled out from the two primary texts alone. This should be stated as a limitation in `contradictions.md` rather than resolved.

## Recency check notes

Report is now well over 12 months old (published Oct 2024; today is 2026-07-06), so a supersession check was performed per protocol rule 8. **Result: not retracted, but explicitly superseded/extended by DORA 2025** (already extracted in sources/dora-2025.md), which reports a directional flip on the throughput half of this finding while the instability finding persists. The 2024 report remains citable as the primary historical anchor and as evidence about pre-agentic-era measurement methodology (per PROTOCOL §2 era-transfer rule 2(b): "evidence about measurement methodology... still valid"), but any exec-facing claim about *current* AI/throughput association must lead with 2025 (or later) data and cite 2024 only as the historical baseline half of the trendline. No evidence found that Google/DORA has withdrawn or corrected the 2024 report's headline AI findings; the "v.2024.3" versioning appears to reflect minor post-publication edits (typical DORA practice, mirrored by 2025's "v2025.2"), not a retraction.
