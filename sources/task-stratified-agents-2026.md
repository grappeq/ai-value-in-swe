## Tier, era, independence

- **Tier: B.** This is large-N (7,156 PRs) observational mining of PR-acceptance outcomes with no experimental manipulation, no adopters-vs-non-adopters counterfactual, and no difference-in-differences or matched-control design — it is a stratified descriptive/inferential comparison (Chi-square tests, phi effect sizes, linear/LOESS trend fitting) across agents and task categories within a single non-randomised dataset. This is squarely PROTOCOL §3's "structured observational: large-scale telemetry without randomisation" — Tier B, not A (no counterfactual design of the kind that earns the CMU-lab papers their A grade) and not merely C (this is peer-reviewed, methodologically disciplined large-N mining, not a practitioner narrative).
- **Era: E3, confirmed by tool identity, not fully by modality-per-task.** All five studied agents (OpenAI Codex, GitHub Copilot [agent mode, not classic autocomplete — the AIDev dataset attributes only agent-authored PRs], Devin, Cursor, Claude Code) are genuinely agentic, multi-file, PR-authoring tools, consistent with the source's own framing of "autonomous AI teammates... completing complex development activities with minimal human supervision." No E1/E2 contamination risk of the kind flagged for `.cursorrules`-based Cursor detection in `cursor-velocity-quality-2025`, because AIDev's PR attribution is behavioural (agent-authored commit/PR), not a proxy config file.
- **Independence:** Academic, no vendor affiliation, ethics-board-approved, no funding/COI statement located in the fetched text (may exist in an acknowledgments section not captured, but none found). **AIDev-provenance flag applies** (see overlap section above) — independent authorship, shared dataset lineage with `agents-vs-ides-2026` and with the Rahman/Rabbi/Zibran sibling.

## Method & population

- **Dataset:** AIDev (Li, Zhang & Hassan 2025; HuggingFace + arXiv:2602.09185/2602.15003), AIDev-POP subset (repos with ≥100 GitHub stars), raw 33,596 PRs. Filtered to **7,156 PRs** by: (1) closed PRs only, (2) permissive licence (MIT or Apache-2.0), (3) at least one review or comment from someone other than the PR's creator before closure ("ensuring meaningful evaluation" — this is a **selection filter that excludes unreviewed/rubber-stamped PRs**, which the authors do not flag as a limitation on external validity but which likely deflates the overall acceptance rate relative to an unfiltered population — see Cross-ref discrepancy note above for why this may explain why this paper's 69.3% overall rate sits well below the 83.8%-merge-rate figure queued for `arXiv:2509.14745`).
- **Agents (n PRs, observation window, PRs/week, overall acceptance — Table 1, reconciled):**

  | Agent | Dataset-coverage start | PRs | Active weeks | PRs/week | Overall acceptance |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | Devin | 24 Dec 2024 | 2,252 | 32 | 70.4 | 61.6% |
  | OpenAI Codex | 16 May 2025 | 2,002 | 12 | 166.8 | 77.9% |
  | GitHub Copilot | 19 May 2025 | 2,194 | 11 | 199.5 | 68.0% |
  | Cursor | 1 May 2025 | 569 | 13 | 43.8 | 74.5% |
  | Claude Code | 24 Feb 2025 | 139 | 19 | 7.3 | 71.9% |
  | **Total** | — | **7,156** | 87 (union) | — | **69.3%** |

  "Start" reflects first appearance in the filtered dataset, not the tool's official release date. Observation windows are **not aligned** across agents (11–32 active weeks) — the authors explicitly flag this ("agents cannot be compared across equivalent maturity periods, and observed patterns may reflect deployment contexts rather than intrinsic capabilities") and partially address it with an 11-week common-window sensitivity check (see Nulls/negatives below).
- **Acceptance rate defined as:** proportion of closed PRs with a non-null `merged_at` timestamp. Because only closed PRs are retained, acceptance + rejection = 100% by construction (no "still open" category in this analysis).
- **Task-type taxonomy (12 categories, LLM-classified by the AIDev dataset itself, not by these authors):** feat, fix, docs, chore, style, ci, build, refactor, test, perf, revert, other. Three categories (style, revert, other) excluded from the Chi-square comparisons "because multiple agents have zero observations for these categories" in the filtered 7,156-PR sample — leaving 9 task types × 10 agent-pairs = 64 stratified tests, Bonferroni-corrected to α≈0.00078; 17 of 64 used Fisher's exact test (expected cell counts <5).
- **Statistics:** linear regression (weekly acceptance-rate trend, R² reported) + LOESS (span 0.5) for RQ1 (temporal); task-level mean acceptance rate + review-frequency correlation for RQ2; pairwise Chi-square/Fisher's exact + phi coefficient effect size for RQ3.
- **Explicit observational framing (authors' own words):** *"we emphasize that this study is observational: we document patterns without claiming causality, acknowledging potential confounders such as repository concentration, user population dynamics, model updates, and shifts in task distributions."*

## Key findings

**RQ1 — temporal trends (all measured, not self-reported):**
- Devin: only agent with a statistically consistent positive trend, **+0.77 percentage points/week over 32 weeks** (R²=0.34), rising from ≈60% to ≈80% acceptance; LOESS suggests three phases (initial plateau, rise, re-stabilisation). Authors explicitly caveat: "we cannot isolate underlying causal mechanisms" (model updates vs. user learning vs. task-mix shift all confounded).
- OpenAI Codex and GitHub Copilot: **plateau/no significant trend** — consistently high acceptance from their first observed week (a genuine **null** for the "agents improve with more deployment time" hypothesis for 2 of 5 agents).
- Cursor and Claude Code: not separately characterised by a trend statistic in the fetched text beyond "remain largely stable."

**RQ2 — factors associated with performance (measured):**
- **Task type is the dominant factor**, per the paper's own framing: a **29-percentage-point gap** between the highest (chore, 84.0%) and lowest-non-zero (perf, 55.4%) task-level mean acceptance rates — exceeding typical inter-agent variance for most task types. Among the two highest-volume categories: docs 82.1% vs. feat 66.1%, a **16pp gap**.
- Review frequency varies by agent and correlates (not causally) with acceptance: GitHub Copilot PRs receive 4.94 reviews/PR vs. OpenAI Codex's 1.39 reviews/PR, coinciding with Copilot's lower acceptance (68.0% vs. 77.9%) — authors explicitly decline the causal reading: "this does not imply a causal link: varying review counts may reflect confounders such as task complexity or repository reviewing policies."

**RQ3 — task-stratified agent comparison (measured):**
- **No single agent wins everywhere.** OpenAI Codex has the broadest, most consistent strength: 59.6%–88.6% across all 9 tested task categories, leading in fix (83.0%) and refactor (74.3%). But Claude Code leads docs (92.3%, n small — 139 PRs total company-wide, "interpret with caution") and feat (72.6%); Cursor excels in test (77.8%, "also with few samples") and, per the abstract and the practitioner-implications paragraph, fix (80.4%) — both figures for Cursor are given in different sections without being flagged as contradictory; they describe different task categories where Cursor leads, not a discrepancy.
- **Devin is the clear laggard specifically on fix tasks: 45.6%**, well below Codex (83.0%), Copilot, and Cursor (80.4%) on the same task type — despite Devin's positive overall temporal trend (RQ1) and its highest-n overall PR count (2,252). This is the paper's sharpest single agent-specific weak spot.
- Largest single inter-agent gap on any task: **test tasks, 44.4pp** (Cursor 77.8% vs. Claude Code 33.3%) — the paper reads this as evidence that "more complex tasks exhibit greater [inter-agent] differences," though "test" being harder than "docs" is an interpretive claim, not independently validated against a task-difficulty instrument.
- **Statistical significance, corrected:** of 64 pairwise Chi-square/Fisher tests (10 agent pairs × 9 remaining task types), Bonferroni-adjusted α≈0.00078, **only 6 are significant** — a large majority (58/64) show no statistically distinguishable difference once corrected for multiple comparisons, itself a notable null. Of the 6 significant results, **5 involve `fix`** and 1 involves `feat`:

  | Comparison | Task | p-value | Winner | Effect size (φ) |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Codex vs Devin | fix | <0.0007 | Codex | 0.39 (medium) |
  | Copilot vs Devin | fix | <0.0007 | Copilot | 0.20 (small) |
  | Devin vs Cursor | fix | <0.0007 | Cursor | 0.28 (small) |
  | Codex vs Copilot | fix | <0.0007 | Codex | 0.19 (small) |
  | Codex vs Devin | feat | <0.0007 | Codex | 0.11 (small) |
  | Cursor vs Copilot | fix | <0.0007 | Cursor | 0.11 (small) |

  Every significant Devin comparison on `fix` shows Devin losing — consistent, convergent evidence (not just the single 45.6% headline number) that Devin specifically underperforms at bug-fixing relative to the other four agents in this sample.

**Confounders the authors explicitly flag (directly answering the task brief's brief):**
1. **Task assignment is not randomised across agents** — Table 3 (task-mix % of each agent's own PR portfolio): GitHub Copilot handles proportionally more `fix` (41.6%) than any other agent; Claude Code is heavily `feat`-weighted (52.5%); Devin `feat` 41.5%; Codex and Cursor are more evenly spread. The paper's own framing: *"an agent that handles mostly documentation tasks... may appear superior to one that handles complex feature implementations, even if the latter has stronger underlying capabilities"* — this is exactly why they stratify, but stratification controls for task-type composition, not for **within-task** difficulty or repository selection (see point 3).
2. **Repository-level clustering is not modelled** in the Chi-square tests — "if certain repositories systematically accept or reject PRs regardless of quality, this could inflate statistical significance"; authors recommend future mixed-effects models, meaning the reported p-values likely understate true uncertainty.
3. **Observation windows are unaligned and vary 11–32 weeks**, addressed only partially via an 11-week common-window sensitivity check (see below) — full-window and common-window rankings differ somewhat, so the headline overall-acceptance ranking is window-dependent.
4. **Task-mix drifts over time within agents**, confounding the temporal-trend analysis: Devin and Copilot shifted toward `feat` over the study period (+9.8pp, +8.1pp), Codex shifted away from `feat` (−5.7pp), Claude Code shows the largest shift (+18.4pp feat, −23.0pp docs). The authors note this cuts **both ways** for interpreting Devin's positive trend: if Devin took on progressively harder (`feat`) work over time and still improved, "the observed improvement may understate actual capability gains" — but equally, the trend could partly *be* the task-mix shift rather than genuine capability improvement, and the paper does not fully disentangle these.
5. **Sample-size asymmetry**: Claude Code (139 PRs total) is repeatedly flagged as too sparse for confident per-task claims; its 92.3% docs and 72.6% feat headline numbers carry this caveat explicitly in the text.
6. **What merge rate can and cannot support (authors' own words, echoing He et al. 2026):** *"PR acceptance does not equal code quality (construct validity), as merged PRs may contain bugs."* Merge/acceptance rate is an **acceptance proxy** — it tells you whether reviewers were satisfied enough to merge, filtered further here by requiring at least one independent review — but it carries **no information on defect rates, rework, post-merge reverts as a quality signal** (revert is treated only as a task-type label, not tracked as a downstream outcome of *other* PRs), or long-run maintainability. The paper's own closing recommendation is that acceptance rate needs pairing with "static analysis, complexity, maintenance burden" metrics (citing He et al. 2026 directly) — i.e. this source itself argues against over-weighting its own headline metric.
7. **External validity:** only repos with ≥100 GitHub stars, permissive licences, ≥1 independent review, 8 months of coverage — explicitly "limiting generalization to smaller projects" and, by implication, to proprietary/closed-source codebases entirely absent from AIDev.
8. **No causal claims anywhere** — "we cannot establish causality (internal validity)."

**Quality signals beyond merge rate:** the only one reported is **review frequency (reviews/PR)**, used as a covariate correlated with acceptance (Copilot 4.94 vs. Codex 1.39 reviews/PR), not as an outcome in its own right. No review-round count, no post-merge fix/revert-as-downstream-outcome tracking, no defect data. This paper does not measure review rounds, post-merge fixes, or any quality signal beyond acceptance + review-count-as-covariate.

**Nulls/negatives (collected per CLAUDE.md rule 3):**
- 4/5 agents (all but Devin) show no significant temporal trend in acceptance rate — a null for "agents improve over deployment time" outside Devin.
- 58 of 64 Bonferroni-corrected pairwise agent comparisons are not statistically significant — the large majority of task-stratified agent-vs-agent differences do not survive multiple-comparison correction.
- `other` task category: 0.0% mean acceptance across all 4 observation-cells (every PR in this residual bucket was rejected) — but n=4 cells only, essentially noise-level and explicitly excluded from the formal statistical tests; record as a curiosity, not a supported finding.
- `revert`: also excluded from formal tests (n=4 cells; multiple agents have zero PRs in this category).
- **Sensitivity check (aligned 11-week window, 19 May–30 Jul 2025, all five agents active):** overall ranking changes somewhat from the full-window ranking — Codex 79.9%, Cursor 74.4%, Claude Code 72.6% (n=113 in this window), Devin 68.0%, Copilot 68.0%. Devin's full-window advantage over Copilot (61.6% vs. 68.0%, i.e. Devin actually *behind* in the full window) inverts to a tie in the aligned window — the ranking is not fully robust to the choice of comparison window, itself a limitation worth flagging rather than picking one window as "the" answer.
- GMM-style causal mechanism claims (of the kind `cursor-velocity-quality-2025` makes) are **not attempted** here at all — this paper is purely descriptive/correlational, a notch below its CMU-lab-authored sibling sources in causal ambition, consistent with its Tier B (not A) grade.

## Limitations (authors' own, near-verbatim)

- Cannot establish causality — observed trends could reflect model updates, user learning, or task-distribution shifts, all confounded.
- External validity limited to ≥100-star, permissively-licensed, ≥1-independently-reviewed OSS repos over an 8-month window; "limiting generalization to smaller projects" (and, by omission, to proprietary codebases).
- Construct validity: "PR acceptance does not equal code quality... merged PRs may contain bugs" (citing Perry et al. 2023 on AI-code security).
- Repository-level clustering not modelled in the Chi-square tests — could inflate significance if some repos systematically accept/reject regardless of quality.
- Observation windows vary 11–32 active weeks and some agents show discontinuous activity — "requiring cautious interpretation of observed trends."
- Sample-size range 139–2,252 PRs across agents, with Claude Code's 139 "limiting reliability."

## What this can and cannot support

**Can support (Tier B, E3, AIDev-POP-bounded, review-filtered-OSS-bounded):** that within this sample, task type produces a larger acceptance-rate spread (29pp, chore vs. perf) than typical inter-agent variance within a task type — direct, well-evidenced support for RQ1/RQ3's premise that task type, not just tool choice, is a first-order driver of AI-coding outcomes; that no single studied agent dominates across all task types, with agent-specific relative strengths and weaknesses (Codex broadly strong and consistent; Devin specifically weak at `fix`; Claude Code and Cursor each lead in narrower task niches, on thinner samples); that acceptance-rate differences between agents are far more often statistically indistinguishable than distinguishable once corrected for multiple comparisons (58/64 null); that raw/task-unstratified agent rankings can be materially confounded by each agent's own task-type mix (Table 3 heterogeneity is large and non-random); and that PR acceptance/merge rate is explicitly, by the authors' own argument, an acceptance proxy that cannot by itself support any code-quality, defect-rate, or long-run-maintainability claim.

**Cannot support:** any causal attribution of the Devin temporal-improvement trend to model updates, user learning, or genuine capability growth (explicitly undetermined, and possibly confounded by task-mix drift in either direction); any claim about codebase maturity/complexity effects (repo age/size is not measured or reported as a moderator in this paper — see Cross-ref below on why this is a distinct question from "task type"); any generalisation beyond ≥100-star, permissively-licensed, independently-reviewed OSS repositories over an 8-month 2025 window (proprietary/enterprise codebases, smaller repos, and non-permissive-licence projects are entirely out of scope); any quality, defect, rework, or post-merge-outcome claim (no such data collected); and any claim that the studied "GitHub Copilot" entries reflect Copilot's autocomplete/chat modality rather than its agentic PR-authoring mode — the paper's scope is explicitly the latter throughout, consistent with the AIDev dataset's agent-authored-PR attribution method.

## Cross-reference assessment (for orchestrator — not written to contradictions.md)

**(a) vs. `arXiv:2509.14745`'s 83.8% agentic-PR merge rate (queued, not yet extracted) — plausible reconciliation, not yet a contradiction.** This paper's overall acceptance (69.3%, 7,156 PRs) sits well below 83.8%. The most likely explanation, based on this paper's own stated filtering: this paper requires **at least one independent review/comment before closure** — a filter that selects for PRs that received human scrutiny, which plausibly *lowers* the measured acceptance rate relative to a broader population that includes unreviewed/nearly-automatic merges (several practitioner sources already in this evidence base, e.g. `cloudflare-2026-ai-review` and `he-2026-2x-mandate`'s own review-coverage decline, describe a substantial and growing share of PRs bypassing substantive human review entirely). If `arXiv:2509.14745`'s 83.8% figure is computed over an unfiltered or less-reviewed population, the two numbers may not be in tension at all — they would be measuring acceptance under different review-intensity regimes, not disagreeing about the same construct. **Recommend the orchestrator check `arXiv:2509.14745`'s own filtering criteria before treating this as either confirmation or contradiction; flag as an open reconciliation item, not a resolved cross-ref.**

**(b) vs. `agents-vs-ides-2026` (CMU/Vasilescu lab, full AIDev corpus) — same provenance hub, complementary not overlapping analysis.** `agents-vs-ides-2026` studies *prior AI-IDE exposure as a moderator of agent-adoption velocity/quality trajectories* over time (a different research question, the IDE-first-vs-agent-first cohort split); this paper studies *task-type as a moderator of cross-sectional acceptance rate*. They do not report a shared metric that could directly confirm or contradict each other, and — per the overlap verdict above — any apparent convergence should not be read as independent triangulation, since both trace to AIDev-family data. No numeric overlap was found to compare directly.

**(c) vs. PROTOCOL §5 tension 2 (greenfield/brownfield split) — does NOT provide direct support, despite superficial fit; this is the single most important cross-ref finding of this extraction.** The triage note flagged this paper's task stratification as mapping onto T1–T11 and, by extension, as potentially informative for the greenfield/brownfield tension. Having read the full text: **this paper's "task type" axis (feat/fix/docs/chore/etc.) categorises the *kind of change*, not the *codebase context* (age, size, complexity, maturity) that tension 2 is actually about.** Repository age/size is used nowhere as a reported moderator (identical gap to the one flagged in `cursor-velocity-quality-2025`'s record, where repo age is a *matching covariate* only, never an analysed heterogeneity cut). All 7,156 PRs come from established, ≥100-star OSS repositories — i.e. the entire sample sits on one side of a coarse maturity threshold already, with no within-sample brownfield/greenfield contrast constructed or reported. Superficially, one could note that `feat` (arguably the most context-hungry, brownfield-relevant category) shows a *below-average* acceptance rate (66.1%, below the 69.3% overall mean) while `docs`/`chore` (low-context, well-scoped) score highest (82–84%) — this is *thematically* consonant with the broader "AI does better on well-scoped, low-implicit-context work" narrative that underlies tension 2, but it is a different construct (task type) being used as a loose proxy, not a genuine repo-maturity moderator analysis, and should not be cited as task-level evidence for the greenfield/brownfield split. **Recommend the synthesis memo not cite this paper for tension 2**, or cite it only to note that task-type and codebase-maturity are analytically distinct axes that this literature sometimes conflates.
