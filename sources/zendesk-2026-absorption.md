## The "absorption capacity" concept — what exactly

Extracted precisely, quote-checked across passes:

- **Core definition:** absorption capacity is "what turns a diff into dependable value" — encompassing "deciding what to build, fitting it into the system, proving it behaves, and understanding the value it delivers."
- **Central contrast (the post's thesis in one line):** "Generative AI accelerates the production of *implementation*. It does not, by itself, accelerate the production of *clarity*."
- **Components named as part of absorption capacity** (these are the elements the post treats as the actual constraint once code-writing is cheap):
  - **Problem framing / clarity of what to build** — deciding what a change should do before writing it
  - **Review capacity** — explicit: "pull requests arrive faster than human-driven review can stay meaningful"; review is framed as the verification gate asking "is this safe to merge and ship?"
  - **Verification confidence** — CI, static analysis, security checks, observability, staged rollouts (named as the mechanisms that need strengthening, not as things Zendesk has already strengthened — see below)
  - **Architectural coherence** — "architecture and conventions as scaffolding for AI," with the claim that "AI will scale whatever structures you already have" (i.e. good or bad architecture gets amplified, not fixed, by AI)
  - **Product-decision alignment / shared understanding across the codebase** — framed as part of "clarity," not separately elaborated
- **Historical framing device:** the post uses Liebig's law of the minimum (a crop's yield is capped by its scarcest nutrient, not its most abundant one) and the Haber–Bosch nitrogen-fixation and Toyota Production System examples as analogies for "removing one constraint reveals the next one" — argumentative colour, not data about software delivery.

This is conceptually adjacent to, but independently articulated from, Agoda's white/black/grey-box taxonomy: both use a constraint-relocation logic (Brooks/Amdahl-style for Agoda; Liebig's-law-style for Zendesk), and both land on review/verification and specification/problem-framing as the new bottleneck. Zendesk's version is somewhat more granular in naming discrete sub-components (review capacity, verification confidence, architectural coherence, product alignment) rather than Agoda's three global modes.

## What Zendesk changed operationally — and the critical distinction: prescriptive, not descriptive

**Zendesk states no operational change it has made.** The post's final section, "How we increase absorption capacity," despite the first-person plural title, is written **entirely in prescriptive language** ("should," "can't," "treat," "make... part of the work") describing what organisations in general ought to do, not what Zendesk itself has done. Verified explicitly across two independent fetch passes: **zero "we do X" statements** describing Zendesk's own practice appear anywhere in the piece.

The four prescribed practices (recommendations, not reported changes):
1. **Make problem framing part of the work, not a preamble** — treat specification/clarity as a first-class engineering task rather than a throwaway step before "real" work begins
2. **Lower the cost of confidence** — invest in verification mechanisms (the post names CI, static analysis, security checks, observability, staged rollouts as the toolkit) so that checking correctness is cheap enough to keep pace with generation
3. **Treat architecture and conventions as scaffolding for AI** — codify architectural decisions as explicit, machine-legible constraints (Architecture Decision Records plus CI guardrails are named as mechanisms) rather than leaving them as tacit knowledge, on the logic that "AI will scale whatever structures you already have"
4. **Measure throughput instead of output** — a warning against optimising for volume-of-code metrics; measure whether value reaches production reliably, not how much code was generated

No adoption data, no before/after figures, and no statement of which (if any) of these four practices Zendesk has actually implemented, piloted, or measured the effect of. This is a materially important distinction from how the InfoQ headline ("Zendesk Says...") could be misread — Zendesk's engineering blog states a *framework*, not a *rollout report*.
