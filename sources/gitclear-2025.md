## Tier & era & independence

- **Tier: B, with a mandatory COI flag — flag strengthened by the full 2026 text.** PROTOCOL §3 places "multi-company code-quality analyses" (large-scale telemetry without randomisation) in Tier B, and this is exactly that: ~1bn lines classified, 211M–623M meaningful changes analysed, mixed private-corporate and open-source population. However, GitClear **sells the code-analysis product that produces every metric in the report**, and the full 2026 PDF confirms this is now a **two-vendor** commercial exercise: the title page reads "Sponsored by GitClear and GitKraken Research", and Appendix A4 ("Contact & Participation") explicitly pitches paid GitClear/GitKraken measurement products against every signal in the report, closing with "Email hello@gitclear.com while mentioning this research to receive a subscription discount code." The Diff Delta / operation-classification method remains proprietary and not independently auditable. Treat as **Tier B (COI-flagged, vendor-published, now dual-vendor)**: carries claims with caveats, never an exec-brief claim without independent corroboration. Graded B not D because the underlying data is genuine multi-company telemetry at scale, not opinion or survey marketing — but the COI flag is non-negotiable and, if anything, firmer than at last verification.
- **Era: trendline spanning E1→E3; findings remain era-aggregate, not modality-segmented — now confirmed explicitly by the authors themselves.** GitClear telemetry does not identify which lines were AI-authored (beyond a coarse per-commit "measurable AI assistance" flag, new in 2026 — see below) or which tool modality produced them; every finding is a year-over-year population trend correlated with the era of AI adoption. 2025 edition (data Jan 2020–Dec 2024): **E1/E2** — Copilot-dominant, autocomplete-era; baseline/trendline only. 2026 edition (data 2023–H1 2026): **E2/E3 blend with E3-era endpoints, still unmeasured as to modality mix** — and the PDF's own Appendix A6 ("A Note on Per-Model Comparisons") states this directly: *"We are not yet prepared to make per-model claims with the confidence this audience deserves... not yet large or evenly distributed enough to support head-to-head conclusions... We would treat any pattern visible [in the live per-model dashboard] today as a hypothesis to be confirmed: not yet a robust finding."* The 2026 edition also introduces a coarse adoption metric — **24.1% of 2026 commits show "measurable AI assistance"** (p.6), which the report itself flags as likely an under-count ("the lowest defensible number you will find reported", R9, vs. 25–46% claimed elsewhere) — but this is a binary AI/no-AI split, not an autocomplete-vs-chat-vs-agentic split. **Net effect on E3-relevance: still provisional, now provisional by the authors' own explicit admission rather than by absence of evidence.** The 2025–2026 endpoints plausibly contain a growing share of agentic-tool output given the calendar, but the report supplies no way to isolate or verify that.
- **Independence flags:** vendor-published (COI, now dual-vendor per above); population is GitClear's own customer base (opted into "Industry Stats") plus "corporate open repos (e.g., Google, Microsoft, Apple)" — note this naming set differs from the 2025 edition's named examples (Chromium, React, VS Code, Kubernetes, Postgres, cpython; customers NextGen Health, Bank of Georgia), so exact population overlap between editions is unconfirmed; selection is "orgs that buy code-analytics tooling + megaprojects", not a random sample; dataset composition shifts year to year (the 2025 report admits "our line counts this year were not identical to the numbers that our database produced at the beginning of 2024"). **New finding from the 2026 PDF's own appendix sample-size table (A1, p.18):** the record count underlying every one of the eight signals jumps by a near-uniform **~3.3×–3.8× between 2024 and 2025** (e.g. copy/paste 16.7M→60.7M records, duplication 4.08M→15.5M, obfuscation 1.19M→3.97M, churn 10.2M→35.3M, function connectivity 1.28M→4.58M, long-term update 12.2M→46.1M) — both 2024 and 2025 are presented as complete years, so this is not the partial-year effect the report uses to caveat 2026 YTD figures. This uniform, simultaneous jump across every unrelated signal is much more consistent with a large block of new customers/repos being onboarded to GitClear's dataset during 2025 than with organic behavioural change, and it is **not discussed or controlled for anywhere in the report** — a material, undisclosed confound for every year-over-year trend the report presents, not only churn (see the dedicated churn-contradiction note below). **No causal identification** otherwise either — no counterfactual, no AI-attribution of individual lines — so layoffs, team churn, and repo-mix drift remain unexcluded confounders on top of this newly-identified composition jump.

## Method & population/dataset

- **Measurement basis: measured (telemetry)** throughout — server-side classification of git commits. The only self-reported numbers quoted are secondary (Stack Overflow 2024: 63% of professional developers use AI, 14% plan to).
- **2025 edition dataset:** 211 million meaningful changed lines, Jan 2020–Dec 2024, from a ~10,000-repo sample; ~2/3 private corporations (e.g. NextGen Health, Bank of Georgia named as customers), ~1/3 open source (25 named repos + ~80 more). Roughly half of raw "lines changed" (GitHub-style counting) are excluded as no-op/auto-generated before analysis.
- **2026 edition dataset (confirmed from the full PDF):** 623 million analysed changes, **2023–H1 2026** — a narrower time window than the 2025 edition (which went back to Jan 2020). Population: "GitClear's database of customers (opted into Industry Stats) & corporate open repos (e.g., Google, Microsoft, Apple)" (p.4) — specific customers are not named in this edition (2025 named NextGen Health, Bank of Georgia; 2026 does not repeat these names or the 2025 OSS list). Per-signal population sizes are **not uniform**: each of the eight signals is computed only over the subset of changes where it is defined (e.g. function connectivity only within files containing functions/methods; churn only for customer repos, not the OSS subset) — exact per-signal record counts are given in Appendix A1 (see Independence flags above for the 2024→2025 composition-jump finding drawn from that table). **2026 figures throughout are year-to-date (through ~June 2026) on a smaller, in-progress sample** and are explicitly flagged by the authors as "a leading indicator rather than a closed annual figure" — this caveat is repeated three times in the report (main text, methodology notes, and table footnotes).
- **Operation definitions (2025 PDF, Appendix A0; confirmed unchanged in the 2026 PDF, Appendix A2):** every changed line is classified as one of seven operations — **Added** (new distinct lines), **Deleted** (removed, not re-added within two weeks), **Moved** (cut-and-pasted to a new file or function, content unchanged — "the signature of code reuse"/refactoring), **Updated** (existing line modified by roughly three words or less), **Find/Replaced** (same string substituted in 3+ locations), **Copy/Pasted** (identical line contents, excluding language keywords, committed to multiple files or functions **within a single commit**), **No-op** (excluded). **Churn** (not an operation): a line "authored, pushed to the git repo, and then reverted or substantially revised within the subsequent two weeks" — glossed as changes "either incomplete or erroneous" when first pushed. The 2026 PDF states explicitly: "This is the same classification framework used in prior GitClear AI research" — methodology continuity confirmed, not merely assumed.
- **Duplicate block detection (2025, Appendix A8; definition repeated in 2026 Appendix A5):** any region of 5+ contiguous repeated "substantive" lines (ignoring blank lines; a substantive line is non-keyword and non-purely-symbolic), Type-1 clones, present in more than one location within or across files; excludes test files, auto-generated/compiled files, and "key/value" languages (CSS, XML, templates) where duplication is expected. Pre-2024 years (2025 edition) backfilled by scanning the 1,000 largest commits per repo — a method the authors argue biases *toward* finding duplication in earlier years, i.e. against their own conclusion.
- **New metrics introduced in the 2026 edition (not present in the 2024/2025 editions):** **Obfuscation rate** (error-masking constructs — see Key Findings below for full definition), **Function Connectivity rate** (cross-file reuse signal), and **Throwaway percent** (1-week churn, a stricter sibling of the existing 2-week churn metric). The four carried-over metrics are Copy/Paste, Move/Refactor, Churn (2-week), and Duplication.
- SQL/ActiveRecord queries for the headline tables are printed in the 2025 appendix; the underlying line-level data and the Diff Delta classifier are not public in either edition. The 2026 PDF adds that live, continuously-updated per-model segment graphs are published at gitclear.com/industry_stats/ai_code_quality_signal_graphs, separate from the static report.

## Key findings

All measured (telemetry). Primary task-type tag **T5** (refactoring/tech debt — moved-lines and legacy-maintenance metrics are direct T5 measures); duplication/churn findings are cross-cutting quality-debt signals affecting **T4** (maintainability of mature codebases); none of the findings are per-task-type.

### 2025 edition (data 2020–2024; era E1/E2 baseline)

1. **Moved (refactored) lines collapsed:** 24.1% of changed lines (2020) → 20.5% (2022) → 15.8% (2023) → **9.5% (2024)**; −39.9% YoY 2023→2024. "2024 marked the first year GitClear has ever measured where the number of 'Copy/Pasted' lines exceeded the count of 'Moved' lines." [T5; measured]
2. **Copy/pasted lines rose:** 8.3% (2020) → 10.6% (2023) → **12.3% (2024)**; +17.1% YoY. (Appendix percentages differ by decimals from the body table: 8.86% → 12.32%; body table quoted here.) [T5/T4; measured]
3. **Duplicated blocks (5+ lines) surged:** percent of scanned commits containing a duplicate block: 0.70% (2020), 0.45% (2022), 1.80% (2023), **6.66% (2024)** — the landing-page "4x growth in code clones" is 1.80→6.66; the body also describes "an 8-fold increase in the frequency of code blocks with 5+ duplicated lines during 2024" (total blocks found 20,448 → 63,566 against a smaller rise in commits scanned) and "approximately 10x higher than two years prior". Record all three phrasings; they use different denominators. [T4; measured]
4. **Two-week churn rose:** 3.1% (2020) → 3.3% (2022) → 4.5% (2023) → **5.7% (2024)**; +26% YoY. Note: this **undershot** GitClear's own 2024 projection of 7.1% — their prior-year prediction that churn would double vs 2021 did not materialise (they acknowledge: "Thankfully, short-term churn has not yet reached 7%"). New-lines-only churn (Appendix A7, 45M lines): revised within 2 weeks 4.78% (2020) → 7.04% (2024); revised within a month 5.49% (2020) → 7.91% (2024) — i.e. "a 20–25% increase [vs 2021] in the percent of new lines that get revised within a month". (Secondary summaries citing "7.9% churn" are quoting this revised-within-a-month figure, not the headline churn series.) [T4; measured]
5. **Added code share grew:** 39.2% (2020) → **46.2% (2024)** of changed lines; find/replaced +16.7% YoY (credited partly to lint-autofixing IDEs like Cursor). [measured]
6. **Age of revised code shifted sharply newer:** lines revised within 2 weeks of authorship: ~60% (2020–2023) → **69.7% (2024)**; changes touching code over a year old fell (24.7% → 16.9% for <1 year-old bucket shrinking, 2+ year-old code at ~1%). Only ~20% of 2024 modified lines touched code older than a month (vs ~30% in 2020). [T5; measured]
7. **Defects — indirect only.** The 2025 edition, despite its title, contains **no direct defect measurement**. Its defect argument is: (a) its own 2024-edition prediction that Google DORA 2024 would show quality decline "came true" — DORA 2024 modelled a **7.2% decrease in delivery stability per 25% increase in AI adoption** (quoted from DORA, not GitClear data); (b) the code-clone literature (Mo/Zhang 2023: 57.1% of co-changed clones involved in bugs; Mondal 2017 and Wagner 2016: 17–18% propagated-bug/fault rates in clones) implies rising duplication ⇒ rising defects. Chain of inference, not measurement. [measured-by-others; do not cite as a GitClear defect finding]
8. **2025 projections (not measurements):** moved 3.1%, copy/paste 13.9%, churn 6.9% (Appendix A1 "2025" row). Their 2024 projections got direction right, magnitude wrong in both directions (moved fell faster than projected; churn rose slower).

1. **Refactoring line moves down 70%, but the baseline year is 2023, not 2022 as the executive summary's phrasing implies.** The exec-summary sentence reads "refactoring line moves are down 70%... vs 2022 levels", but the only calculation that actually produces −70% is 2023→2026 YTD (12.7%→3.8%; (12.7−3.8)/12.7 = 70.1%). Using the report's own separately-cited 2022 figure (21%, quoted in the body text on p.12 when comparing 2022's "2x refactor-over-redundant" ratio to 2026's "~5x redundant-over-refactor" ratio) would give a **−81.9%** drop, not −70%. This is an internal inconsistency in the PDF's own framing — the 2022 baseline is borrowed from the prior report vintage (2024 edition, R10) for a *different* comparison (the redundant:refactor ratio), then the "vs 2022" language appears to have been reused loosely in the executive summary for the headline delta, which the report's own tables (starting at 2023) do not support. Recorded here as a report-internal error, not a correction to GitClear's data. Separately: the 2025 PDF's "20.5%" for 2022 vs. this edition's "21%" for 2022 are consistent within rounding — the earlier "disagreement" flagged in this record's prior version is resolved. Copy/paste is now ~5x more prevalent than refactoring (15.7% vs 3.8%), versus a ~2x relationship in 2022 (9% copy/paste vs 20% moved, per the report's own comparison). [T5; measured]
2. **Within-commit copy/paste +41%**, 11.1% (2023) → **15.7% of changed lines (H1 2026)**. Confirmed exactly against Appendix A1. [T4/T5; measured]
3. **Code block duplication +81%:** 40.3 per million changed lines (2023) → 46.7 (2024) → 69.7 (2025) → **73.0 (YTD 2026)** — highest on record; confirmed exactly. Duplication is described as "the single signal most directly tied to defects in the literature" (p.16). [T4; measured]
4. **Cross-file function calls (Function Connectivity, a new metric) down 35% since 2023:** 342.7 → 312.3 → 254.1 → **223.2** per 1,000 changed lines. Confirmed exactly. Framed as evidence that new code is "less and less woven into the existing codebase... isolated in self-contained files." [T4/T5; measured]
5. **Long-term update percent down 74%:** 1.71% (2023) → 1.62% (2024) → 0.82% (2025) → **0.45–0.46% (YTD 2026)**. Confirmed (see rounding note §). Framed as legacy maintenance "increasingly not happening... [older strata] gradually calcify until something breaks." [T5; measured]
6. **Error-masking constructs ("Obfuscation rate") +47% — now fully defined (previously gated).** GitClear's definition (Appendix A5, "Encyclopedia" entry, canonical): *"The count of newly introduced obfuscating lines per 1,000 changed lines in non-key/value files. These lines reduce a maintainer's ability to reason about interactions between the method and the outside world, and include catch/rescue blocks, safe navigators (characters that obfuscate whether an argument is null-ish), and stub/mock methods in tests."* The construct spans three concrete patterns: (a) broad rescue/catch blocks that swallow exceptions, (b) safe-navigation operators (e.g. `&.` in Ruby/JS) that silently return null instead of surfacing a contract violation, and (c) stubbed/mocked methods in tests that make an integration test pass without reconciling the real external service's behaviour. Values: 16.4 (2023) → 18.5 (2024) → 20.2 (2025) → **24.1 (YTD 2026)**, +47%. **Unit inconsistency found in the PDF itself:** the body text (p.8) states these values are "per million changed lines," while both the earlier metrics-definition table (p.5–6) and the Appendix A5 canonical definition say "per 1,000 changed lines" / "per 1,000 changes." The relative change (+47%) is unaffected either way, but the absolute unit as stated in the narrative section is internally inconsistent with the report's own glossary — treat "per 1,000 changed lines" (A5) as authoritative. The report's own explanation for the rise: "AI assistants are incented to reach for these constructs... They reduce measured defects in code. They save tokens that would be required to research external callers or systems... They are ready to commit faster." [T8-adjacent; measured; now fully VERIFIED, including definition]
7. **Two-week code churn +15% — baselines now confirmed (previously gated): 16.1% (2023) → 15.7% (2024) → 18.9%/19.0%* (2025) → 18.6% (YTD 2026).** The path is **not a smooth trend** — churn *fell* slightly 2023→2024, then jumped sharply in 2025, then eased slightly into 2026 YTD. A companion metric new to this edition, **Throwaway percent (1-week churn)**, moves in lockstep: 14.2% (2023) → 13.6% (2024) → 16.4% (2025) → 16.3% (2026), and has held at a near-constant **~87–88% of two-week churn in every single year** (88% in 2023, 88% in 2026). The report's own reading of this stability: "the growth in churn is not being driven by code that lingers for a week or two before revision; it is overwhelmingly immediate rework." **See the dedicated churn-contradiction assessment below** — the 2024→2025 step in churn coincides with the ~3.3–3.8× jump in underlying sample size documented in the Independence-flags section above, which the report does not address as a possible confound. [measured; now fully VERIFIED, including definition and baselines]
8. **Defect-rate claims: still indirect, chain-of-inference — now confirmed and one internal inconsistency found.** The 2026 PDF's defect argument (p.15–16, "Do These Signals Predict Real Cost?") rests entirely on: (a) the same clone-defect literature cited in 2025 — Mo, Zhang et al. (2017) found "**up to 33%** of clone fragments experiencing bug-fix changes contained propagated bugs," and separately that Wagner et al. GitClear still performs **no direct defect measurement of its own** in either edition, despite the report's framing (title: "Do These Signals Predict Real Cost?"). [measured-by-others / chain-of-inference; do not cite as a GitClear defect finding]
9. **Framing quote (confirmed in full context):** "The headline is not 'AI writes bad code.' It is that today's AI coding agents are incentivized to deliver atomic code — a happy-path feature, a passing unit test, a closed ticket — while quietly taxing the invisible and the deferred: the reuse, consolidation, and error-surfacing that determine how expensive a codebase is to own by year three." - "This is not to say that AI isn't incredibly useful."
- Dataset composition evolves year to year as customers change; line counts are not stable across report vintages.
- Duplicate-block backfill favoured large commits (argued to bias against their conclusion).
- The study includes commented lines and does not split test vs non-test code ("future research").
- Their own prior projections missed (churn overshoot documented above) — to their credit, recorded in the report.

### Authors' own caveats (2026 PDF — newly extracted, full text)

- **No per-model / AI-attribution comparison yet possible (Appendix A6, most substantive caveat in the document):** "We are not yet prepared to make per-model claims with the confidence this audience deserves. The model-attributed portion of our dataset, while growing quickly, is not yet large or evenly distributed enough to support head-to-head conclusions that would survive the kind of scrutiny we apply to the aggregate trends in this paper." They explicitly frame any pattern visible on their live per-model dashboard as "a hypothesis to be confirmed: not yet a robust finding."
- **2026 figures are year-to-date on a smaller, in-progress sample** and should be read as "a leading indicator rather than a closed annual figure" — repeated in the main text, Appendix A2 methodology notes, and table footnotes.
- **Per-signal population sizes are not uniform** — each signal is computed only over the subset of changes where it is meaningful (e.g. function connectivity restricted to files with functions/methods; churn restricted to customer repos).
- **Self-aware likely-conservative adoption estimate:** footnote R9 acknowledges their 24.1% "AI-assisted commits" figure sits at the low end of publicly claimed AI-authorship rates (25–46% from other sources).
- **Standing commitment repeated from prior editions:** "we pledge to publish results whether they are positive, negative, or null" (Appendix A4).
- **Not stated as a caveat by the authors, but a data-quality observation from this review** (kept distinct from the authors' own list per PROTOCOL's measured/self-reported separation principle): the report does not discuss or control for the ~3.3–3.8× jump in per-signal record counts between 2024 and 2025 documented in Appendix A1 (see Independence flags above) — this is a reviewer-identified gap, not an author-acknowledged limitation.

## What this can and cannot support

Can support: directional, era-aggregate claims that refactoring/code-reuse activity has collapsed (moved-lines 12.7%→3.8% 2023–2026 YTD; function connectivity −35%; long-term update −74%) and that duplication, copy/paste, error-masking, and churn have all risen, across a large multi-company codebase population coinciding with AI adoption (with corroboration required per COI flag). The 2026 edition additionally supports: a full, quotable definition of the error-masking/obfuscation construct; confirmed churn baselines; and confirmation that GitClear's own defect argument is entirely literature-derived, not measured. ## Churn contradiction assessment (informs `synthesis/contradictions.md` — that file was not edited; for the human's review)

The full 2026 PDF **weakens** GitClear's side of the "Churn rise in the AI era: real or artefact?" contradiction (logged against arXiv 2507.10422). Reasoning:
1. The +15% churn rise is not a steady climb — it is concentrated almost entirely in a single 2024→2025 step (16.1%→15.7%→18.9%→18.6% across 2023–2026 YTD; i.e., churn was flat-to-falling for the first year of the window and flat-to-falling again in the most recent year, with all of the net rise occurring in one annual step).
2. That same step coincides exactly with a **~3.3–3.8× jump in the record count underlying every one of the report's eight signals simultaneously** (Appendix A1 sample sizes), not just churn — e.g. duplication's sample also jumped 3.8×, function connectivity's 3.6×, in the same year. Two consecutive complete years (2024, 2025) showing a uniform ~3.5× jump in dataset size across every unrelated metric is much better explained by a large block of new customers/repos joining GitClear's analysed population in 2025 than by organic behavioural change — and the report neither discloses nor investigates this as a possible driver of the churn (or any other) step-change.
3. GitClear's own churn definition and methodology are otherwise unchanged and internally consistent between editions, so this is not a definitional artefact — it is a population-composition risk specific to the *magnitude and timing* of the trend, sitting on top of the already-acknowledged year-to-year dataset drift.
4. Net effect: this does not resolve the contradiction (arXiv 2507.10422 remains unextracted per the ledger, so its own methodology is untested), but it shifts the balance of plausibility somewhat toward "GitClear's churn signal is at least partly an artefact of a changing customer base" and away from "churn genuinely rose economy-wide in step with AI adoption" — reinforcing, not undermining, the case for treating GitClear's churn figure as the weakest of its eight signals. Recommend the human weigh this when arXiv 2507.10422 is eventually extracted and the contradiction entry is revisited.
