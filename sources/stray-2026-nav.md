## Tier, era, independence

- **Tier: B — structured observational longitudinal case study with real telemetry, no causal identification design.** Justification: this is genuine git-history telemetry (PyDriller-mined commit data, 703 repos, cleaned to 26,317 commits / N=4,095 weekly observations across 105 weeks), not a self-report survey alone, and it is peer-reviewed (HICSS, best-paper nominee). But there is **no randomisation and no quasi-experimental identification device** (no RDD, no matched control group, no instrument for the self-selection into Copilot adoption) — the "before/after" comparison is a plain non-random cohort contrast, and the Copilot-user/non-user split is itself the confound the paper's own headline finding is about (see self-selection below). This sits below churn-151-repos-2025's Tier A (which used RDD + placebo checks) and above a bare Tier C practitioner write-up (real, cleaned, statistically-tested telemetry across 39 individually tracked developers with a matched survey/interview layer). Graded B per PROTOCOL §3's "large-scale telemetry without randomisation, longitudinal industry reports" bar.
- **Era: E1, with an explicit E1→E2 modality-disclosure gap the paper itself does not resolve.** The measured telemetry window is **2022-09-04 to 2024-09-01** (exact dates, from Figure 2's caption), with Copilot introduced at NAV IT on **2023-09-01**. This is squarely the GitHub Copilot inline-suggestion/autocomplete era — before Copilot's agent-mode and Copilot Workspace features existed (both launched ~2025). The paper never mentions agent mode, multi-file edits, or plan-execute-verify workflows; developers describe Copilot generating "a starting point to build on" and handling "boilerplate code, documentation, and repetitive tasks" — classic E1 autocomplete framing. **Caveat:** GitHub Copilot Chat (a chat-panel, E2-adjacent feature) was already GA within the study window (from ~March 2023), and the paper does not disaggregate inline-suggestion use from chat use — so a small E2 admixture cannot be ruled out, but the dominant, described modality is E1. **Per PROTOCOL's era-transfer rule, this is pre-agentic evidence: admissible for RQ2's measurement-methodology point (self-report vs. telemetry) and for the plausibly era-independent perception–reality-gap tension, but any effect-size claim about *current* (E3) Copilot agent-mode productivity must not be drawn from this source.**
- **Independence: high — no vendor COI found, and this is plausibly the cleanest independence line in the evidence base to date.** Authors are affiliated with the University of Oslo and SINTEF (Norway's largest independent, not-for-profit research institute); funding is disclosed as "Research Council of Norway through the projects TransformIT (grant 321477) and Kairos (grant 357147)" — Norwegian public national research funding, not GitHub/Microsoft or any vendor. NAV IT itself is the Norwegian Labour and Welfare Administration's IT division — a public-sector body with no commercial stake in Copilot's success. No GitHub/Microsoft author, funder, or named data-sharing arrangement was found anywhere in the text (the dataset was self-collected by the researchers via GitHub's public API and PyDriller, not supplied by GitHub). **No COI to flag.**

## Method & population/dataset

- **Site:** NAV IT, the technology division of NAV (the Norwegian Labour and Welfare Administration), described as "nearly one thousand employees" across developers, designers, infrastructure/cloud specialists, architects, data scientists, and team leads.
- **Adoption:** September 2023 — 100 developers volunteered for an initial GitHub Copilot licence (opt-in, not randomised or mandated). By May 2025, Copilot users had grown to 250 (outside the analysed window).
- **Telemetry (measurement basis: measured):**
  - Data source: all of NAV IT's public GitHub repositories, cloned and mined locally with **PyDriller**, then deleted, retaining only commit data for developers who had linked their survey response to a GitHub username.
  - Raw dataset: 37,974 commits. Cleaning: removed cross-repo duplicates; trimmed at the 95th percentile per metric to remove non-human-generated outliers (e.g., log/build-file churn); excluded developers averaging <1 commit/week (deemed unrepresentative of professional developers — an explicit, non-trivial exclusion that skews the retained sample toward more prolific contributors).
  - **Final cleaned dataset: 26,317 non-merge commits across 703 repositories, for 39 developers (25 Copilot users, 14 non-users).** Aggregated into weekly chunks (weeks with zero commits imputed as 0), giving **N=4,095 weekly observations** (105 weeks × 39 developers).
  - **Exact date range: 2022-09-04 to 2024-09-01** (one year pre-Copilot, one year post — Copilot introduced 2023-09-01).
- **Survey (measurement basis: self-reported):** distributed March–April 2024. Single relevant item: *"Since getting access to GitHub Copilot, have you noticed a change in the following: Your own productivity"* on a 5-point Likert scale from "Major decrease" (−2) to "Major increase" (+2). 63 respondents in total provided their GitHub username to allow survey-to-telemetry linkage; the final matched analysis sample (after the telemetry-side cleaning above) is the 25 Copilot users (and separately, non-users answered a role-only portion of the survey, not the productivity item, since it was only asked of self-identified adopters).
- **Interviews (qualitative):** 13 semi-structured interviews, Nov–Dec 2023, 30–60 minutes (avg. 47 min), AI-transcribed then manually corrected, analysed thematically (open coding, memoing, comparison).
- **Population/role detail:** roles self-reported via survey — Data Engineer, Data Scientist/Analyst, Platform Engineer, Tech Lead, Developer (back-end), Developer (front-end) — Figure 1 shows broadly similar role-mix between the 25-user and 14-non-user groups, **including Tech Lead present in both groups**, which the authors use to argue the activity gap is not simply a role/seniority artefact (though they also cite Cui et al. 2024's finding that seniors code less as a candidate confound they cannot fully rule out with their data).
- **No randomisation anywhere in the design.** Adoption is self-selected (voluntary opt-in); the user/non-user split is an observational cohort comparison, not an assigned treatment.

## Key findings

**All items below carry an explicit measurement-basis tag per CLAUDE.md rule 4.**

1. **[measured] Self-selection, not a treatment effect, explains most of the user/non-user activity gap.** Comparing one year pre- and one year post-Copilot introduction: Copilot adopters (n=25) were already committing **more than twice as frequently**, and contributing substantially more code (~188 lines added / ~105 removed per week pre-adoption) than non-adopters (n=14, ~80 added / ~40 removed per week) **before Copilot existed at NAV IT at all**. **Mann–Whitney U test, p < 0.00555, both pre- and post-adoption periods** (this is the exact and only formal significance-test statistic the paper reports for the activity comparison; it is a between-group test at each period, not a within-group before/after test — see point 2's caveat). Authors' own words: *"Copilot adopters were already significantly more active developers to begin with, and they remained more active afterwards, indicating a self-selection effect."* This is the paper's primary, and cleanest, quantitative finding.
2. **[measured] No statistically significant within-group change in commit activity after Copilot adoption — but note a genuine reporting gap.** The abstract and discussion both assert "no statistically significant changes in commit-based activity for Copilot users after they adopted the tool," and the discussion section repeats this ("there is no statistically significant difference after adopting Copilot"). **However, on a careful read of the full text, no specific before-vs-after (within Copilot-user-group) test statistic or p-value is reported anywhere in the paper for this specific comparison** — the only formal test given (point 1's Mann-Whitney, p<0.00555) is the *between-group* comparison at each time period, not a within-group pre/post test. The "no significant change" claim therefore rests on the paper's descriptive figures (95% CIs shown in Figures 2–3, visibly overlapping before/after for the Copilot-user group) rather than on a named, numbered hypothesis test. **This should be flagged, not silently smoothed over: the null finding is real and visually well-supported by the reported CIs, but it is not accompanied by an explicit statistical-test citation in the primary text**, which is a modest evidentiary gap in the source itself.
3. **[measured] Small positive point-estimate change in net code volume.** Lines added ticked up from ~188/week to ~200/week; lines deleted ticked down from ~105/week to ~98/week; net effect **+16 net lines of code per week on average** for Copilot users post-adoption (versus a roughly flat-to-slightly-declining trend for non-users over the same window). Commit *frequency* itself stayed essentially flat for Copilot users across the two periods (Figure 3). This is the paper's only positive quantitative movement, and it is explicitly not claimed as statistically significant.
4. **[measured] No code-quality degradation detected.** Structural metrics (function complexity, average module size) "remained virtually unchanged and showed no significant differences between users and non-users." No numeric values are given for these metrics in the text — a qualitative/descriptive claim only, not a reported effect size, and should not be cited with false numerical precision.
5. **[measured — the paired perception-vs-measured finding, primary value for tension 1] No significant correlation between an individual's change in measured commit activity and their own self-reported productivity change, across all six activity metrics tested.** This is a genuine **individual-level, same-person pairing**: for each of the 25 Copilot users, the paper correlates (a) their own before→after change in a GitHub activity metric against (b) their own Likert-scale self-reported productivity-change answer from the same survey.
   - Commit count: **Spearman ρ ≈ 0.17, p = 0.406** (the strongest of the six correlations, still non-significant).
   - Net lines of code (Δ Diff, i.e. average weekly net diff after minus before): **ρ ≈ 0.09** ("negligible"; exact p not separately stated but described as consistent with the non-significant pattern).
   - The other four of the "six activity metrics we examined" are referenced only in aggregate ("across all six activity metrics... correlations... were not found to be significant") — **their individual ρ/p values are not given in the text**; only commit count and net-lines-of-code are individually reported with numbers. This should be recorded as an incompleteness in the source, not filled in by inference.
   - **Multiple-comparisons correction: Bonferroni-corrected threshold α = 0.0083 for six tests** — none of the six metrics cleared this threshold.
   - **Directionality/pattern:** points cluster around "No change" to "Slight Increase" on the perceived-productivity axis across a wide spread of actual commit-activity deltas (some developers who committed *less* still reported feeling more productive, and vice versa) — i.e., the null is not "self-report tracks measured output but noisily," it is "self-report shows essentially no linear relationship to measured output change at all," in either direction.
   - **Notably, no Copilot user in the sample reported a productivity *decrease*** (all 25 responses were "No change," "Slight increase," or "Major increase"), despite several of them showing a measured *decline* in average weekly activity over the same period. This asymmetry (uniformly non-negative self-report against a genuinely mixed measured pattern) is itself a finding, not merely the correlation's non-significance.
6. **[self-reported, interview-derived] Mechanisms offered for the perception–activity decoupling.** Interviewees attribute felt productivity gains to: reduced time on boilerplate/syntax lookup, fewer "search the web" detours, improved subjective "flow" and reduced frustration/struggle, and (for at least one developer) increased time spent *reviewing* AI-generated suggestions rather than writing more code outright — the latter offered as a candidate mechanism (citing a companion paper, Stray, Moe, et al. 2025) but **not independently measured within this paper** (no review-time telemetry was collected). Illustrative quotes: I12 ("I'm more productive, absolutely, since I started using Copilot... it's rarer now that I sit and struggle with something all day that might not lead to anything"); I13 ("Just getting code generated as a starting point to build on is incredibly good"); I1, expressing scepticism about measurability itself ("I have no faith that we'll get good numbers (to measure this)"); I5, resisting a tool-centric framing entirely ("I don't think the tool is the most important part of the job. Understanding the task and collaborating are more important.").
7. **[self-reported] Non-adoption reasons.** Some developers declined or dropped Copilot: one (I11) because his team had already eliminated boilerplate through architectural choices, making Copilot "superfluous"; another (I9) after an unconvincing internal demo. Recorded with equal diligence as the positive interview material, per CLAUDE.md rule 3.
8. **[no data / RQ4-relevant null] No cost, licence-spend, or review-time data reported.** The paper contains no token/licence cost figures and no measured review-overhead statistic (the review-time mechanism above is asserted, not measured).

## Authors' own limitations (stated, paraphrased faithfully)

- Possibly too early in adoption to detect a real effect (echoing Humlum & Vestergaard 2025's similar framing for a Danish cross-occupation survey).
- Commits/LOC are acknowledged, upfront, as weak productivity proxies — motivating the mixed-methods design in the first place.
- Small final matched sample (39 developers; only 63 of an unknown larger population linked their username, itself a non-random subsample of survey respondents — willingness to share a GitHub username is not obviously independent of engagement/enthusiasm, a selection-on-selection risk the paper does not explicitly address).
- Low-activity-user exclusion (<1 commit/week) biases the retained sample toward more prolific developers.
- Self-report subject to social-desirability bias and a possible "subject-expectancy effect" (a highly anticipated tool inflating self-perceived performance independent of real change) — the authors raise this explicitly as a candidate explanation for their own null correlation.
- Individual work-pattern heterogeneity (batch committers vs. steady committers vs. flexible schedules) muddies what "activity" even measures.
- Single-organisation, single-country (Norwegian public sector) case study; generalisability not claimed.
- No causal design: the self-selection finding is explicitly used by the authors themselves to warn against naive before/after or user/non-user causal readings ("if one naively compared Copilot users vs. non-users after rollout, one might wrongly conclude Copilot 'caused' more output").

## What this can and cannot support

**Can support:** (a) a real, individually-paired, non-vendor, telemetry-plus-self-report null finding that an individual developer's *change* in self-reported productivity does not correlate with their *change* in measured commit activity (six metrics tested, none significant post-correction) — directly usable for RQ2/RQ6 and tension 1, at medium-high confidence given the clean independence and genuine telemetry, tempered by the small n (25) and single-org scope; (b) a clean illustration of self-selection into AI-tool adoption confounding naive before/after comparisons — directly relevant to RQ6's "what to instrument/avoid" question, since the paper's own worked example (comparing Copilot users to non-users) is exactly the naive comparison RQ6 should warn internal stakeholders against; (c) a genuinely independent (no vendor funding, public-sector site, academic/non-profit-institute authorship) E1-era data point that no code-quality degradation was detected on simple structural metrics, though only descriptively.

**Cannot support:** any effect-size claim about current agentic-tool (E3) productivity — this is Copilot's autocomplete-era functionality, explicitly pre-agent-mode; a causal claim that Copilot has *no* effect on productivity — the design cannot separate "no effect" from "effect masked by self-selection, small n, early-adoption timing, or a proxy metric that doesn't capture the real gain" (the authors themselves refuse to draw a causal null); a precise within-group before/after significance statistic — the paper asserts "no significant change" without citing a specific test value for that specific comparison (see finding 2); a generalisable population estimate — n=39 matched developers, one Norwegian public-sector IT division.

## Cross-reference: tension 1 (perception–reality gap) — report only, `synthesis/contradictions.md` NOT edited

**Verdict: yes, this is a genuine and valuable fourth data point for tension 1, and it has a distinct shape from the three already on file.**

Existing entries:
- **METR (Tier A, E3):** experienced OSS devs *overestimated* — believed ~20% faster, measured 19% slower. A directional magnitude bias, aggregate not individually paired to my knowledge of the existing record's framing.
- **Peng et al. 2023 (Tier A, E1):** freelancers *underestimated* — self-estimated ~35% speedup vs. 55.8% measured. Opposite directional bias to METR.
- **Stanford/Denisov-Blanch (Tier B, talk-grade):** self-rated vs. measured productivity *percentile* "almost as good as flipping a coin" — cross-sectional miscalibration with no consistent direction, off by ~30 percentile points on average.

**Stray et al. adds a fourth, methodologically distinct flavour, not a repeat of any of the above:**
1. **It is a longitudinal, within-person *change-score* correlation, not a cross-sectional level comparison.** METR and Peng compare a single before/after *group-level* percentage (belief vs. measured effect size); Stanford compares self-rated *absolute percentile standing* vs. measured percentile standing at one point in time. Stray instead asks, for the same 25 individuals: does *your own* change in measured activity correlate with *your own* reported direction of productivity change? This is a genuinely different statistical object (a correlation of paired deltas, not a comparison of two point estimates), and it is — to this evidence base's knowledge at time of writing — the only source here that pairs self-report and telemetry **at the individual level with both variables measured on the same people**, rather than comparing a survey-average to a study-average (METR, Peng) or a self-rank to a measured-rank cross-sectionally (Stanford).
2. **The direction question doesn't even arise here, because there is effectively no relationship to have a direction.** METR overestimates; Peng underestimates; Stanford is directionless-but-noisy (a real but scattered correlation, "1-in-3 within own quartile" implies some signal). Stray's ρ≈0.17 (p=0.406, ns) and ρ≈0.09 for the other reported metric are close to zero — the null here is starker: not "miscalibrated in some direction" but "no detectable linear relationship between the two variables at all," on the one dataset in this evidence base where both variables are genuinely measured on identical individuals over the identical window.
3. **A further twist that strengthens the case for real perceptual decoupling (not just noise): uniformly non-negative self-report against a mixed measured pattern.** No Copilot user in the sample reported a productivity *decrease*, even though several showed a measured *decline* in weekly activity. This is not merely "the correlation is weak" — it is "self-report is bounded away from the negative region regardless of what the telemetry shows," which is a stronger and more specific claim than the raw correlation coefficient alone conveys, and is arguably the single most citable line from this paper for an exec-facing "don't trust self-reported AI-productivity surveys" argument.
4. **Independence and era position, relative to the other three:** Stray is the only one of the four with zero vendor funding and a public-sector (non-tech-industry) host organisation — a materially cleaner independence profile than Peng (GitHub/Microsoft COI) and cleaner than Stanford (talk-grade, methodology not fully published in this repo's record). It sits in the same E1 era as Peng (pre-agentic), not E3 like METR — so, per the era-transfer rule, it corroborates the *measurement-methodology* point (self-report is an unreliable instrument) at any era, but cannot itself extend the perception-gap finding into the agentic era.
5. **Recommended framing for the human, if this is folded into `contradictions.md`:** not as a fifth "side" in a for/against sense (there is no real disagreement about *direction* to adjudicate — Stray doesn't claim over- or under-estimation, it claims *no measurable relationship*), but as a **reinforcing, methodologically distinct corroboration** that self-report is not a trustworthy magnitude *or even direction* signal for AI-driven productivity change — arguably the strongest single form the "gap" can take, because it removes the possibility that the gap is just "people are bad at estimating a percentage" (METR/Peng) and shows instead that perceived change doesn't even correlate with the sign of measured change, in a paired individual-level design.

**Fit with dx-reock-2026 (cross-org modest-gains trend), per the task brief's request:** directionally compatible, not directly comparable. DX's ~+7.76% aggregate PR-throughput trend (Tier D, E2/E3 blend, undisclosed modality, no significance testing, Nov 2024–Feb 2026) and Stray's own ~+16 net-lines/week (not statistically significant, E1, 2022–2024) both land in the same qualitative bucket — "small, largely unremarkable aggregate movement in raw output metrics, well short of vendor 3×/10× framing" — but they are not the same claim: DX is a cross-company aggregate with no individual pairing and no significance test; Stray is a single-org, individually paired, explicitly-tested null. The consistency across both is worth one sentence in synthesis ("modest-to-null aggregate output effects recur across very different eras, methods, and populations") but should not be presented as the same finding replicated — they are independent, weakly corroborating signals from different eras, not a tight replication pair. Both, notably, undercut the loudest vendor productivity claims without needing to invoke a single T4/brownfield mechanism — Stray's population is not obviously brownfield/greenfield-differentiated in the text (no codebase-age variable reported), which is itself a gap relative to tension 2.
