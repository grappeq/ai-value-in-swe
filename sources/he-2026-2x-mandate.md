## Tier, era, independence

- **Tier: A.** This is a single-firm case study, not multi-company, and adoption is self-selected rather than randomised — the authors themselves decline to claim "exact causal attribution." But the design clears the PROTOCOL §3 Tier A bar ("large-N telemetry with counterfactuals"): a staggered difference-in-differences with developer fixed effects, a genuine comparison group (113 never-adopters), a parallel-trends event-study check (flat pre-adoption trends), a placebo test for the model-release confound, and three alternative estimators (dropping never-treated, Callaway–Sant'Anna, Borusyak imputation) run for agreement/disagreement. This is quasi-experimental identification of a kind rarely seen in this literature, not a bare observational correlation. **Justification for A over B:** the identification strategy, not the raw developer count, is what the protocol's Tier A language is gating on, and this clears it; single-firm external validity is a distinct *generalisability* caveat (recorded below), not a tier downgrade in itself.
- **Era: E3, confirmed.** Tools studied are Cursor (telemetry Feb 2025–Feb 2026, deprecated) and Claude Code (telemetry from Jul 2025, became dominant post-mandate), plus in-house custom agents (recent months, no usage logs) — genuinely agentic, multi-file, plan-execute tooling, not autocomplete or chat-panel copy-paste. The study window also spans three frontier-model generations (Sonnet 4.5/Claude Code 2.0 Sep 2025, Opus 4.5 Nov 2025, Opus 4.6 Feb 2026) but the paper's own analysis finds the model-generation channel **not separable** from the calendar-wide usage ramp (see Key findings) — a genuinely useful negative finding about era/generation tagging: even within a single company's telemetry, "which model" and "how much more use is happening this month" are confounded.
- **Independence:** Academic-led (CMU + Stanford + one independent researcher), not vendor-published — not Tier D. Flags to record:
  1. **Shared author with stanford-denisov-blanch.** Yegor Denisov-Blanch co-authors this paper too. This is *not* an independent replication of the Stanford telemetry programme's claims — it is the same research group's output on a different (single-enterprise) dataset. Treat convergence between this paper and the Stanford quadrant talks as within-group consistency, not cross-group triangulation.
  2. **Company anonymity.** The studied enterprise is unnamed ("identifying details withheld," ref [12]); we cannot independently verify the authors' assurance that "the company's role was limited to data sharing; it did not participate in the analysis or writing, and no company employee is an author" (footnote 1) or that independent researcher Pavel Azaletskiy has no undisclosed tie to the firm. Taken at face value per the paper's own statement, but flagged as unverifiable from outside.
  3. **Self-selected, favourable site.** The authors explicitly chose this firm *because* it is "close to a near-ideal scenario for AI mandates" — young, AI-forward, uncapped tool budgets, public CTO commitment. They repeatedly and explicitly frame their own headline result as an "upper envelope," not "a representative industry average." This is unusually candid self-flagging, but it means the source cannot support any claim about typical enterprise outcomes.
  4. **No funding-disclosure statement** was found in the pages read (main text + appendix); no vendor/tool-company sponsorship is claimed or apparent.

## Method & population

- **Case:** one unnamed mid-sized B2B software company, R&D org of "several hundred engineers." June 2025: CTO publicly announced a "2× mandate" — double engineering productivity via AI adoption within 12 months, tracked via **merged pull requests per engineer per month** as the company's own metric.
- **Data:** developer-month panel, 802 developers, Jan 2024–Apr 2026 (28 months). Estimation sample restricted to developers with ≥3 active months: **564 developers (451 AI-tool adopters, 113 never-adopters over the window)**. PR-level dataset: **196,212 non-bot pull requests across 364 active repositories**; 30.2% (59,407 PRs) carry the company's own `created-by-ai` label. HR role-coverage ≈91% for the seniority analysis.
- **Adoption definition:** ground-truth Cursor/Claude Code usage telemetry (not the PR label, which is checked separately for robustness — see below).
- **Identification strategy — staggered difference-in-differences.** Baseline specification: `log(y_it) = α_i + β1·Adopted_it + β2·Post_t + ε_it`, developer fixed effects `α_i` absorbing time-invariant differences, `Adopted_it` = 1 in months a developer uses Cursor or Claude Code (pooled), `Post_t` = months after the June 2025 mandate. **Treatment:** the 451 developers, each aligned on their own adoption month (not the mandate date). **Control:** the 113 never-adopters, who "anchor" the shared calendar trend. Preferred specification replaces the single `Post_t` dummy with full calendar-month fixed effects absorbing every organisation-wide monthly shock — this more conservative estimate is carried as the paper's headline.
- **Threats to identification addressed:**
  1. *Non-random adoption/selection:* early adopters have systematically higher pre-adoption throughput (standardised mean difference >1.9) — addressed by within-developer comparison (each developer is their own control).
  2. *Parallel trends:* event study shows output flat before a developer's own adoption month, then stepping up after (Suppl. Fig. 14) — supports, does not prove, the assumption.
  3. *Calendar confounding with the mandate:* the mandate is a single shared date, so its direct effect cannot be separated from a generic organisation-wide trend; staggering on each developer's own adoption month (not the mandate date) is what makes the adoption effect separable at all.
  4. *Frontier-model-release confound:* three model releases occurred inside the window. Addressed via a **placebo test** — a fabricated "release" inserted in August 2025 (a month with no launch). Result: the placebo moved the outcome about as much as the genuine releases, and the three genuine release coefficients sign-flip (Sonnet 4.5 −0.123**, Opus 4.5 incremental −0.208***, Opus 4.6 incremental +0.613***) — the authors conclude the **model-generation channel is not credibly identified** at all and report it only "to document the limit of firm-level calendar designs," explicitly declining to credit any specific model generation with the gains.
  5. *Estimator sensitivity when effects grow over time:* re-estimated with Callaway–Sant'Anna and Borusyak imputation (which compare adopters only to *not-yet-adopted* developers). Effect shrinks to non-significant (+0.162) under Callaway–Sant'Anna — but the authors show this is because that estimator charges the steep early-2026 organisation-wide usage ramp to "calendar time" rather than "AI," which mechanically understates an effect that is still growing with use; they read this as localised attenuation, not a failure of the main result.
  6. *Adoption-signal robustness:* re-defining adoption using telemetry-OR-PR-label (instead of telemetry alone) reclassifies 36 developers from control to treatment and moves the coefficient **up** (+0.354→+0.410***), which the authors read as evidence the telemetry-only definition is conservative, not inflated.
- **Explicit causal framing (their words, not a summary):** *"Because adoption and usage intensity were not randomly assigned, we read this evidence as strongly implicating an adoption-and-use channel rather than as exact causal attribution."* And: *"we frame the headline as a throughput doubling during a near-best-case rollout that within-developer and dose-response evidence strongly tie to adoption and accumulated use, while treating the exact causal magnitude as bounded rather than point-identified."*

## Key findings (all **measured**, telemetry-based; none are self-report)

1. **Headline throughput — 2.09× — and what it is a ratio of.** Mean PRs *per active developer* rose from **21.2** (pre-mandate baseline, Jan–Apr 2025) to **44.3** (Apr 2026) = **2.09×**. This is the raw aggregate pre/post ratio on **authored** PRs (not the mandate's own "merged PRs" metric, though re-estimating on merged PRs changes the adoption coefficient only marginally: +0.354 vs +0.347). **This 2.09× is not the causal/within-developer estimate** — it bundles calendar drift, hiring, and mandate effects together with genuine adoption effects. Per-*engineer* (including zero-output months in the denominator, closer to the mandate's own construct): 2.42× (authored), 2.31× (merged).
2. **The causal/within-developer decomposition is smaller than the headline.** Decomposing the gain (Table II) at April 2026 usage levels: **adoption jump β1 = +0.143*** (contributes +0.14 log points), accumulating-use slope β = +0.041*** (contributes +0.45 log points — the larger share), residual mandate term θ = −0.05 (contributes −0.05, i.e. slightly negative/near-zero once adoption and use are accounted for). Total = +0.54 log points = **1.72×** under the baseline spec; **1.46×** under the more conservative full-calendar-month-FE spec (the paper's preferred headline); rising to **1.99× by nine months on tool** in the month-by-month event study. The pooled single-coefficient estimate (Table I, +0.354 ≈ +42%) is smaller again than both, because it averages short and long horizons into one jump. **The mandate itself is a catalyst, not a direct driver** — its own residual coefficient is essentially zero or negative once adoption and accumulated use are controlled for.
3. **Newer-code concentration — exact numbers, and what "newer code" means.** "Newer code" = **repository age (created 2022 or later vs. pre-2022 "legacy")**, not new developers, new files within old repos, or task type. On a developer×cohort×month panel: adoption raises throughput by **+0.364*** (≈+44%) in post-2022 repositories** but only **+0.117 (≈+12%, not significant)** in pre-2022 legacy repositories; the difference (+0.25) is itself significant at p<0.01. This is the paper's clearest §5-tension-2-relevant finding: the gain "concentrates where the code is newer" and is "barely present" in legacy code, inside the very same near-best-case firm.
4. **Review findings — the core of §5 tension 6, exact numbers.**
   - Human review coverage (share of PRs receiving ≥1 human review): **89% → 68%** (−21 percentage points).
   - Automated review adoption: **~19% → ~84%** (+65 pp), **overtaking human review shortly after the mandate**.
   - Substantive human review (review with a human-written comment): **~39% → ~21%** of PRs (main text); Figure 10's own chart labels read **47% → 21%** — a minor internal inconsistency between the authors' prose and their figure annotation, both pointing the same direction (substantive review roughly halves), flagged here rather than silently resolved.
   - Silent (approval-only) human review: held roughly flat at **~50%** of PRs — so the human review that remains is increasingly "bare approval," not substantive.
   - Per-reviewer load: **roughly doubled (2.0×)**. Organisation-wide PR volume grew **3.1×** over the early-2025 baseline while the pool of developers acting as reviewers grew only **1.5×** — the mechanical source of the bottleneck.
   - Per-PR AI premium, controlling for author/month/change-size (Table IV, post-mandate column): comments +0.083*** (≈+9%), coding lead −0.028* (≈−3%, i.e. AI PRs are opened slightly *faster*), pickup +0.139*** (≈+15%), **review lead +0.181*** (≈+20%)**, **total cycle time +0.202*** (≈+22%)**, review rounds −0.007 (flat, n.s.), human review coverage −0.002 (flat, n.s.). The latency premium is concentrated specifically in the interval between first human review and merge — "more reviewer engagement, not more fixing," per the authors — not in extra revision rounds or reduced review likelihood.
   - **Conservative-estimate caveat the authors flag themselves:** late in the window the company began routing PRs through AI-driven review/auto-approval, which collapses human-review latency for a growing share of PRs and **biases the measured review-latency premium toward zero** — so the +20%/+22% figures above likely understate the true premium for PRs still going through human review.
   - Organisation-level corroboration (Suppl. Fig. 12): 90th-percentile end-to-end cycle time climbed to a peak of **~66 hours** through 2025, then *receded* through early 2026 — but decomposing by review depth shows this recovery is a **composition shift, not a faster queue**: cycle time for PRs that got a *substantive* human review climbed to its own hump near **114 hours** and remains around **73 hours** in 2026 (still above the ~63-hour pre-ramp level); what actually fell is the *share* of PRs routed through substantive review at all (pre-mandate peak ~48% of merged PRs → ~21%). The organisation "solved" its aggregate latency metric by routing work around human review, not by reviewing faster.
5. **Quality/defect/rework outcomes — coarse proxies only, no defect or incident data.**
   - Developer-level (Table I): Merge rate +0.006 (n.s.); revert rate −0.004+ (marginal, near-zero-to-slightly-negative).
   - PR-level, AI-authored vs. human, controlling for author/month/size (Table IV): P(merged) pooled +0.006 (n.s.), post-mandate +0.011+ (marginal); **P(reverted) pooled −0.059***, post-mandate −0.067*** — i.e. AI-authored PRs revert *less* than comparable human PRs, not more, and this is not a size artefact (AI-authored PRs are if anything larger).**
   - **No defect, incident, or rework-hours data of any kind is reported.** The authors explicitly caveat their own quality signals: *"the created-by-ai label is not a randomized marker and may select toward safer changes, and merge and revert rates are coarse, short-horizon proxies that miss defects, incidents, and maintainability."* This is a null/negative result for the "AI degrades measurable quality" hypothesis, but on very thin instruments — record it as such, not as a clean bill of health.
6. **Cost data (RQ4) — thin, as pre-registered.** Only one cost figure exists in the paper: **per-capita Claude Code token spend rose ~20× over the window, from $42 to $856 per developer-month** (Jul 2025–Apr 2026), explicitly labelled a "Claude-only lower bound" because Cursor usage is not dollar-denominated. No infrastructure/compute cost, no headcount/reallocation cost, no dollar figure for review overhead. Consistent with RQ4's pre-registered expectation of scarce cost data even in a rigorous source.
7. **Seniority heterogeneity (§5 tension 5) — broadly uniform, one outlier.** Table III: IC +0.235*** (+27%, n=163), Senior +0.352*** (+42%, n=110), Staff +0.330*** (+39%, n=55), Principal +0.323+ (+38%, n=16, imprecise). A model allowing the effect to vary by tier finds these **statistically indistinguishable across the IC-to-Principal ladder** — i.e. **no significant seniority asymmetry** among individual-contributor-track developers in this firm. Management is the one exception: +0.619*** (≈+86%), the largest proportional gain of any group — interpreted by the authors as a "re-entry effect" (managers who had drifted from hands-on coding face a steep ramp-up cost that AI lowers, amplified by a low base rate of prior code output), not as evidence of a general seniority gradient.
8. **Model-generation effects — explicitly not separable (see Threats above).** Positive but non-causal association with *cumulative use* (β≈+0.04***, robust across specifications); no credible attribution to any specific frontier-model generation.

## What this can and cannot support

**Can support (Tier A, E3, single-firm-bounded):** that a near-doubling of per-developer throughput is *attainable* under a favourable, well-resourced enterprise AI mandate, that the gain is driven by accumulated use more than a one-off adoption jump, that gains concentrate in newer/non-legacy codebase areas even within the same best-case firm (direct §5-tension-2 support), that a throughput surge under fixed review capacity produces a measurable and severe review-bottleneck/automation-substitution effect (the strongest quantitative evidence for §5 tension 6 found in this research programme to date), and that coarse merge/revert quality proxies did not visibly deteriorate in this instance. Can also support, at lower confidence given the endogeneity caveats the authors themselves apply: that the effect grows with cumulative/dose-response use (association, not causal) and that seniority gradients may be flatter in agentic-era enterprise settings than the Cui et al./Microsoft-Accenture pre-agentic literature suggests (see cross-ref below).

**Cannot support:** any claim about typical or organic (non-mandated) AI adoption outcomes — this is a self-selected, unusually favourable, single, unnamed firm, explicitly framed by its own authors as an "upper envelope... not a representative industry average"; any defect/incident/technical-debt claim (none measured — only merge and revert rates, both flagged by the authors as coarse and short-horizon); any attribution of gains to a specific frontier model generation (explicitly not identified — placebo test failed to distinguish real releases from a fabricated one); any claim that the review-latency premium is fully quantified (the authors' own late-window auto-approval caveat means the true premium is probably understated); and no exact point-causal magnitude — the authors themselves insist the causal reading is "bounded rather than point-identified."

## Cross-ref guidance (for orchestrator — not written to contradictions.md)

**(a) vs. the Stanford quadrant (stanford-denisov-blanch):** Directionally consistent — both find gains shrinking or vanishing as codebase context/maturity increases (Stanford: complexity × greenfield/brownfield quadrant, self-reported team classification, log-scale codebase-size decay from 1,000–10M LOC; He et al.: objective repo-creation-date cohort, +44% newer vs +12% n.s. legacy). **However this is not an independent replication** — Denisov-Blanch co-authors both, so treat this as within-group consistency across two different datasets/methods, not cross-group triangulation. The two studies use different operationalisations of "brownfield" (subjective complexity/greenfield rating vs. objective repo age) — worth noting in synthesis as convergent-but-methodologically-distinct rather than a single numeric confirmation. Also worth noting: this same author group has a directly related companion paper already flagged as a follow-up lead (He, Miller, Agarwal, Kästner, Vasilescu, MSR 2026, "Speed at the cost of quality: How Cursor AI increases short-term velocity and long-term complexity in open-source projects") which the paper cites as ref [16] — internally consistent narrative across the group's own output.

**(b) vs. Faros/Agoda/Zendesk bottleneck-displacement claims:** I did not find evidence in the ledger (not checked directly, but not part of this task's remit) that these specific practitioner sources have been extracted yet in this project. Recommend the orchestrator check `LEDGER.md`/`evidence-table.tsv` directly. Substantively: this paper is, to my knowledge, the single strongest *quantitative, identification-strategy-backed* confirmation of tension 6 found anywhere in the literature so far — per-reviewer load 2.0×, automated review overtaking human review (84% vs 68%), substantive review halving, review-lead-time +20%, total-cycle +22%, all while merge/revert rates hold flat. If Faros/Agoda/Zendesk-class Tier C/D sources report the same *qualitative* pattern (review queues growing under AI-driven authoring throughput), this is the anchor to triangulate them against; if any report the opposite (no review strain), that is a genuine, worth-flagging contradiction given how carefully this paper's bottleneck finding is identified.

**(c) vs. metr-2025-rct:** Not a head-to-head contradiction despite the very different headline directions (METR: −19% measured slowdown for experienced OSS devs on isolated tasks vs. self-reported +20% belief; He et al.: +46–72% within-developer causal gain, +109% raw aggregate, in a single enterprise's own codebase over up to two years). The paper's own related-work section explicitly positions itself against METR (cited as ref [8], "the largest field study of experienced open-source developers found a 19% slowdown") and frames its own result as testing whether a different context — one's own codebase, sustained accumulated use, organisational investment, later/better models — could produce a different outcome; it does not claim to overturn METR, only to show a different mechanism (accumulated use inside a familiar codebase) can compound to a much larger effect than a one-off RCT on unfamiliar OSS tasks can detect. **Recommend flagging this as a genuine RQ2/RQ5 synthesis point rather than a resolved or contested §5 contradiction**: effect sizes in this literature appear to be strongly horizon- and familiarity-dependent, and single-session RCTs (METR) and multi-year accumulated-use panels (He et al.) may simply be measuring different things — this itself is a finding for RQ2 (measurement methods) about what a study's *time horizon* can and cannot capture.

**Also note for RQ1 value-map purposes:** the paper's introduction contains a useful, already-vetted-by-domain-experts ladder of the "most credible field estimates" as of mid-2026 — Peng et al. 21–56% (Copilot RCT, E1/E2), Cui et al. Management Science 2026 (updated field-experiment version, seniority asymmetry), Paradis et al. ICSE-SEIP 2025 (an actual enterprise RCT — rare and high-priority), METR −19%, Stray et al. 2025 (public-sector, no significant change — a null result), and Heilman et al. 2026 (Microsoft, 16,223 engineers, ~40% more PRs in heaviest-usage weeks, dose-response design) which the authors call "the within-engineer study closest to ours." This citation cluster is itself a useful map of the current E3-era evidence base and several are strong follow-up leads (below).

## Notes on internal consistency

One minor discrepancy within the paper itself, flagged for transparency rather than silently resolved: the main text states substantive human review fell "from ~39% to ~21% of PRs," while Figure 10's own chart-label annotations read "47%... 21%." Both point the same direction and magnitude of decline (roughly halving); this is most likely the difference between a stated single pre-mandate baseline figure and a chart-read peak value, not a substantive contradiction, but record both figures rather than picking one.
