## Tier & era & independence

- **Tier: C** (downgraded from the original study's Tier A, deliberately, per the task brief). Justification: this is nominally the same task-level-randomised design as the anchor, run by the same rigorous team — but the authors themselves state the data give "an unreliable signal of the current productivity effect of AI tools," citing severe, non-random participation and task-selection effects that undermine the interpretability of the randomisation (see Key findings §2). It was published as a blog post with a raw CSV and a one-line regression spec, not a paper — none of the original study's 21-factor robustness analysis, screen-recording validation, or expert-forecast calibration was repeated or reported for this wave. Good pedigree and candour, badly compromised execution, thin public write-up: this is practitioner-grade signal, not Tier A/B evidence, despite the source. It does not drop to Tier D — METR is not a vendor and has no commercial stake in the result, and their disclosure of the data's own unreliability is a mark of Tier-C-appropriate candour, not COI.
  - A further distinction within this record: the *quantitative* wave results (returning/new-dev point estimates) are Tier C at best. METR's stated *belief* that speedup has "likely improved" since early 2025 is not a measured or even self-reported statistic — it is explicitly sourced to "conversations with study participants" (anecdotal impression), and the authors themselves say it is only "very weak evidence." This belief statement should not be tiered or cited as an effect-size claim at all; treat it as an expert opinion note, not evidence.
- **Era: E3, later-generation than the anchor, but imprecisely specified.** The post refers generically to "agentic tools among open-source developers, such as Claude Code and Codex" as characteristic of the current landscape — a more autonomous/CLI-agentic reference set than the anchor's Cursor Pro IDE-agent + Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet. However, unlike the original paper, this post discloses **no per-task or per-developer tool/model breakdown** for the late-2025 wave — "Claude Code and Codex" are illustrative examples, not a confirmed description of what this cohort actually used. Tag as E3 with a precision caveat: later-era agentic tooling is plausible and implied, but not evidenced at the granularity the anchor achieved.
- **Independence:** Independent non-profit; same institutional lineage as the anchor; no vendor sponsorship identified. Carries over the anchor's independence flags (commercial evaluation relationships with frontier labs; recruitment via professional networks) plus one new, self-inflicted flag: METR **cut participant pay from $150/hr to $50/hr** for this wave, which they themselves cite as a likely driver of differential selection — a methodological choice, not an external confound, that materially changed who agreed to participate at all.

## Method & population

- **Design:** Continuation/expansion of the original RCT design (task-level randomisation, AI-allowed vs AI-disallowed), begun **August 2025**. **57 developers** total: **10 returning** from the original early-2025 study ("panel" developers) and **47 newly recruited**. **143 repositories**, **800+ tasks**. Pay: **$50/hour** (vs $150/hour in the original study). Median developer experience: **10 years** open-source contribution.
- **New-cohort population shift:** the post states the new developers were "recruited from a more diverse set of repositories — including smaller, more greenfield, and less mature repositories" — i.e. the population is not a like-for-like replication of the original's large/mature/high-bar-repo sample; this alone would be expected to shift measured effects even absent any data-quality problems, and confounds any direct comparison to the original 19% figure.
- **No breakdown given** in the source for AI-allowed vs AI-disallowed task counts, or for returning- vs new-developer task counts, beyond the aggregate "800+ tasks" and the two headline regression estimates.

## Key findings

1. **Original early-2025 headline, restated for comparison (measured, from the anchor, not new):** "Our early 2025 study found the use of AI causes tasks to take 19% longer, with a confidence interval between +2% and +39%." [verbatim; matches metr-2025-rct.md's point estimate of 18.8%/CI (1.3%, 39.5%) to within rounding] [T4/T8; measured; not this source's own finding]
2. **Returning developers, late 2025 (measured, but self-declared unreliable):** "For the subset of the original developers who participated in the later study, we now estimate a speedup of -18% with a confidence interval between -38% and +9%." Sign convention (confirmed by direct query to the source): **negative = speedup/faster, positive = slower** — the same convention as the original paper's S statistic. So the point estimate is an 18% *reduction* in completion time (i.e., AI *faster*), but the 95% CI runs from −38% (strong speedup) to +9% (mild slowdown) — **it crosses zero and is not statistically significant.** [T4/T8, panel subset; measured but explicitly flagged unreliable by the authors]
3. **New developers, late 2025 (measured, but self-declared unreliable):** "Among newly-recruited developers the estimated speedup is -4%, with a confidence interval between -15% and +9%." Also crosses zero; also not statistically significant. Population is skewed toward smaller/greenfield/less-mature repos (see Method above), so this is not a clean like-for-like update even setting aside reliability concerns. [T3/T4 mix, new-recruit subset; measured but explicitly flagged unreliable]
4. **Why METR judges the wave unreliable — verbatim, itemised (self-reported methodological critique of their own instrument):**
   - Participation selection: "we have observed a significant increase in developers choosing not to participate in the study because they do not wish to work without AI, which likely biases downwards our estimate of AI-assisted speedup" — stated as **the primary reason**.
   - Within-study task selection: "30% to 50% of developers told us that they were choosing not to submit some tasks because they did not want to do them without AI" — meaning the tasks that *do* enter the sample are systematically those with lower AI value, biasing estimates toward smaller measured effects.
   - Pay-rate confound: pay was reduced "from $150/hr to $50/hr in the original study" — a self-inflicted change plausibly altering who chose to participate at all.
   - Measurement breakdown under concurrent agent use: "Some developers reported it was challenging to report time-spent in completing tasks when they used agentic tools" (concurrent/parallel agent workflows made self-reported time tracking unreliable).
   - Task-type substitution: "Some developers told us the types of tasks they attempted were different with agentic AI, leaning on the strengths of AI" — comparability across AI-allowed/disallowed conditions degraded.
   - Output-quality drift: "Some developers told us the quality of the final work, for a given task, was different between AI-allowed and AI-disallowed conditions" — a further confound to the time-based outcome measure.
   - Differential non-compliance: "Some developers were less likely to complete tasks that they submitted if they were assigned to the AI-disallowed condition" — attrition itself correlated with treatment arm.
   [All of the above: methodology finding, era-independent in the sense that it documents a general hazard of longitudinal RCT designs on voluntary panels, not an effect-size claim.]
5. **METR's stated belief about current (2026) speedup (anecdotal, explicitly NOT a measured or reliable estimate):** "Based on conversations with study participants, we believe it is likely that developers are more sped up from AI tools now — in early 2026 — compared to our estimates from early 2025." Immediately qualified: "Because of the selection effects in our experiment, our data is only very weak evidence for the size of this increase." [belief/anecdotal — no effect size attached; must never be cited alongside a percentage figure, because no reliable figure exists for this claim]
6. **Planned design changes (methodology note, not a finding):** METR lists five candidate redesigns — shorter/more-intensive experiments at higher pay; continued observational tracking of their developer pool; more careful survey/time-use instruments; reverting to a simpler fixed-task design; and randomising at the developer level rather than the task level. Signals that METR itself does not consider the task-level-randomised panel design, as currently run, fit for purpose for tracking this effect over time.

## What this can and cannot support

**Can support:** that METR's own attempt to extend its RCT design into a second wave (Aug 2025 population, later agentic tooling) produced wide, zero-crossing, statistically non-significant point estimates, and that METR itself attributes this to named, specific selection and measurement confounds rather than presenting it as a real-world estimate — a useful RQ2 case study in how hard longitudinal RCTs on voluntary developer panels are to sustain, and a caution against taking any single-wave "speedup went from −19% to −18%" headline (as circulated in some secondary commentary, see leads) at face value.

**Cannot support:** any quantitative claim about actual 2026 AI speedup or slowdown magnitude for experienced developers on mature codebases — the authors explicitly say their own data is "only very weak evidence for the size of this increase," and both point estimates are statistically indistinguishable from zero. Cannot be used to revise, confirm, extend, or weaken the original −19% figure's *measured* status. Cannot be used to assert that "speedup has improved" as a graded fact — that is an unquantified author belief, not a finding, and must not appear in the evidence table with an effect size attached.

## Relation to metr-2025-rct

- **Does not contradict the −19% anchor.** METR does not retract, revise, or dispute the original early-2025 point estimate or its confidence interval; both are restated here unchanged, and the original paper's status (Tier A, verified) is untouched by this record.
- **Does not confirm or extend it either.** The new wave's own numbers are self-declared unreliable by the study's authors; triangulating them against the anchor would misuse evidence the source itself disclaims. Treat the two returning/new-dev point estimates as **not usable for RQ1 effect-size claims**, only as an RQ2 methodology illustration.
- **Bounds the anchor's scope rather than updating it.** This update reinforces — rather than loosens — the anchor's existing scoping caveat: the −19% figure is early-2025-tools/experienced-devs/familiar-mature-repos specific, and this source is best read as METR itself warning against extrapolating that figure forward to 2026 tools, while candidly admitting they cannot yet supply a reliable replacement number. Net effect on how the anchor should be cited: unchanged in substance, strengthened in the "do not extrapolate to today's tools" caveat that sources/metr-2025-rct.md already carries in its Recency check notes.
- **Tension 1 (perception–reality gap, PROTOCOL §5.1): untouched by this specific source.** The fetched blog post does not report a forecast-vs-actual perceived-speedup comparison for the late-2025 wave — that mechanism was central to the original paper's design (pre-registered forecasts vs measured outcomes) and is not repeated or referenced here. This source therefore neither replicates nor bears on tension 1 either way. Note for the queue: METR published a separate post, "Measuring the Self-Reported Impact of Early-2026 AI on Technical Worker Productivity" (2026-05-11, https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-11-ai-usage-survey/), whose title suggests it may be exactly the kind of 2026 self-report source that *would* bear on tension 1 — flagged below as a high-priority Phase 1 lead, not extracted here.
- **Net verdict for cross-referencing purposes:** this source should be filed alongside the anchor as a "same team, attempted follow-up, inconclusive" record — it is evidence about the difficulty of measuring the effect over time, not competing evidence about the effect's current size.
