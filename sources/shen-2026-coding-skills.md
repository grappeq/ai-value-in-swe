## Tier, era, independence

- **Tier: A-candidate.** Genuine RCT: individual random assignment to AI-assisted vs. control arms (26/26), pre-specified outcome instrument (comprehension quiz), pre-registered-style power analysis (a priori conservative effect size assumption of d = 0.85, half the pilot's observed effect). Caveats that keep it "candidate" rather than an unqualified A: N = 52 is small; single task/library/session (~1 hour); crowdworker population rather than engineers-in-context; outcome measured *immediately* post-task with no longitudinal follow-up; ethics review was **internal to Anthropic**, not an accredited external IRB ("reviewed and approved by internal reviewers at Anthropic").
- **Era: E2 (chat-assisted).** The AI condition was explicitly a **chat interface**, not autocomplete and not agentic: *"The base model used for this assistant is GPT-4o, and the model is prompted to be an intelligent coding assistant."* No multi-file edits, no autonomous plan-execute-verify loop, no IDE integration described — squarely E2 by the modality-not-year rule (PROTOCOL §2), despite being a January 2026 publication. This matters for the era-transfer rule: the effect-size numbers below are **not**, by default, licensed to support E3 (agentic) claims in the exec brief. The authors themselves argue for provisional era-independence (see "What this can and cannot support" below) but that is their own untested claim, not evidence.
- **Independence / COI — two-directional, per task brief:**
  - **Flag:** Both authors are Anthropic employees; the study ran under the Anthropic Fellows Program; hosted on Anthropic's own research blog. Standard Tier-D-style vendor-research flag applies to provenance.
  - **Cuts against simple marketing COI (direction 1):** the finding is unflattering to AI coding assistance in general — it reports a learning cost, not a productivity win, and explicitly states AI assistance "should be carefully adopted into workflows... particularly in safety-critical domains," a caution rather than a sales pitch.
  - **Does not fully clear COI either (direction 2):** (a) the AI condition tested is **a competitor's model, GPT-4o, not Claude** — Anthropic is not testing its own product, but this also means the study cannot be read as "Anthropic found its own tool harms learning" (a stronger, more costly admission it did not make); (b) the result is consonant with Anthropic's public safety-first brand positioning and its Fellows Program mandate, so it carries a *reputational/strategic* COI even where it lacks a *product-marketing* COI — a caution narrative about competitor-model chat assistants is cheap for Anthropic to publish and costs it nothing competitively. Record both directions; do not resolve to "no COI."

## Method & population

- **Design:** Between-subjects RCT. Two arms: AI-assisted (chat access to a GPT-4o-based assistant with visibility into the participant's current code) vs. control (no AI). 26 participants per arm (52 analysed; 53 completed all three parts, 1 disqualified for leaving four quiz questions blank).
- **Task:** Learn the Python **Trio** library (structured-concurrency/async I/O) via two standalone coding tasks: (a) a timer that prints every second while other functions run concurrently; (b) record retrieval with error handling. Framed by the authors as sufficiently self-contained that either task is completable independently. This is a **learning/tutorial exercise on unfamiliar library material**, not a production task — closest T-taxonomy analogue is **T3 (greenfield/prototyping)** by code novelty, but it should be tagged separately as a **skill-formation exercise**, not treated as ordinary T3 evidence, since the object of measurement is the human's resulting comprehension, not delivered code. Structure: 10-minute warm-up (unrelated Python task) + 35-minute main task + comprehension quiz.
- **Population:** N = 52 crowdworker-recruited developers. Inclusion criteria: ≥1 year Python experience, coding at least weekly, tried AI coding assistance "a few times," and — critically — **never used Trio** (controls for prior-knowledge confound on the specific tested library). Experience mix: 1–3 years 7.7% (n=4), 4–6 years 36.5% (n=19), 7+ years 55.8% (n=29) — i.e. the sample is **majority-experienced by tenure**, not majority-junior; "mostly junior" per the triage brief should be read loosely (freelance/crowdworker population, not necessarily junior by seniority). Compensation: flat $150 for ~58.5 minutes average (max 1h15m).

## Key findings

### 1. Comprehension outcome (primary; measured)
- 14-question quiz across 7 Trio concepts (async/await, starting functions, error handling, coroutines, memory channels, nursery management, sequential vs. concurrent execution); scored out of **27 points**, restricted to debugging/code-reading/conceptual question types (code-writing questions excluded from this score to avoid syntax-error confounds).
- **Treatment (AI) group scored significantly lower than control:** raw difference **4.15 points on the 27-point quiz**, described by the authors as **"a 17% score difference or 2 grade points."** **Cohen's d = 0.738, p = 0.010.** Controlling for warm-up-task time as a covariate, effect remains significant: d = 0.725, p = 0.016.
- **Numeric caveat (flag for the ledger/next reader):** repeated fetches of the full text gave inconsistent absolute-percentage reconstructions of the two group means (variously ~64–66% AI vs ~80–81% control across different fetch passes, versus the triage brief's cited "50% vs 67%" from Anthropic's own announcement). All fetches agree exactly on the *relative* statistics that matter (d = 0.738, p = 0.010, 4.15-point/17%/"2 grade points" gap on a 27-point quiz) — those are treated as verified. The **absolute group-mean percentages are not reliably pinned down** from this session's fetches (table/figure rendering, not raw text, is the likely failure point) and should not be quoted as precise figures without a direct look at Table/Figure 6 or the OSF-hosted data. Use the effect size and p-value, not "50% vs 67%," as the load-bearing number until this is resolved.
- **Breakdown by question type:** largest AI-vs-control gap on debugging questions; smallest gap on code-reading questions (directional only from the fetched text; exact per-domain point values not confirmed).

### 2. Task speed/completion (secondary; measured; null result)
- Completion time: AI condition median ≈ 19.5 minutes vs. control ≈ 23 minutes (session-reported approximation) — **not statistically significant**; authors state plainly: *"we did not find a statistically significant acceleration in completion time with AI assistance."* Record as **null**, per CLAUDE.md's "record nulls with the same diligence" rule.
- **Completion rate asymmetry:** 100% of the AI arm completed both tasks within the time limit vs. 85% (22/26) of the control arm — a secondary measured outcome partially offsetting the "no speed benefit" framing (AI raised the floor on *finishing*, even without raising the *ceiling* on speed).
- **Query-composition time eats into the claimed time budget:** some AI-arm participants spent **up to 11 minutes total, and up to 6 minutes on a single query**, composing prompts within the 35-minute task window — the authors explicitly flag this as material given a ~19-minute median completion time.
- **Error counts:** control group median 3 errors vs. AI group median 1 error; control-group errors skewed toward Trio-specific errors (RuntimeWarning, TypeError) requiring conceptual understanding to resolve — consistent with the comprehension-loss story (AI group hit fewer errors but also built less error-driven understanding).

### 3. Usage-style moderation — six interaction patterns (exploratory/observational; NOT causal)
- Identified via **post-hoc qualitative annotation of screen recordings within the AI arm only** — this is explicitly **observational and conditions on post-treatment behaviour**, exactly the CLAUDE.md-flagged risk pattern. It is not a randomised sub-comparison; participants were not assigned to interaction styles, they were clustered by how they behaved after being assigned to the AI arm. Any causal reading ("conceptual questioning *causes* better retention") is confounded by whatever prior disposition or skill led a participant to interact that way in the first place.
- **Three low-scoring patterns** (avg quiz <40%): (1) *AI Delegation* (n=4) — wholesale reliance on AI to write code, fastest completion, few errors, poor retention; (2) *Progressive AI Reliance* (n=4) — started with questions, drifted to full delegation, especially on the second task; (3) *Iterative AI Debugging* (n=4) — leaned on AI to resolve errors rather than to build understanding, more queries, relatively slower.
- **Three high-scoring patterns** (avg quiz ≥65%): (4) *Generation-Then-Comprehension* (n=2) — generated code first, then asked follow-up questions to understand it; (5) *Hybrid Code-Explanation* (n=3) — paired generation requests with explanation requests, spent more time reading explanations; (6) *Conceptual Inquiry* (n=7, the largest high-scoring cluster) — asked only conceptual questions, coded independently, hit many errors but resolved them unaided; fastest among the high-scoring patterns and second-fastest overall (behind AI Delegation).
- **Query-type counts (AI arm, n=25 analysed):** explanation queries most common (84% of participants used them, 79 total); code-generation queries used by 64%; debugging queries rarer (9); capability/appreciation queries rare (4 each).
- Authors' own framing: high-scoring patterns "generally involve more cognitive effort and less AI reliance," but they explicitly label these as behavioural clusters, not experimental arms.

### 4. Other outcomes (measured/self-reported, mixed)
- **Self-reported learning (self-reported):** control group reported *higher* subjective learning on a 7-point scale than the AI group — i.e. the self-report direction is *consistent* with the measured quiz result here (unlike METR's perception-reality reversal — see Cross-ref below). Both groups reported similarly high task enjoyment.
- **Perceived task difficulty (self-reported):** AI group found the coding task easier; both groups rated the post-task quiz as similarly challenging.
- **Adoption method sub-analysis (measured, small-n):** directly pasting AI-generated code (n=9) was fastest; manually retyping AI-suggested code (n=9) matched control-arm speed; both adoption methods produced *similar* quiz scores to each other — suggesting the copy-paste-vs-retype distinction, on its own, does not explain the learning gap (the interaction-*style* moderation above is the stronger signal).

## Authors' stated limitations (Section 7.1, near-verbatim)

- Single task, single library, chat-only interface: *"This should be a lower bound for cognitive offloading since agentic AI coding tools would require even less human participation"* — the authors' own hypothesis that agentic tooling would show the *same or worse* effect, not evidence that it does.
- One-hour window; no longitudinal skill-retention measurement — immediate post-task quiz only, explicitly flagged by the authors as not informative about durable retention.
- Crowdworker population lacked the "real job" incentive to learn the library that an actual employee would have.
- Self-reported AI familiarity collected, but prompting skill/technique not independently measured.
- No human-assistance counterfactual (AI vs. no help, not AI vs. a human tutor/pair).
- Quiz-based evaluation only; alternative evaluation designs (task-based, coding-design assessments) not tried.

## What this can and cannot support

**Can support:** at Tier A-candidate, E2, that in a controlled one-hour setting, chat-based AI assistance (specifically GPT-4o-class, non-agentic) produced significantly worse immediate comprehension of new library material than unassisted learning (d=0.738, p=0.01), without a significant time-to-completion benefit — a genuine causal (randomised) finding, not a correlational one, for *this* outcome. Can support, at lower (observational) confidence, that *how* people use AI chat assistance — conceptual questioning vs. delegation — correlates strongly with whether learning is preserved.

**Cannot support:** claims about agentic/E3 tooling (not tested; the authors flag their own design as a plausible lower bound on offloading, which is a hypothesis, not a transferred finding); claims about durable/longitudinal skill retention (measured immediately post-task only); claims about real engineering-org populations under real job incentives (crowdworker sample, artificial stakes); a causal reading of the six interaction-pattern clusters (post-treatment, observational); or precise absolute quiz percentages (flagged numeric caveat above — use d and p, not "50% vs 67%," pending direct verification against the primary data/OSF repository).

## Cross-reference assessment (report only — not written to contradictions.md)

- **Cognitive-debt cluster / Vella & Blincoe (queued, arXiv 2605.23135, two-wave longitudinal, Tier B, E3):** complementary, not contradictory. Vella & Blincoe report professional engineers' *self-reported* productivity holding stable (84% report gains at both waves) while *self-reported* developer experience worsens — eroding flow state and cognitive load, with a shift toward "supervisory engineering work." Shen & Tamkin supply a **causal, measured** mechanism candidate for part of that pattern (heavy AI reliance measurably degrades comprehension/debugging skill in a controlled setting) even though the two studies sit at different eras (E3 field self-report vs. E2 lab RCT) and different outcome types (perceived cognitive load/flow vs. measured comprehension). Triangulation value: medium — same direction, different method and era, no shared authorship or population.
- **Stanford perception-miscalibration finding (stanford-denisov-blanch.md, finding 10, Tier B, talk-transcript-verified):** that finding is a *general* self-report-vs-measured-productivity miscalibration (43 developers, off by ~30 percentile points, one-in-three within their own quartile) — not comprehension-specific and not AI-specific in cause. Shen & Tamkin's self-report item is narrower (learning, 7-point scale) and, notably, **does not reverse** here — the control group *correctly* self-reported higher learning than the AI group, the same direction as the measured quiz gap. This is a useful counter-note to the "self-report is always miscalibrated in the optimistic direction" reading that METR's −19%/+20% reversal invites (already logged in contradictions.md, "Perception–reality gap: direction is not consistent"): here self-report and measurement agree. Worth flagging in synthesis that the perception-reality gap is outcome-dependent — self-reported *speed* is unreliable (METR, Peng, Stanford), but this source's self-reported *subjective learning* tracked the measured comprehension result. Do not merge into the existing contradiction entry without the human/orchestrator reviewing — flagging only.
- **PROTOCOL §5 tension 5 (seniority asymmetry):** this source sharpens the tension rather than resolving it. Cui et al. and other E1/E2 field experiments found *task-completion* gains concentrated in junior/less-experienced developers — a floor-raising story. If (as this RCT's mechanism suggests, though not proven for real juniors) the developers who benefit most in short-run task speed are also the ones most likely to fully delegate and therefore learn least, the "AI helps juniors most" narrative becomes two-sided: same population, opposite sign, depending on whether the metric is this sprint's task or this year's skill trajectory. Two important caveats before this can be used as tension-5 evidence: (a) this sample was majority-experienced by tenure (55.8% at 7+ years), not majority-junior, so it does not directly test junior developers; (b) the "juniors delegate more / gain more short-run speed" half of the argument is not measured here at all — it is a plausible mechanism import from the seniority-asymmetry literature, not this paper's own finding. Recommend logging as a *reasoned hypothesis* in the RQ4/RQ6 synthesis memo, explicitly labelled as inference bridging two different studies' populations, not as a resolved or even a directly-evidenced tension-5 position.
