## Tier, era, independence

Both (a) and (b) are DX's own newsletter/blog content, written by its CEO (Noda) and a named staff analyst (Reock, whose DX title was not independently confirmed in the fetches but who is consistently bylined on DX's newsletter).
- **COI — direction matches the pattern already established for Faros, and is worth stating precisely again because it recurs.** DX's core product is a developer-experience/productivity **measurement** platform (DX Core 4, the AI Measurement Framework — see `sources/space-dx-core4.md`). A "the hype is wrong, gains are real but modest, and here is the nuanced measurement you actually need to see your own numbers" narrative is a direct pitch for DX's own instrumentation product line — structurally the same reverse-COI pattern already flagged for Faros ("alarming numbers create demand for governance tooling"), here inverted to "deflating numbers create demand for *credible measurement* tooling that can tell a leadership team what their *real* 10% looks like, as opposed to trusting vendor claims or ungrounded internal narratives." Both pieces link directly to DX product collateral: Noda's mechanism piece links to DX's own "prompting guide" and "advanced prompting guide" and to DX job postings; Reock's piece links to a DX webinar registration and to the same underlying DX report. This is recorded as a directional COI note, consistent with how Faros's COI was recorded, not as a reason to discount the direction of the finding outright.
- **Era: E2/E3 blend, materially less specified than Faros's own era disclosure.** The stated window is **November 2024 – February 2026** (~16 months). Neither piece names which specific AI tools contributed to the "AI usage +65%" figure, nor splits the metric by tool modality (autocomplete vs chat vs agentic). Given the window extends well into 2026, agentic tools (Cursor, Claude Code, Copilot agent mode) are almost certainly present in the mix by the later months, but DX gives no basis to estimate what fraction of "AI usage" this represents at any point in the window — a materially thinner era disclosure than Faros's report, which at least named its tool list and gave an agentic-authorship-share figure (<1% of PRs). Tag as **E2/E3, un-split, modality composition unknown** — treat any effect-size claim drawn from this source as *not* cleanly attributable to the agentic era specifically, more cautiously than Faros.
- **Independence flags:** vendor-published; own dataset. **The piece does not state whether the "400 companies" are DX's own platform customers or an independently assembled sample.** No other plausible data-collection mechanism exists for a company to obtain PR-throughput-per-company telemetry, cross-referenced against an "AI usage" metric, for 400 companies over 16 months, other than through instrumentation those companies have installed — i.e., DX's own paying customer base, the same self-selection pattern already documented in this repo for Faros, GitClear, and Uplevel. This is an *inference*, not a quoted admission, and is recorded as such — it was not found stated explicitly in either piece or the report landing page despite three targeted fetches asking the question directly. If a customer base, the population is pre-selected toward organisations that already buy developer-analytics tooling (a plausible proxy for "engineering-metrics-conscious," not a random cross-section of the software industry) — the phrase "a random sample out of 400 companies" (Reock's own words) most plausibly means "randomly sampled from DX's book of customers," not "randomly sampled from the industry," though this reading is unconfirmed.

## Method & population/dataset

- **Sample: "a random sample out of 400 companies"** (Reock's own phrase, confirmed verbatim across two fetches), described elsewhere in secondary aggregation as "more than 400 engineering organizations" / "400+ companies." **Selection criteria, industry mix, company-size distribution, and geography are not disclosed anywhere found** (three separate targeted fetches of the newsletter piece, the report landing page, and the methodology-framework page all failed to surface this detail). Contrast with He et al. (2026), which discloses exact developer/repo/PR counts and an anonymity rationale, and with Faros (2026), which discloses an explicit exclusion criterion ("non-standard infrastructure" orgs) even if underspecified — DX's disclosure here is thinner than either.
- **Time window:** November 2024 – February 2026 (confirmed verbatim, "between November 2024 through February 2026," consistent across every fetch pass).
- **Design type — longitudinal, organisation-level, not within-team/within-developer.** One secondary aggregation (WebSearch synthesis of getdx.com content) paraphrases DX's own framing precisely: *"Rather than using cross-sectional analysis comparing individual developers, DX looked at longitudinal data at the organizational level to assess how AI adoption has impacted organizational velocity and throughput as it matured."* This is a materially coarser unit of analysis than either Faros (within-team, per-team quarter-pair contrast) or He et al. (within-developer fixed effects with a genuine control group). No developer- or team-level breakdown of any kind is published in either piece.
- **Primary metric: PR throughput**, explicitly chosen over SPACE/DX Core 4's fuller metric set for its plainness: per Reock's own stated rationale (secondary-aggregated, not independently re-verified verbatim), the study "examined multiple metrics including SPACE and the DX Core 4, but focused on PR throughput as the primary metric because it's what most organizations are talking about and focusing on" — i.e., a deliberate choice to use the metric practitioners already track, at some cost to construct validity (PR throughput is explicitly named in PROTOCOL §5 tension 7 as a "gameable, meaning-free" candidate anti-metric; DX's own anti-gaming exclusion, below, is itself an implicit admission of this risk).
- **"AI usage" — measurement basis undefined in the primary text.** Neither piece states the precise operationalisation of the +65% "AI usage" figure (seat licences? weekly-active-users of a specific tool? percentage of PRs carrying an AI-authorship signal? self-reported survey?). DX's own separate methodology page (`getdx.com/blog/measure-ai-impact/`, fetched for corroboration) lists several *possible* AI-usage measurement approaches DX recommends generally — "tool analytics dashboards" for MAU/WAU/DAU, "Git analytics + AI tool telemetry" for percentage of AI-assisted PRs, "developer surveys + telemetry" for percentage of AI-generated code — but does not confirm which of these, if any, underlies this specific study's +65% figure. **This is a genuine, unresolved measurement-basis gap** — the study's independent variable is not precisely defined in any fetched primary text.
- **Anti-gaming exclusion — the one methodological safeguard explicitly disclosed.** Verbatim (confirmed across two independent fetches): *"filtered out potential gamification effects by excluding teams that set PR throughput targets for individual engineers, which could drive metric inflation rather than genuine output."* No count of how many teams/companies this excluded, no sensitivity check showing what the headline number would have been without the filter, and no discussion of *other* plausible gaming vectors (e.g., PR-splitting behaviour, which is a well-known confound for any PR-count metric and is not mentioned).
- **Statistical methodology: none disclosed.** No confidence intervals, no significance test, no correlation coefficient, no stated aggregation method (mean vs median is itself ambiguous — Reock's own piece says "increased by an average 7.76%," while one independent secondary aggregation paraphrases it as "median PR throughput increased by an average 7.76%," conflating the two terms; this may be the aggregator's own imprecision rather than DX's, but no primary-text sentence was found that unambiguously resolves whether the 7.76% is a mean, a median, or a pooled aggregate change). This is a materially thinner statistical disclosure than Faros's report, which specifies Spearman's ρ, a p<0.05 filter, and a ≥6-company reporting threshold (see Cross-ref).
- **No breakdowns of any kind** by team, seniority, task type, company size, or industry are published in either piece — an explicit gap relative to He et al.'s seniority/repo-age breakdowns and Faros's per-metric significance filtering.
- **Qualitative supplement:** the telemetry piece includes one anecdotal quote from an unnamed senior developer (not independently useful as evidence; recorded only because the task instructions asked for full extraction) and references "qualitative interviews with developers conducted to understand modest gains" (per one secondary aggregation) — no interview data, count, or findings are given in the fetched text.

## Key findings

**(a) Reock, "AI productivity gains are ~10%, not 10x" — all measured/telemetry basis except where noted:**

1. **AI tool usage increased by an average of 65%** over the study window (metric undefined — see Method).
2. **PR throughput increased by 7.76%** over the same window (aggregation method — mean/median — ambiguous per Method section).
3. **Headline framing: "for most organizations, a 5–15% throughput gain is what the current generation of AI coding tools is delivering"** — explicitly contrasted against "vendor claims of 3x or even 10x improvements." This 5–15% range appears to be Reock's own interpretive banding around the single 7.76% point estimate (no distribution, percentile breakdown, or company-level histogram was found to substantiate the range directly), not a separately computed statistic.
4. No task-type, seniority, or team-size breakdown of any kind.
5. Anti-gaming exclusion (above) is the only disclosed data-quality control.
6. **Reock's own interpretive framing (asserted, not measured):** *"Who wouldn't invest in the 10 or 15 percent uplift in overall productivity?"* and *"I think we just need to start allowing ourselves the grace that 10 or 15 percent is actually successful"* — both quotes from secondary reception coverage of Reock's public remarks, consistent in substance with the piece's own framing; recorded as the author's normative/interpretive gloss, clearly separated from the measured 7.76% figure itself.
7. **Cost/quality data for RQ4: none.** No licence/token spend, no review-hours costing, no defect or rework data in either piece.

**(b) Noda, "Why aren't AI productivity gains higher?" — a mechanism argument, explicitly interpretive/asserted rather than independently measured within this piece:**

1. **Core thesis:** the ~10% aggregate gain is structurally capped because coding is a small share of a developer's total work, and AI has not meaningfully touched the rest of the SDLC.
2. **The 16% claim — verbatim:** *"Microsoft's research puts coding at around 16% of a developer's time."* Hyperlinked directly to https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.15287 (the Time Warp paper, citation (c) above). **See "Sourcing-accuracy problem" below — this figure does not appear in the cited source as read in full for this record.**
3. **Five stated bottlenecks (Noda's own framing, asserted/interpretive, not each independently measured within this piece):**
   - (i) Coding isn't the main bottleneck — other SDLC work dominates.
   - (ii) Partial SDLC automation creates *new* bottlenecks — code review scrutiny absorbs the time saved in coding (directly consistent with §5 tension 6 and with both Faros's and He et al.'s review-bottleneck findings, though Noda's piece cites no numbers of its own for this claim — it is asserted as a mechanism, not measured here).
   - (iii) Social friction — polarisation between pro-/anti-AI engineers slows organisational uptake (asserted, no data).
   - (iv) Tooling and skill gaps — immature tools, steep learning curves (asserted, no data).
   - (v) Missing context — *"the knowledge of how things work lives in people's heads"* and is inaccessible to AI (asserted, no data; consistent in direction with §5 tension 2's brownfield/context-dependency finding, but offered here as prose, not evidence).
4. **Other observations (asserted, no independent data given in this piece):** documentation-quality improvement claimed but unquantified; AI "especially powerful in greenfield projects" (directionally consistent with tension 2, but this piece adds no new measurement — see He et al. and Stanford/Denisov-Blanch for the quantified version already in this evidence base); a stated concern that developers are "offloading critical thinking" (directly relevant to the skill-formation/cognitive-load cross-cutting tag per CLAUDE.md v0.4 — asserted, not measured, in this piece).
5. **Explicit caveat (Noda's own words):** *"The 10% figure is not the ceiling"* — the piece frames the ~10% as a current-state observation, not a permanent bound, and argues future gains require automating non-coding SDLC work.

**Sourcing-accuracy problem — the single most important finding of this extraction, escalated for human attention below.** The Time Warp paper (arXiv:2502.15287), read in full directly from the PDF, reports developers spending **≈11% of their *actual* workweek on "Coding new features"** (Fig. 2/Section III-A) and **≈20% of their *ideal* (self-described preferred) workweek** on the same activity (Section III-B) — it separately reports 16 as the *count* of key activities identified in the survey design (Table I), not as any time-share percentage. **No sentence, figure, or table anywhere in the paper's 11 pages states or implies a "16%" figure for coding time.** The number DX's piece attributes to "Microsoft's research" therefore does not match the cited source as read. Two candidate (unconfirmed) explanations: (i) 16% may be an average or blend of the paper's own 11% (actual) and 20% (ideal) figures — 11 and 20 average to 15.5%, close to 16% — but no sentence in the paper performs or licenses this averaging, and blending a self-reported "actual time spent" figure with a self-reported "ideal/preferred allocation" figure would itself be a substantive methodological error if that is what occurred, since the two are conceptually different constructs (observed vs aspirational allocation), not two measurements of the same thing; (ii) DX may have confused the paper's "16 key activities" (a category count) with a percentage. Neither explanation can be confirmed from the materials fetched. **This should be treated as a citation-accuracy problem in DX's own piece, not as a confirmed fact about Microsoft/Time Warp's findings**, and any downstream synthesis use of "coding is ~16% of dev time, per Microsoft" should cite the corrected figures (≈11% actual / ≈20% ideal, single-company Microsoft IC-only self-report survey, 484 respondents, 8.06% response rate) with this discrepancy flagged, not DX's secondary paraphrase.

## What this can and cannot support

**Can support:** a directional, telemetry-based signal from a large (400-company) but methodologically thinly-documented cross-company sample that (a) aggregate PR throughput rose only modestly (mid-single-digit percent) over a 16-month window in which AI-usage metrics rose substantially, and (b) this modest aggregate gain is broadly consistent, in direction and rough order of magnitude, with the "gains are real but far below hype" framing found independently in Faros (2026) and in practitioner reception (HN discussion of this exact piece independently invoked Amdahl's-law-style bottleneck reasoning). Can support, as a Tier D single-vendor source, a directional contribution to the triangulation case for §5 tension 6 (bottleneck displacement) via Noda's mechanism piece, **when read as an assertion requiring independent corroboration**, not as its own measured finding (Noda's piece measures nothing new; it interprets and contextualises Reock's telemetry finding and cites one outside source for its central mechanism claim).

**Cannot support:** any precise magnitude claim, given the essentially undisclosed statistical methodology (no significance testing, no defined AI-usage metric, ambiguous mean/median aggregation, unstated sample-selection process); any claim about how the +7.76% breaks down by seniority, task type, or codebase context (no breakdown published); any claim that "coding is 16% of developer time, per Microsoft research" — this specific figure could not be verified against the cited source and should not be repeated without the correction noted above; any claim of independence from vendor interest (Tier D, no claim admissible alone per PROTOCOL §3/§8); any claim about the agentic era specifically (tool modality mix within "AI usage" is undisclosed).

## Cross-ref assessment (for the human's review — synthesis/contradictions.md was NOT edited)

**1. Faros vs DX — adjudicating the provisional contradiction entry.**

The existing `contradictions.md` entry frames the gap as DX's PR throughput +7.76% against Faros's headline throughput figures (task throughput/dev +33.7%, epics/dev +66.2%), a "4–8×" discrepancy. Having now extracted DX's own text in detail, I think this framing, while not wrong, **overstates the comparability of the two headline figures being contrasted** and the adjudication should be sharpened as follows:

- **Metric alignment matters more than the raw "4–8×" framing suggests.** Faros's own metric set includes a genuinely PR-specific throughput figure — **PR merge rate/dev +16.2%** — which is the closer conceptual match to DX's "PR throughput +7.76%" than Faros's task-throughput (+33.7%) or epics-completed (+66.2%) figures, which measure different units of work (tasks/epics, not PRs) and which Faros itself flags as its "single finding most directly tied to business outcomes" precisely because it is *not* the same as raw code/PR output. On a PR-to-PR comparison, the gap is **roughly 2×** (16.2% vs 7.76%), not 4–8×. The wider 4–8× framing is real but is partly an artefact of comparing DX's narrower PR metric against Faros's broader task/epic metrics — a genuine estimand mismatch, exactly as tension in the existing entry's "plausible reconciliation" already anticipates, now confirmed in more precise terms.
- **Design and disclosure are not symmetric, which matters for how much weight either "gap" number deserves.** Faros discloses a specific, checkable statistical procedure (per-team lowest-vs-highest-adoption-quarter contrast, Spearman's ρ, p<0.05, ≥6-company reporting minimum, explicit exclusion criteria). DX discloses almost none of this: no significance testing, no defined "AI usage" metric, ambiguous mean/median use, undisclosed sample-selection process, no breakdown of any kind. **Both are Tier D and neither carries a claim alone, but if forced to weight the two designs against each other, Faros's is the more transparently specified of the two — this asymmetry, not just "different estimands," is itself worth naming in synthesis.**
- **Independent variable mismatch:** Faros gates "high adoption" on a defined ≥50% WAU threshold; DX's "AI usage +65%" has no stated definition at all. Even where the two studies' *outcome* metrics could be aligned (PR throughput), their *treatment* variables may not be measuring the same underlying construct.
- **Verdict:** this is **both** a genuine estimand mismatch (task/epic throughput vs PR throughput; within-team quarter-pair contrast vs pooled cross-company trend-correlation) **and**, once the closest comparable metrics are lined up, **a real residual disagreement in magnitude** (≈2× on PR-specific throughput), which the primary texts cannot resolve given DX's thin methodological disclosure. I would revise the existing entry's framing from "4–8× smaller" to something like: **"4–8× smaller on the broadest headline figures; ≈2× smaller on the closest matched metric (PR-specific throughput); unresolved beyond that because DX discloses materially less methodology than Faros."** Both still agree on direction (positive, modest, well below vendor 3×/10× hype) and both independently gesture at the same downstream mechanism (review/absorption bottleneck) — Noda's mechanism piece and Faros's own review-latency findings are directionally convergent, even though neither DX piece publishes review-latency numbers of its own to compare directly against Faros's Finding #6 or He et al.'s review-cycle-time figures.

**2. Coexistence with he-2026-2x-mandate's 1.46–1.72× within-developer estimate.**

Yes — a +7.76% cross-company aggregate PR-throughput trend and a 1.46–1.72× (i.e. +46–72%) within-developer causal estimate are not in tension; they are close to what one would mechanically expect to see together, for several independent, non-exclusive reasons, all traceable to details already extracted in `he-2026-2x-mandate.md`:

- **Dose-response / accumulated-use dependency.** He et al.'s own decomposition attributes the larger share of their effect to an *accumulating-use* slope (β≈0.041, contributing +0.45 of the total +0.54 log-point effect), not the one-off adoption jump (β1=0.143, contributing only +0.14) — and their month-by-month event study shows the effect climbing from an early, much smaller figure up to 1.99× only by **nine months on tool**. A cross-company sample spanning Nov 2024–Feb 2026, in which many organisations are at earlier, shallower points on their own adoption curve, will show a pooled average far below the multi-month compounded effect a single deeply-adopted cohort can reach.
- **Explicit "upper envelope" caveat, already recorded in this repo.** He et al.'s own authors state their single studied firm is "close to a near-ideal scenario for AI mandates" and repeatedly frame their headline result as "an upper envelope... not a representative industry average." A 400-company cross-section (even if drawn from DX's own devex-conscious customer base, which is itself not a neutral population) will necessarily include many organisations far less favourably positioned than a young, AI-forward, uncapped-budget, CTO-mandated firm.
- **Aggregation level mismatch.** DX's metric is a company-level (or pooled) trend against a company-level "AI usage" trend — it is not conditioned on individual developer adoption status the way He et al.'s within-developer fixed-effects design is. If, as He et al. also found, gains concentrate specifically in newer/greenfield code (+44% vs +12% n.s. in legacy) and specifically among actual adopters (not the whole developer population), then a company-wide aggregate mechanically dilutes a large within-adopter, favourable-context effect toward a much smaller organisation-wide number, simply by averaging over non-adopters, light adopters, and legacy-code-bound work.
- **Metric non-identity.** DX's "PR throughput" (company-level) is not the same construct as He et al.'s "PRs per active developer, developer fixed effects, own-adoption-month-aligned" — different denominators, different conditioning, different identification strategy.
- **Net read:** the ~6–9× gap between DX's aggregate +7.76% and He et al.'s within-adopter +46–72% is itself a plausible, even expected, empirical signature of "local/within-adopter gains do not scale linearly to organisation-level throughput trends" — which is a genuinely useful RQ5 synthesis point in its own right (the translation question), independent of whichever exact number from either study one prefers. It is *not* evidence that either study is wrong; it is evidence that they are measuring different points on the same underlying dilution curve (best-case single-adopter causal effect vs population-average cross-company trend).

**3. Consistency with Noda's mechanism piece and the wider evidence base.** Noda's "coding ≈16% of dev time" argument (independent of the sourcing-accuracy problem above) is directionally the same argument DX's own AI Measurement Framework and Reock's telemetry piece jointly make, and is broadly consistent with — though not independently corroborating, since it cites no new data of its own — the review-bottleneck mechanism already well-evidenced in this repo by He et al. (Tier A) and Faros (Tier D). Once corrected to the Time Warp paper's actual figures (≈11% actual / ≈20% ideal), the substantive point ("coding is a minority of a developer's time, so accelerating it alone caps the achievable aggregate productivity gain") still holds directionally — the *specific number* is simply wrong as cited, not the underlying mechanism argument.
