## Tier & era & independence

- **Tier: A** (large-N randomised field experiments — 4,867 developers across three independently-run company RCTs, with instrumental-variables treatment-on-treated estimation, now peer-reviewed in a top journal). Carries claims on its own per §3.
- **COI flag (independence):** Two of six authors (Jaffe, Peng) are Microsoft employees, and the paper thanks named Microsoft/GitHub/Accenture employees for "data help." The tool studied (GitHub Copilot) is a Microsoft/GitHub product. This is a genuine conflict-of-interest signal — the study is not fully external to the vendor. Mitigating factors: (a) randomisation was run by the companies for their own business decisions, not designed by the researchers to produce a favourable result; (b) four of six authors are unaffiliated academics (Princeton, MIT ×2, Wharton); (c) results are directionally modest (26% vs. the ~55% lab RCT the same group discusses) and include a *negative* secondary result (build success rate falls, i.e. a mild quality-debt signal) that a pure marketing exercise would be unlikely to publish; (d) peer-reviewed at Management Science with a standard review/revision cycle (Feb 2025 submission → Aug 2025 acceptance). **Flag this COI in every downstream use of this source**, but treat the Tier A grade as retained rather than downgraded to D — this is an independently-refereed academic study with vendor-affiliated co-authors and vendor-supplied data, not a vendor white paper.
- **Independence of the three experiments:** genuinely three separate organisations (Microsoft, Accenture, and an anonymous Fortune 100 electronics manufacturer) running their own ad-hoc business rollouts; the paper pools them post hoc. This is a real triangulation strength within a single paper.

## Era

**E1 — autocomplete era, confirmed.** The tool studied is explicitly described as "an AI-based coding assistant suggesting intelligent code completions" (i.e. classic inline Copilot completions, not Copilot Chat, not agentic/multi-file). Timing corroborates this: the Microsoft experiment started the first week of September 2022 — before ChatGPT's release and before Copilot Chat existed. Accenture ran from late July 2023 and Anonymous Company from October 2023; both are still within the autocomplete-only Copilot product era for the features described (code completions, not chat/agent). **Correction to the protocol seed:** §6 tagged this source "[E1/E2]" — the E2 tag is not supported by the paper's own description of the tool or the experiment timing. This should be recorded as **E1 only**.

## Method & population

- **Design:** Three separate randomised (or randomised-timing) field experiments, run by the companies themselves for business decisions, not designed as academic RCTs from the outset:
  - **Microsoft:** started 1st week of September 2022; n = 1,746 developers, primarily US-based; 50.4% randomised to treatment (616 individually randomised, 1,130 team-randomised, avg team size 6.2); ran to 3 May 2023 (ended early because the control group started self-adopting as Copilot awareness grew).
  - **Accenture:** started last week of July 2023; n = 320 developers in Southeast Asian offices; 61.3% randomised to treatment at the individual level; treatment group also got a training session and manager encouragement (a confound the paper does not fully net out); control group given access December 2023.
  - **Anonymous Company (Fortune 100 electronics manufacturer):** started October 2023; n = 3,054 developers; not a treatment/control split but a **staggered rollout** with randomised invitation timing (weekly invites Sept–Oct 2023, team-level).
  - Pooled n = 4,867 developers across all three.
- **Identification:** preferred estimates use an instrumental-variables approach (treatment assignment instruments for actual Copilot usage/adoption) to get treatment-on-the-treated effects, given imperfect compliance (adoption rates well below 100% even in the treated group). A "weighted-IV" (W-IV) estimator up-weights periods with larger treatment/control adoption gaps; unweighted and DiD estimates are also reported and sometimes diverge in sign/magnitude from the experimental (W-IV) numbers.
- **Population / task context:** ordinary working developers doing their regular jobs (engineering, design, testing) in production/brownfield environments at large organisations — **not** a lab task or greenfield benchmark. Task-type tag: **T4 (feature dev in mature codebases)**, since this is professional software development inside existing production codebases as part of normal business, directly relevant to RQ5.

## Key findings

All outcome variables below are **measured** (GitHub telemetry: pull requests, commits, builds, build success, Copilot suggestion/acceptance logs) — **not self-reported**. The paper contains no survey/perception data; there is no measured-vs-self-reported delta to report for this source (methodologically notable in itself for RQ2: a rare pure-telemetry RCT).

**Pooled effects (precision-weighted average across the three experiments, W-IV, expressed as % of control-group mean):**
- Pull requests completed (primary outcome, "completed tasks"): **+26.08% (SE 10.3%)** — statistically significant. [T4, measured]
- Commits: **+13.55% (SE 10.0%)** — not statistically significant at conventional levels. [T4, measured]
- Builds (compile events): **+38.38% (SE 12.55%)** — statistically significant. [T4, measured]
- Build success rate (quality proxy): **−5.53% (SE 3.64%)** pooled — small, directionally negative, not significant pooled, but **negative and significant at Accenture specifically** (W-IV ≈ −17.4%, SE 7.12, marked significant in-text). The paper flags this as weak evidence for a "trial-and-error" / quality-debt interpretation (more building, not more successful builds) but does not treat it as conclusive.

**Per-company breakdown (Table 2; reconstructed from a garbled PDF table layout and cross-checked against in-text prose, which independently confirms the pooled figures — treat per-company cell values as best-effort reconstruction, not a verbatim table transcription):**
- **Microsoft:** DiD pull requests +7.63% (SE 2.49, significant); W-IV pull requests +27.38% (SE 12.88, significant) — the only individually significant experimental (W-IV) effect at Microsoft. Commits and builds directionally positive but not significant in the W-IV specification. Build success rate ≈ flat, not significant.
- **Accenture:** pull requests W-IV +17.94% (SE 18.72, not significant); builds W-IV +92.40% (SE 26.78, significant); build success rate W-IV −17.40% (SE 7.12, significant and negative — the one clearly negative secondary signal in the paper).
- **Anonymous Company:** pull requests W-IV +54.03% (SE 42.63 — extremely noisy, not significant); no commits/builds/build-success data available for this company (the paper notes data limitations from the short staggered-rollout design).
- The authors are explicit that "standard errors are consistently large and the effect sizes differ across the three different companies" — the pooled 26.08% is doing real work to buy statistical power that no single company experiment has on its own.
- A second, abandoned Accenture experiment (disrupted by a 42%-headcount layoff) is relegated to an appendix; with conservatively-imputed adoption it shows a **negative, non-significant** pull-request point estimate (−39.18%, SE 36.78%) — recorded here per the "record nulls with the same diligence" rule. Including it barely moves the pooled estimate (21.34% vs. 26.08%).

**Seniority/tenure heterogeneity (Microsoft only — the only company with tenure/level data; this is the anchor's core value for §5 tension 5):**
- *Adoption:* short-tenure developers are 9.4pp (SE 2.2pp) more likely to adopt by end of sample (81.6% vs. 72.1% long-tenure); junior-level developers are 4.7pp (SE 2.2pp) more likely to adopt (79.2% vs. 74.4% senior). [measured]
- *Retention:* short-tenure developers are more likely to keep using Copilot beyond one month post-adoption than long-tenure developers; no significant junior-vs-senior difference in this dimension. [measured]
- *Suggestion acceptance rate:* long-tenure developers accept ~4.3% fewer suggestions (≈1.0pp) than short-tenure; senior developers accept ~1.8% fewer (≈0.5pp) than junior — same direction, smaller magnitude for the level split. Low-pre-productivity developers accept substantially more suggestions than high-pre-productivity developers. [measured]
- *Output effects (the headline heterogeneity result):* "short-tenure developers increase their output by 27% to 39% while long-tenure developers have smaller gains of 8% to 13%" across the three main outcomes (pull requests, commits, builds); "junior developers increase their output by 21% to 40% while senior developers have more marginal gains of 7% to 16%." **The paper is explicit that these heterogeneity estimates are noisy and not statistically significant at conventional levels** (p-values shown on the figure range ~0.13–0.77) — the pattern is described as directionally consistent across outcomes, not as a statistically clean result. This nuance is easy to lose in secondary summaries and should be carried forward: **the junior/senior split is a real and consistently-signed pattern across three outcome measures, but it is not itself statistically significant** — the paper's confidence rests on directional consistency, not on any single significant coefficient.
- The paper frames this as siding with the "AI helps lower-skill/lower-experience workers more" literature (Brynjolfsson/Li/Raymond; Noy/Zhang) against the opposing "AI helps the most productive workers more" literature (Otis et al.; Toner-Rodgers) — for *this* task domain and *this* tool generation.

**Context vs. the lab RCT:** the paper explicitly benchmarks itself against Peng et al. (2023) — the ~55.8% task-time reduction found in a controlled lab task — and notes its own field estimate (26.08%) is roughly half that, attributing the gap to real-world task heterogeneity and coding being only part of a developer's job. This is itself a data point for §5 tension 2 (greenfield/lab vs. real-world brownfield gap).

## What this can and cannot support

**Can support:** (a) a baseline E1 effect-size anchor for autocomplete-era Copilot on measured telemetry outcomes in real production settings (T4); (b) methodology points for RQ2 — a rare pure-telemetry (no self-report) large-N RCT design, useful for contrasting against perception-based studies; (c) the seniority-asymmetry pattern as a **plausibly era-independent effect** per the §2 era-transfer rule — but only as a *directionally consistent, non-statistically-significant* signal from one company (Microsoft), not a settled result. **Cannot support:** any E3/agentic-era effect-size claim, any claim about chat or multi-file agent workflows, or a *statistically confirmed* junior-vs-senior effect (the underlying heterogeneity coefficients are not significant) — and per the live tension flagged below, cannot settle whether the junior-gains pattern even holds directionally once tools become agentic.

## Corrections vs. the seed description in PROTOCOL §6

1. **Era:** seed tagged "[E1/E2]"; corrected to **E1 only** — the tool and timing described in the paper are pure autocomplete-era Copilot, no chat/agent component.
2. **Year:** seed cited "Cui et al. (2024)"; the earliest full draft found is dated **February 2025**, with the paper now **published in Management Science, online 27 Feb 2026**. No 2024-dated version was located (the AEA pre-registration is dated 2024, which may be the source of the seed's year).
3. **Third company:** seed said "and possibly a third anonymous company" — confirmed as definite, not speculative: an anonymous Fortune 100 *electronics manufacturer*, n = 3,054, staggered-rollout design.
4. **Precision of headline figure:** seed said "~26%"; confirmed exact figure is **26.08% (SE 10.3%)**, stable and unchanged across the Feb 2025 draft, June 2025 draft, and the Feb 2026 published abstract.
5. **Nuance not in the seed:** the seniority-heterogeneity result, while directionally strong and consistent across three outcome measures, is **not statistically significant** in the paper's own reporting — this should travel with the claim everywhere it is used, since "gains concentrate in junior developers" as stated in the seed reads more conclusively than the paper's own hedged framing.
6. **Secondary quality signal:** the seed's description omits the build-success-rate finding — a mild, Accenture-specific *negative* quality signal (more building, not more successful building) that is directly relevant to §5 tension 4 (quality debt) and should be cross-referenced there.

## Follow-up leads (not extracted — leads only)

- **Peng, Kalliamvakou, Cihon & Demirer (2023), "The Impact of AI on Developer Productivity: Evidence from GitHub Copilot"** (arXiv 2302.06590) — the lab RCT (~55.8% faster on a standardised HTTP-server task) this paper benchmarks itself against; already separately queued in the ledger as its own anchor. Worth noting it also reports Copilot "benefited less experienced, high-workload, and older developers" — an E1 lab-RCT companion data point to the seniority-asymmetry theme.
- **Shifting Work Patterns with Generative AI** (arXiv 2504.11436, Microsoft-affiliated authors incl. Cambon, Peng) — a related Microsoft telemetry study on how GenAI use shifts the *composition* of developer work; potentially relevant to RQ1/RQ6 bottleneck-displacement questions. Not yet checked for era or rigour.
- **"Firms' GitHub Copilot adoption and labor market outcomes for software engineers"** (Contemporary Economic Policy / Wiley, DOI via onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/coep.70035) — labour-market-outcomes angle (hiring/wages) on Copilot adoption; adjacent to but outside core SDLC scope, flagged for RQ1 context only.
- **"Developer Productivity With and Without GitHub Copilot: A Longitudinal Mixed-Methods Case Study"** (arXiv 2509.20353, Sept 2025) — a longitudinal case study that may contain more recent (E1/E2-boundary) telemetry with seniority breakdowns; not yet checked.
- **No direct academic critique or replication of Cui et al. was found** in searches (including a targeted search for commentary from prominent AI-research critics). This appears to be an under-discussed paper relative to METR or DORA — possibly because it is a modest, hedged result rather than a striking one.
- **Live §5 tension 5 question — does the junior-gains pattern reverse in the agentic era?** Informal 2026 practitioner commentary (e.g. Pragmatic Engineer's "AI Tooling for Software Engineers in 2026" newsletter, and lower-tier blog posts such as aidevstack's "Can AI Replace Junior Developers in 2026?") suggests a **directionally opposite** pattern for agentic tools: senior developers are described as extracting more leverage from agentic tools (better task specification, better ability to catch subtle errors) while junior developers are said to need more guardrails. This is Tier D/anecdotal at best and was not evaluated for rigour here — it is flagged as a lead for Phase 1 E3 sweep, and as the single most important open question for whether this anchor's seniority finding transfers across the era boundary (per §2's era-transfer rule, seniority asymmetry is only "plausibly" era-independent, not assumed so). **This should be actively hunted for in Phase 1** as it bears directly on the strategic implication in §5 tension 5 (does AI lift the floor or the ceiling).
