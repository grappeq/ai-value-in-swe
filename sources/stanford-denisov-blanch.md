## Tier, era, independence

- **Tier: B.** Large-scale multi-company telemetry (120k+ developers, 600+ organisations, tens of millions of commits) without randomisation — squarely the PROTOCOL §3 Tier B definition. Not A: no counterfactual design, and the substantive findings are unreviewed talk material; the only written methodology is a non-peer-reviewed preprint. Caveat within B: effect sizes are talk-only and should be quoted as ranges with that label attached.
- **Era: mixed E1/E2, with 2025–2026 waves reaching E3.** Telemetry spans 2022–2026. The June 2025 quadrant figures aggregate "AI coding tools" that over that window were predominantly Copilot-class autocomplete and chat assistants (E1/E2); the talks do not split findings by tool modality, so the quadrant numbers **cannot be cleanly tagged E3**. The November 2025 wave, the claimed December 2025 "inflection point", and the July 2026 spin-off paper (arXiv:2607.01904) do cover agentic tooling (E3). Tag the quadrant findings E1/E2-dominant-mixed; tag the 2026 material E3.
- **Independence:** Academic group (Stanford; Kosinski is GSB faculty), no identified tool-vendor funding — **not Tier D**. Flags: (1) participating companies self-select by trading Git repository access for free productivity insights/dashboards ("real-time performance transparency" per the study site) — population skews to organisations of 50+ engineers that want measurement; (2) co-author Obstbaum is a former industry CTO; the offering resembles a commercial DevProd product, though no startup or licensing arrangement was found in this verification pass; (3) the group's earlier "ghost engineers" claim was publicised via X/press before any paper, drawing junk-science criticism — a pattern of findings-by-talk that applies to this source too.

## Method & population/dataset

- **Population:** ~120,000 developers (was >50k in Nov 2024, ~100k in June 2025), 600+ companies, tens of millions of commits, private repositories, since 2022. Cross-industry; participation requires 50+ engineers.
- **Method:** A panel of 10–15 expert engineers rated commits on quality/maintainability/complexity/time; a model was calibrated to reproduce those ratings (reported r = 0.82 for coding time, r = 0.86 for implementation time; ICC2,k 0.78–0.82 across raters — arXiv:2409.15152). The model then scores every commit in participating repos, measuring "functionality delivered over time" rather than commit counts or LOC. AI impact is inferred by comparing output across adoption status and time — **observational, no randomisation**.
- **Measurement basis:** measured (telemetry + model-scored commits) throughout. Not self-report. The talks explicitly contrast this with developers' perception ("developers felt like they were delivering more because volume of code and commits was higher" while much of it was rework) — a perception-vs-measured delta relevant to RQ2/§5 tension 1, though not quantified as a paired estimate the way METR's is.
- **Known methodological critiques (collect for RQ2/RQ6):**
  - Model reportedly trained/calibrated on a small ground-truth set — cited as ~70 Java commits rated by expert panels, 18 engineers ([Jellyfish critique](https://jellyfish.co/blog/ghost-engineers/), of the same model behind the "ghost engineers" claim) — generalisation across languages/domains unproven.
  - Commit-output-only lens: invisible work (mentoring, review, design, incident response) is uncounted; an engineer's value is equated to scored commit deltas ([HN threads](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=42284502), [DevOps.com](https://devops.com/code-busters-are-ghost-engineers-haunting-devops-productivity/)).
  - Core analyses unpublished/un-reviewed; effect sizes not accompanied by CIs or model uncertainty in any public artefact.
  - Jellyfish is itself a DevProd analytics vendor — its critique carries its own COI; the HN critiques are independent.

## Key findings (transcript-verified where marked; all **measured** unless stated)

Era tag E1/E2-mixed unless noted; neither talk splits findings numerically by tool modality, confirming the era-tagging caveat above. Transcripts obtained via `yt-dlp` auto-captions, 2026-07-06 (see Verification status).

1. **Greenfield vs brownfield × complexity quadrants** (June 2025 talk, `tbDDYKRFjhk`; the §5 tension 2 crux; T3 vs T4) — **TRANSCRIPT-CONFIRMED, exact match to prior record:**
   - Greenfield, low complexity (T3): **+30–40%** productivity gain
   - Greenfield, high complexity (T3): **+10–15%**
   - Brownfield, low complexity (T4/T5): **+15–20%**
   - Brownfield, high complexity (T4): **+0–10%**
   Verbatim: *"low complexity green field 30 to 40% gains right from AI, high complexity but green field more modest gains 10 to 15, brown field and low complexity pretty good 15 to 20%. And most importantly, high complexity brownfield tasks 0 to 10%."* Sample size given explicitly: *"this slide has a sample size of 136 teams across 27 companies."*
2. **Rework/churn** (T4/T5 adjacent; RQ4-relevant) — **TRANSCRIPT-CONFIRMED:** AI increases gross delivered volume by roughly 30–40%, offset by rework, leaving a net average of 15–20%. Verbatim: *"with AI coding you generate or you increase your productivity by roughly 30 40%... you got to go back and kind of fix some of the bugs that code introduced... which in turn gives you an average productivity gain across all industries, all sectors, everything of roughly about 15 to 20%."* The record's paraphrase "roughly half of gross gains get eaten by rework" is a reasonable characterisation of the 30–40%→15–20% gap, not a figure the speaker states in those words.
3. **Headline-number discrepancy — RESOLVED for two of the three figures; the third could not be located in either transcript:**
   - **15–20% average** is the June 2025 talk's (`tbDDYKRFjhk`) own stated headline: the **net average across the full quadrant sample** (136 teams/27 companies), explicitly called out as an **average**, not a median (quote above, finding 2).
   - **Median ~10%** is the November 2025 talk's (`JvosMkuNxF8`) own stated figure, but for a **different, smaller, matched sub-cohort measured differently**: *"we took 46 teams that were using AI and we matched them with 46 similar teams that were not using AI and we measured their net productivity gains from AI quarterly... the dark blue line is the median which as of July of this year stands at about 10% for this cohort."* So this is a **median**, from a **46-vs-46 matched-pair design**, read at a **July 2025** data point in an ongoing quarterly series — not the same statistic, population, or time-point as finding 1's average. The two numbers are not in tension; they are different statistics from different sub-analyses, as the prior record speculated.
   - **~7–9% "for Copilot-class assistants"** ([getcoai summary](https://getcoai.com/video/does-ai-actually-boost-developer-productivity-100k-devs-study-yegor-denisov-blanch-stanford/)) — **searched for in both full transcripts and not found**; neither talk states this figure or splits results by "Copilot-class" vs other tooling anywhere in the spoken content. This figure should be treated as **unverified and possibly a misreading** by the secondary source (it does not correspond to anything the speaker says, including the quadrant, rework, or team-cohort figures) until a primary artefact confirms it. Do not carry it into synthesis without independent confirmation.
4. **Language ecosystem split** (June 2025 talk) — **TRANSCRIPT-CONFIRMED, exact match:** high-popularity languages (Python, Java, JavaScript, TypeScript named explicitly) get *"gains between 20% for the low complexity and 10 to 15% for the high complexity"*; low-popularity languages (Cobol, Haskell, Elixir named explicitly) show minimal-to-negative gains: *"AI doesn't really help even with low complexity uh tasks for low popularity languages... for low language popularity and complex tasks, AI can actually decrease productivity because it's so bad at coding in Cobalt or Haskell or Elixir that it just makes you slower"* — caveated as *"maybe five or 10% of the kind of global development work"*.
5. **Codebase size** (June 2025 talk) — **TRANSCRIPT-CONFIRMED, with one correction:** the x-axis range stated is **1,000 to 10 million LOC** (log scale), not "~10k to ~10M" as the prior record had it — minor correction. Verbatim: *"a logarithmic scale of the codebase size right from 1,000 lines of code to 10 million on the x-axis... as the codebase size increases the gains you get from AI decrease sharply."* Three causes given: context-window limits, signal-to-noise degradation, and denser dependencies/domain logic in larger codebases. **Citation-hygiene note:** the supporting curve the speaker shows (Gemini 1.5 Pro coding-task performance dropping from ~90 to ~50 (0–100 scale) by 32,000 context tokens) is **borrowed from an external paper he calls "NoLiMa"** (ASR: "no lima" — likely *NoLiMa: Long-Context Evaluation Beyond Literal Matching*), **not SWEPR's own telemetry** — this specific data point should not be attributed to the Stanford study itself.
6. **Team variance** (Nov 2025 talk) — **PARTIALLY audio-verified, partially slide-only — could not fully confirm:** the transcript confirms the *existence* of a widening top/bottom-performer gap, described only qualitatively and explicitly caveated by the speaker as speculative: *"the discrepancy between the top performers and the bottom ones is increasing. There's a widening gap. And so if we very unscientifically and very illustratively project this forward... this is very directional."* **The specific top-quartile +20–30% / bottom-quartile 0%-or-negative figures, and the 4.8%→19% (Apr 2023–Jul 2025) widening-gap number from [Eichhorst 2026](https://ingoeichhorst.medium.com/state-of-ai-coding-efficiency-2026-1abfa0ab7434), are NOT spoken anywhere in the transcript** — they must come from on-screen chart labels Eichhorst read off slides, which this transcript cannot corroborate. Flag as **slide-only / unverified from audio**; treat with lower confidence than items 1–5 until the slide itself is inspected.
7. **Codebase cleanliness moderator** (Nov 2025 talk; RQ3) — **TRANSCRIPT-CONFIRMED, exact match:** *"there's a point 40 R squared meaning a pretty decent correlation around environment cleanliness and gains from AI or productivity gains from using AI."* Confirms R² ≈ 0.40. The talk adds an explicit mechanism/feedback-loop claim not in the prior record: unchecked AI use accelerates codebase entropy, which degrades the cleanliness index, which in turn *"further collapses your AI gains"* — a self-reinforcing decline the speaker frames as a management responsibility ("you as a human need to push on the other side to... maintain that cleanliness to keep reaping the benefits from AI").
8. **Adoption learning curve (~30–100 hours)** — **NOT FOUND in either transcript.** Neither talk mentions a learning-curve figure in hours, or any adoption-slowdown period, anywhere in the spoken content. This claim's origin could not be traced to either primary source; it should be treated as **unverified** pending location of its actual source (possibly the unread Sept 2025 slides deck, a different interview, or a secondary-source error) — do not cite as talk-confirmed.
9. **December 2025 "inflection point"** — still **not found**; neither transcript mentions an "inflection point" or December 2025 specifically. Remains an unextracted lead per the prior record — confirmed absent from both talks covered here, so it originates elsewhere (a later 2026 artefact per yegordb.com).
10. **Self-report vs measured productivity delta** (June 2025 talk; RQ2/§5 tension 1) — **NEW, transcript-confirmed, directly relevant to the perception–reality-gap tension:** a 43-developer experiment asked each participant to self-rate their productivity percentile (0–100, in 5-point buckets) relative to the group, then compared this to the model's measured score. Verbatim: *"asking someone how productive they think they are is almost as good as flipping a coin. There's very little correlation... people misjudged their productivity by about 30 percentile points. Only one in three people actually estimated their productivity within their quartile."* This is a *general* self-report-validity finding (not AI-specific — it is about productivity self-assessment generally), but it is directly relevant to RQ2/RQ6 as independent corroboration that self-report is a poor productivity proxy, converging with METR's perception-reality gap from a different angle (miscalibration generally, not specifically AI-induced optimism).
11. **Token spend vs productivity gain** (Nov 2025 talk; RQ4) — **NEW, transcript-confirmed:** plotting productivity gain against tokens-per-engineer-per-month (log scale) shows a loose correlation ("quite loose, 0.20 or so linearly") with a **"death valley effect around the 10 million token mark"** — teams at that usage level underperformed teams using somewhat fewer tokens. Speaker's conclusion, verbatim: *"AI usage quality matters more than AI usage value."* Explicitly caveated as directional ("it's very directional but interesting"). Relevant to RQ4/RQ6: token spend alone is a poor productivity proxy and a candidate anti-metric.
12. **AI engineering-practices maturity model** (Nov 2025 talk; era-taxonomy relevant) — **NEW, transcript-confirmed:** a five-level adoption ladder scanned from git-history "AI fingerprints": Level 0 no AI use; Level 1 personal/unshared use; Level 2 team-shared prompts and rules; Level 3 AI autonomously completes specific tasks; **Level 4 "agentic orchestration... where AI just runs the entire process"** (their explicit E3 marker). Presented as "quite directional at this point but evolving" and destined for an open-source tool via the SWEPR research portal (not yet located — follow-up lead). Illustrated with a same-company, two-business-unit case: identical AI tooling/licences/spend, but one BU used AI for "almost 40% of their work" while the other lagged well behind — explicit evidence that **access and even usage do not guarantee comparable adoption depth**, reinforcing RQ6's case for usage-depth instrumentation over binary access/seat metrics.
13. **Case study: PR count vs measured quality/output, 350-person team** (Nov 2025 talk; RQ4/RQ5/RQ6 anti-metrics) — **NEW, transcript-confirmed, concrete numbers:** a large-enterprise team of 350 engineers adopted AI in May 2025; comparing four months before vs four months after: **pull requests +14%**; **code-quality/maintainability score (0–10 scale) fell ~9% and became more erratic**; **their engineering-output metric ("effective output") "didn't increase meaningfully"**; **rework increased 2.5×**. Speaker's explicit framing: *"had this company not measured this more thoroughly and simply measured PR counts, they would have thought, hey, we're doing great... It might be [negative ROI]."* This is the clearest concrete illustration in either talk of PR/commit-count metrics masking a flat-to-negative outcome once rework and quality are measured — strong, named-with-numbers support for the RQ6 anti-metrics list (bad-metric trap: PR/commit counts).

## What this can and cannot support

Can support (with Tier B caveats and an era label): the *pattern* that measured AI gains are moderate on average, concentrate in greenfield/low-complexity work, shrink to ~0–10% in complex brownfield work, and are partially offset by rework — the strongest single quantitative anchor for RQ5/§5-tension-2. Now transcript-verified as what the speaker actually says (findings 1–5, 7, 10–13), not just a secondary-write-up rendering. Can also support, at the same tier/era grading: PR/commit-count as a demonstrated bad metric with named numbers (finding 13), a token-spend/productivity decoupling signal for RQ4 (finding 11), and general self-report-miscalibration evidence adjacent to RQ2 (finding 10). Cannot support: precise effect sizes as point estimates rather than ranges (talk-only, no CIs); the team-quartile and learning-curve figures specifically (findings 6, 8 — not spoken in either transcript, slide-only/unlocated); E3-specific claims from the pre-2025 waves; or any causal reading (self-selected population, no randomisation).

## Corrections vs the seed description (PROTOCOL §6)

- Seed: "gains... shrink toward zero **or negative** in complex legacy systems." As reported, the brownfield/high-complexity quadrant is **0–10% (positive floor at zero)**; *negative* outcomes are reported only for low-popularity languages, bottom-quartile teams, and high-entropy codebases — the seed slightly overstates the headline quadrant. Direction confirmed.
- Seed says "cross-company telemetry" — correct, but note it is **talk-published, not paper-published**; tier grading must reflect that.
- Dataset has grown past the seeded "100k+": now 120k+ developers, 600+ companies (site, 2026).
- The group's newest relevant output is a proper preprint (arXiv:2607.01904, July 2026) — the seed predates it.
- **Codebase-size axis corrected:** prior record said gains "drop sharply... from ~10k to ~10M LOC"; the June 2025 talk transcript states the axis runs from **1,000 (not 10,000) to 10 million LOC** — minor, but the prior record understated the low end by 10×.
- **Headline-number discrepancy (prior finding 3) downgraded from "unresolved, flagged for contradictions" to resolved-by-transcript for two of three figures:** 15–20% (June 2025 talk) and median ~10% (Nov 2025 talk) are both transcript-confirmed as different statistics from different sub-analyses, not competing estimates of the same quantity. The ~7–9% "Copilot-class" figure remains unlocated in either primary source and should not be treated as talk-confirmed.
